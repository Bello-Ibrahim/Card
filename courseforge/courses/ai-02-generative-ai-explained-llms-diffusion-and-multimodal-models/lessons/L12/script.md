# L12 Writing the Comparison Report | Presenter Script

Course: AI-02 · Video: 5 min · Words: 689

## Hook
You have three outputs, three sets of scores and a page of notes. Your manager does not want to read all of that. They want one answer. Which tool should we use for this task, and why? Your report must answer that clearly and honestly.

## Explain
In the last lesson, you ran your tests. Now, in this final lesson, you write the report. This is capstone step three. A good report is short, and it follows six parts.

First, the task. What it is, who needs the output, and why it matters. Second, the method. The three tools, their versions and test dates, the exact prompt, the input, any settings, and your rubric. A reader should be able to repeat your test. Third, a results table, with one row per tool and a weighted total.

Fourth, strengths and failures. For each tool, say what it did well and where it failed. And here is the important part. Do not only describe a failure. Explain its likely cause, using what you learned about how the model works.

An invented fact means the model predicts likely text, not checked facts. An out-of-date statement points to the knowledge cut-off. Wrong letters in an image come from visual patterns. A misread number from a photo is a multimodal reading error. And very different answers each time can come from temperature or random starting noise.

Fifth, your recommendation. Name the tool for this task, and justify it with quality, cost, privacy and risk. Say what checks a person must still do. You can even recommend none of these. Sixth, the limits of your test. One task, one prompt, one day. Being honest here makes your report more trustworthy.

Think of a doctor's summary after tests. It says what was tested, shows the key results, explains what they mean, and gives a clear recommendation. The patient does not need every measurement, but they need to trust the method.

Keep the whole report to two or three pages, including the table. Use short paragraphs and clear headings. Then attach your prompt, your input and your outputs as an appendix, so a reader can check your work.

## Demonstrate
Let's look at an example. Amara is a coordinator at a non-profit organisation in Kigali. She tested three free chatbots on turning volunteer meeting notes into a short action list.

Her results table shows tool A with twenty-three points out of twenty-five, tool B with nineteen, and tool C with seventeen. Accuracy counts double. Tool A is ahead. But the numbers alone are not the report. The words around them matter more.

In strengths and failures, she writes that tool B had the clearest wording, but gave a volunteer a deadline that was not in the notes. She explains why. It is a typical hallucination. The model filled a gap with a likely detail.

Her recommendation is to use tool A for this task, because it was the most accurate and needed the least editing. A coordinator must still check every name and date, and personal phone numbers are removed before pasting. Her limits section says she tested one set of notes on one day.

A common mistake is to write, tool A is the best AI. That is too broad. Your test shows which tool worked best for one task, with one prompt, on one date. So limit your claim. For this task, under these conditions, tool A is the best choice.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a strong report has six parts. Task, method, results table, strengths and failures, recommendation, and limits. Second, explain each failure with its likely cause, using how that type of model works. Third, make a specific recommendation for this task, justify it, and state the limits of your test.
