## L09 Matching the Task to the Model

- **Filename:** `ai-02-generative-ai-explained-llms-diffusion-and-multimodal-models_M3_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (694 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You now know three families of generative AI, and where each one fails. But on a busy Monday morning, with a real task in front of you, which one should you open? A few simple questions can guide the choice.

Welcome to the final week. Choosing a tool is not about finding the best AI. It is about matching the task to the right type of model, and then choosing a tool that fits your limits. Ask five questions, in order.

One. What type of output do I need? Text points to a language model. New images point to a diffusion or other image model. Reading images or audio needs a multimodal model.

Two. How accurate must it be? A list of slogan ideas can include weak ones. A price table or a legal summary cannot include errors. The higher the need for accuracy, the more you give a source, lower the temperature where you can, and check the result.

Three. Is the input private or confidential? Customer data, health records and contracts should not go into a free public tool. Check the tool's privacy terms and your organisation's rules. Sometimes the right choice is to remove personal details, use an approved tool, or not use AI at all.

Four. What will it cost? Think about money, but also the time to write prompts and fix the output. A free tool with daily limits may be enough for you, but not for a whole team. Five. Who checks the result? Every important output needs a reviewer who knows the subject. If nobody can check it, the task may be too risky for AI.

It is like choosing transport. A bicycle, a bus and a lorry are all useful, but for different trips. Nobody asks which vehicle is best without knowing the journey. The answers give you a model type, a tool, and one risk to watch.

Let's meet three people with three different tasks. Nour is a translator in Cairo. She must translate a short tourism brochure from Arabic into English.

The output is text, so she needs a language model. Accuracy must be high, because it will be printed. The brochure is public, so a free tool is fine. She reviews every sentence herself. Her risk to watch: added details, or wrongly translated place names.

Carmela is a secondary school teacher in Manila. She wants to turn a photo of a handwritten class timetable into a clean digital table.

An image goes in and a table comes out, so she needs a multimodal model. She removes student names from the photo first. Accuracy is high, because students depend on it. Her risk to watch: misread times, like one o'clock read as seven o'clock.

Thiago's marketing team in São Paulo needs ten background images for a juice campaign. So they need an image model. Style matters more than facts. They may need a paid plan with commercial rights. Risks to watch: text inside images, and people who all look the same. The designer adds the brand name later and reviews each image.

So, three people, three tasks, and three different model types. Nobody asked which AI is best. Each one started from the task, and named a risk before starting.

A common mistake is to choose the tool first, and force every task into it. That leads to weak results, or to real risks, like pasting confidential data into a free tool. Start from the task. Then choose the model type and the tool.

Let's recap. First, start from the task, not the tool. The output you need points to the model family. Second, accuracy, privacy, cost and who checks the result decide which tool fits, or whether to use AI at all. Third, for every choice, name one risk to watch, based on that family's typical failures.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, complete a decision table for six workplace tasks. For each, choose the model type, a free tool and one risk to watch. It takes about twenty minutes. From the next lesson, you start your capstone, a three-tool comparison report. Next: designing a fair comparison. See you there.
```

## L10 Designing a Fair Comparison

- **Filename:** `ai-02-generative-ai-explained-llms-diffusion-and-multimodal-models_M3_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (683 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A colleague says, tool A is much better than tool B. I tried both last week. But did they give both tools the same task? The same prompt? Did they decide in advance what better means? Without a fair test, a comparison is only an opinion.

In the last lesson, you learned to match the task to the model. Now your capstone begins. You will compare three generative AI tools on one real task from your work or studies. Before you run anything, you need a fair design.

A fair test has four features. First, the same task. Every tool does exactly the same job, such as summarising a meeting transcript for your manager, or creating a poster for a community event. Second, the same input. Every tool gets the same document, photo or data, with personal or confidential information removed.

Third, the same prompt. Write it once and copy it exactly into each tool. If you change the prompt for one tool, you are testing your prompt, not the tool. If a tool needs a different setting, write down the difference.

Fourth, criteria decided in advance. Decide how you will judge the results before you see them. If you decide afterwards, it is easy to choose criteria that favour the tool you already like. Setting the rules first keeps you honest.

For judging, build a simple scoring rubric with four to six criteria. Useful ones are accuracy, usefulness, clarity, speed, and effort to fix. For each criterion, write what a score of one, three and five means. And give more weight to what matters most.

Finally, record the tool version and the date of each test. Generative AI tools change often, and the model behind a free tool may be updated without notice. A result from one month may not be true the next.

Think of a cooking competition. Every cook gets the same ingredients, the same recipe card and the same time. The judges agree on the scoring before they taste anything. If they changed the rules after tasting, the result would mean nothing.

Let's see a fair design. Ingrid is a public health student in Bergen. For her capstone, she chooses a real task. Turn a four-page public research summary into a one-page plain-language fact sheet for community members.

Her input is the public summary, with no personal data, saved as one file. Her prompt asks for a fact sheet for adults with no medical training, using only the text provided, with a title, five key points, and a what you can do section. It also says, do not add anything that is not in the text.

Before testing, she writes her rubric. Four criteria. Accuracy, plain language, following the structure, and effort to fix. Each has a clear description for a score of one, three and five.

She gives accuracy double weight, because a fact sheet with wrong health information could cause harm. Now her task, input, prompt and rubric are fixed, and she is ready to test. She will use three free text tools, and note the version and the date for each one.

A common mistake is to improve the prompt during testing, after the first tool gives a weak answer. Then the other tools get a better prompt, and the test is no longer fair. Finish the prompt and rubric first. Try the prompt once in a separate trial run if you need to. Then keep everything fixed.

Let's recap. First, a fair comparison gives every tool the same task, the same input and the same prompt. Second, decide the scoring criteria before you see any results, and define low, middle and high scores. Third, record the tool version and the date of every test, because generative AI tools change often.

This is capstone step one. In the exercise below, choose one real task, prepare the input with no personal data, write the exact prompt, and build a rubric with four to six criteria. Save it all in one document. It takes about thirty minutes. Next, we move on to running the comparison. See you there.
```

## L11 Running the Comparison

- **Filename:** `ai-02-generative-ai-explained-llms-diffusion-and-multimodal-models_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (687 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your task, input, prompt and rubric are ready. Now comes the part that feels easy, running the tests. But small mistakes here, like one changed word or a lost output, can make the whole comparison unfair. Let's run it carefully.

In the last lesson, you designed a fair test. This is capstone step two. Running it well is about discipline and records. You follow the same routine for every tool.

Before you start, open your capstone document with the fixed prompt, the input and the rubric. Create a results folder, with one folder inside for each tool. And create a log table, with the tool, the version shown, the date, any settings you changed, the output file, the scores and notes.

Then, for each tool. Start a new, empty chat, so earlier conversations do not affect the result. Write down the version the tool shows, or not shown. Note any settings you change. Paste the exact prompt and input, with no improvements.

Save the complete output, and take a screenshot. Then score the result straight away, before you open the next tool. That way you judge each tool against your criteria, not against another tool. Finally, note anything surprising, like a refusal or a long wait.

Which three tools? For a text task, you might use Claude or ChatGPT, Google AI Studio and a third free chatbot. For an image task, use three free image generators. And for photos or audio, choose tools that accept uploads.

It is like a science experiment in a school laboratory. You follow the method exactly, write down every measurement when you take it, and label every sample. If you try to remember the results the next day, nobody can trust the conclusion.

Let's watch a real run. Sione is a tourism officer at a visitor centre in Suva. His task is to turn a public information sheet about a nature park into six frequently asked questions for the centre's website.

He opens his capstone document and shows the fixed prompt. It asks for six questions and short answers for first-time visitors, using only the text, and without inventing missing information.

He opens the first tool, a free chatbot, and starts a new chat. He reads the model name shown on screen and types it into his log, with today's date. Then he pastes the prompt and the information sheet, and runs it.

He copies the full answer into a file called tool one output, and takes a screenshot. Then he scores it straight away. Accuracy four, clarity five, structure five, effort to fix four. His note says one answer adds a parking fee that is not in the sheet.

Next, he opens Google AI Studio, starts a new prompt, and checks that the temperature is at its default value. He records that in the log. Then he repeats the same steps, and saves tool two output.

He runs the third tool in exactly the same way, and saves tool three output. Now his log has three complete rows.

The scores differ, but Sione does not choose a winner yet. First, he checks that every row has a date, a version entry, a saved output and all the scores. The analysis comes next.

A common mistake is to run all three tools first, and score them later from memory, or side by side. Then a good output looks worse next to the best one, and details are forgotten. Score each output against your rubric straight away.

Let's recap. First, use a new chat for each tool, paste the exact same prompt and input, and record any settings you change. Second, save every complete output, with the tool name, the version shown and the date. Third, score each result against your rubric straight away, before you look at the next tool.

This is capstone step two. In the exercise below, run your task in three tools, save each output with the date and version, and score each one with your rubric. It takes about forty-five minutes. In the final lesson, you bring it all together by writing the comparison report. See you there.
```

## L12 Writing the Comparison Report

- **Filename:** `ai-02-generative-ai-explained-llms-diffusion-and-multimodal-models_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (683 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have three outputs, three sets of scores and a page of notes. Your manager does not want to read all of that. They want one answer. Which tool should we use for this task, and why? Your report must answer that clearly and honestly.

In the last lesson, you ran your tests. Now, in this final lesson, you write the report. This is capstone step three. A good report is short, and it follows six parts.

First, the task. What it is, who needs the output, and why it matters. Second, the method. The three tools, their versions and test dates, the exact prompt, the input, any settings, and your rubric. A reader should be able to repeat your test. Third, a results table, with one row per tool and a weighted total.

Fourth, strengths and failures. For each tool, say what it did well and where it failed. And here is the important part. Do not only describe a failure. Explain its likely cause, using what you learned about how the model works.

An invented fact means the model predicts likely text, not checked facts. An out-of-date statement points to the knowledge cut-off. Wrong letters in an image come from visual patterns. A misread number from a photo is a multimodal reading error. And very different answers each time can come from temperature or random starting noise.

Fifth, your recommendation. Name the tool for this task, and justify it with quality, cost, privacy and risk. Say what checks a person must still do. You can even recommend none of these. Sixth, the limits of your test. One task, one prompt, one day. Being honest here makes your report more trustworthy.

Think of a doctor's summary after tests. It says what was tested, shows the key results, explains what they mean, and gives a clear recommendation. The patient does not need every measurement, but they need to trust the method.

Keep the whole report to two or three pages, including the table. Use short paragraphs and clear headings. Then attach your prompt, your input and your outputs as an appendix, so a reader can check your work.

Let's look at an example. Amara is a coordinator at a non-profit organisation in Kigali. She tested three free chatbots on turning volunteer meeting notes into a short action list.

Her results table shows tool A with twenty-three points out of twenty-five, tool B with nineteen, and tool C with seventeen. Accuracy counts double. Tool A is ahead. But the numbers alone are not the report. The words around them matter more.

In strengths and failures, she writes that tool B had the clearest wording, but gave a volunteer a deadline that was not in the notes. She explains why. It is a typical hallucination. The model filled a gap with a likely detail.

Her recommendation is to use tool A for this task, because it was the most accurate and needed the least editing. A coordinator must still check every name and date, and personal phone numbers are removed before pasting. Her limits section says she tested one set of notes on one day.

A common mistake is to write, tool A is the best AI. That is too broad. Your test shows which tool worked best for one task, with one prompt, on one date. So limit your claim. For this task, under these conditions, tool A is the best choice.

Let's recap. First, a strong report has six parts. Task, method, results table, strengths and failures, recommendation, and limits. Second, explain each failure with its likely cause, using how that type of model works. Third, make a specific recommendation for this task, justify it, and state the limits of your test.

Congratulations. You have finished Generative AI Explained. You now know how language, diffusion and multimodal models work, where they fail, and how to choose between them. Your last step is the capstone. Write your two to three page report, check it against the capstone rubric and the submission checklist, and submit it on the course page. Well done, and good luck.
```
