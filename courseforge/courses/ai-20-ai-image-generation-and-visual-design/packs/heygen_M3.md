## L09 Spotting and Fixing Common Flaws

- **Filename:** `ai-20-ai-image-generation-and-visual-design_M3_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (688 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
At first glance, the image looks perfect. Then a customer points out that the waiter has six fingers, and the menu spells chicken wrong. Small flaws like these make a brand look careless. The good news is that most of them follow patterns.

Remember from lesson one, generators learn what things usually look like, not how they work. That is why some flaws appear again and again. So use this five-point quality checklist on every image before you publish it.

One, hands and faces. Count the fingers, and check the eyes, teeth and ears. They have many small parts and positions. Two, text and logos. Look for misspelled words and fake logo shapes, because most models do not spell reliably. Three, reflections and shadows. The model does not calculate real light. It imitates patterns.

Four, repeated patterns, like identical faces in a crowd, copied tiles, or a chair leg that joins the floor twice. The model fills large areas with similar patterns, especially after an expand. And five, style drift across a set, when the style block changed or there was no reference image.

For each flaw, choose one of three fixes. Regenerate when the flaw is large, in the main subject, or when many things are wrong. Edit the area with generative fill when the flaw is small. Crop it out when it is near the edge. And for text, remove it and add real text in Canva.

Checking an AI image is like proofreading a letter before you send it. You read slowly, line by line, looking for the mistakes you know are common. A quick look at the whole page is not enough. The same is true for images.

Let's work through an example. Layla manages a family restaurant in Amman, Jordan. She generated an image of a table with mezze dishes and a printed menu card for her new lunch offer. She checks it with the checklist, one point at a time, at full size.

Hands and faces. A waiter's hand at the edge has four fingers and a thumb that bends the wrong way. It is at the edge, and not needed, so she crops it out. Cropping uses no credits, and the rest of the image stays exactly the same.

Text. The menu card has warped letters, and the word lunch is spelled wrong. She uses generative fill to replace the lettering with a plain cream card, then adds the real menu text in Canva.

Shadows. The bowls' shadows fall to the left, but the window light also comes from the left. That is a large, noticeable error, so she regenerates, and adds soft window light from the left, shadows falling to the right, to the prompt.

Repeated patterns. Two olive dishes are identical in shape and position. She uses generative fill on one dish, with the prompt small bowl of pickled vegetables.

Style drift. Next to her other two menu images, the new one is cooler in colour. So she adds the palette line from her style block, and regenerates. After these fixes, the image is ready for Canva.

A common mistake is to check images only on a small phone preview. Flaws like extra fingers or broken letters are easy to miss at that size. Always view each image at full size, and zoom into hands, text and edges. Another mistake is to regenerate the whole image for every small flaw. That can create new flaws and waste credits. Choose the smallest fix that solves the problem.

Let's recap. First, check every image for five common flaws: hands and faces, text and logos, reflections and shadows, repeated patterns, and style drift. Second, each flaw has a likely cause, and knowing it helps you choose the right fix. Third, use the smallest fix that works, and add real text in a design tool.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, you will review six sample images with the checklist, name each flaw and its likely cause, and choose a fix. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at copyright, licences and ethics. See you there.
```

## L10 Copyright, Licences and Ethics

- **Filename:** `ai-20-ai-image-generation-and-visual-design_M3_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (679 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your image looks great, and the client loves it. But can they put it on a billboard? Can they stop a competitor from copying it? Does anyone need to know it was made with AI? Before you publish, ask the right questions.

First, an important point. This lesson teaches principles, not legal advice. The rules are different in different countries, and they are still changing. For a real client campaign, ask a qualified legal adviser in your country.

One, ownership and copyright. In many places, copyright protects work created by people. Whether a mostly AI-made image can be protected, and who may own it, differs by country. Your own contributions, like selecting and editing, may matter. So keep records of your prompts and edits. They show your creative work.

Two, tool licence terms. Each tool has its own terms of use. They say whether you may use images commercially, what rights you give the tool company, and whether free and paid plans differ. Terms change, so read them before each client project. And treat any company claims about training as statements to check yourself.

Three, people's likeness. Do not create images that look like real, identifiable people without their consent, and never make images that could mislead viewers about a real person. Four, trademarks and characters. Keep other companies' logos, brand names and copyrighted characters out of your prompts and images.

Five, bias. Models can repeat stereotypes, like showing only one gender as doctors, or only one skin colour as professional. Review who appears in your set, and prompt for the variety your audience really has. Six, disclosure. Some tools add visible or invisible signals that show an image was made with AI. Some platforms ask for labels. Do not try to remove these signals.

Publishing an AI image is like using a rented car for business. You can drive it, but the agreement says where you may go and for what purpose. Before a long trip, you read the agreement, and if something is unclear, you ask.

Here is a hypothetical case. Tomasz is a freelance designer in Warsaw, Poland. A small organic juice company asks him for ten AI images for a national poster campaign. Before he starts, he does five things.

First, he opens the terms of use for his tool, and finds the section about commercial use. He notes whether his free plan allows it, and whether paid plans have different rules. Second, he tells the client the images were made with AI, and asks whether they need exclusive rights. He explains that he cannot promise copyright protection.

Third, he finds a prompt line that describes a famous athlete. He removes it, and writes a runner in her forties instead. Fourth, he reviews the draft set for bias. All four family images show the same type of family, so he adds variety.

Fifth, he writes down the questions he cannot answer, and books a short call with a legal adviser. He does not guess. Writing the questions down also shows him exactly what is still uncertain.

A common mistake is to think, the tool let me make it, so I can use it however I want. The tool's ability says nothing about whether you may publish the image. Another mistake is to trust a summary on a blog or social post. Always read the current official terms yourself.

Let's recap. First, copyright and ownership of AI images differ by country and are changing, so treat them as questions to check, not settled facts. Second, read each tool's current terms before a client project, and keep records of your prompts and edits. Third, avoid real people's likeness, logos and characters, check your set for bias, and do not remove AI labels.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, you will read the commercial-use section of one tool's official terms, and write three questions for a legal adviser. It takes about twenty-five minutes. Next comes the capstone. In the next lesson, Capstone: Planning Your Brand Visual Set, you start your own campaign. See you there.
```

## L11 Capstone: Planning Your Brand Visual Set

- **Filename:** `ai-20-ai-image-generation-and-visual-design_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (681 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Twelve images that look like one campaign do not happen by luck. They happen because someone made a plan before the first prompt. In this lesson, you write that plan.

Here is the capstone brief. You produce a consistent set of twelve images for a real or fictional brand campaign, with a one-page style guide. The guide records your prompts, settings, edits and the tool terms you checked. You plan today, and produce in the next lesson. The rubric on the course page shows how your work is assessed.

A good plan has four parts. First, a moodboard. Collect six to ten images that show the feeling you want, like your own photos, earlier AI tests, or colour swatches. Use it only for your own direction, and do not upload other people's images without permission. It helps you see the feeling before you write a single word.

Second, the style guide. This is the heart of the plan. The template is on screen. It fixes your palette, one lighting setup, framing rules, the medium, the mood, two or three recurring elements, and a list of things to avoid, like text, logos and real people.

From the guide, you write one style block, as in lesson six. Describe colours in words in the prompt, and use the exact colour codes later in Canva. Third, the shot list. List all twelve images before you generate, with the subject, the format and where each one will be used.

Mix the formats, like square posts, tall stories and wide banners. And mix close-ups, medium shots and wide shots, so the set feels varied but consistent. Fourth, test images. Generate three very different shots. If they look like one set, the style block works. If not, fix it now.

Planning the set is like drawing a floor plan before you build a house. Changing a line on paper takes seconds. Moving a wall after it is built takes days.

Let's look at an example. Wanjiru is creating a fictional tea brand from Kericho, Kenya, called Highland Morning. Her campaign is called Tea at sunrise.

Her style guide sets a palette of deep leaf green, warm gold and cream. The lighting is low golden sunrise light from the left. The framing is eye level, with the subject in the left third and empty space on the right. And two elements recur, fresh green tea leaves and a woven sisal texture.

Her style block is on screen. It joins all of this into one paragraph of prompt words, and it describes the colours in words, not codes.

Her shot list includes a tea tin close-up as a square post, a steaming cup on a windowsill, tea fields at sunrise as a wide banner, and hands holding a cup, with no face, as a tall story. Eight more shots cover packaging, a breakfast table, loose leaves and seasonal offers.

She tests shots one, three and four. The wide field image has almost no gold light. So she moves low golden sunrise light to the start of the style block, and the next test matches. Three tests use far fewer credits than twelve images.

A common mistake is to start generating straight away and plan afterwards. You end up with thirty attractive images that do not form a set. And remember, consistency means a shared style, not twelve identical pictures.

Let's recap. First, a campaign set starts with a plan: a moodboard, a style guide, a style block and a twelve-image shot list. Second, the style guide fixes palette, lighting, framing and recurring elements, and the style block turns it into prompt words. Third, test three very different shots first, and fix the style block before you produce the full set.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone. You will write a one-page campaign plan with a style block, a palette and a twelve-image shot list, then generate three test images. It takes about forty minutes. In the final lesson, Capstone: Producing and Reviewing the Set, you bring it all together. See you there.
```

## L12 Capstone: Producing and Reviewing the Set

- **Filename:** `ai-20-ai-image-generation-and-visual-design_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (689 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have a plan, and three test images that match. Now comes the real work. Nine more images, each checked, edited and finished, and a style guide that lets someone else continue your campaign next month.

You produce the set in four stages. First, generate in batches of three or four images. Use the exact style block from the last lesson in every prompt, and add your best test image as a reference where your tool allows it. After each batch, place all the images side by side and check for drift.

Second, keep a prompt log. Record every final image as you go: the shot, the tool and settings, the aspect ratio, the full prompt, any reference, the edits, the checklist result and the file name. It is your evidence for the rubric, and it shows your own creative decisions.

Third, check and fix each image with the five-point checklist from lesson nine, and choose the smallest fix. Fourth, finish in Canva, with real headlines, logos you own and exact colours. Resize to the planned formats, and check every size by eye.

Before you submit, review the whole set with four questions. Consistency: do all twelve share the palette, light, framing and recurring elements? Brand fit: does each image serve its use? Rights: did you check every tool's terms, and avoid real people, logos and characters? Disclosure: how will you tell viewers the images were made with AI?

Producing the set is like a kitchen during a busy dinner service. The recipe is fixed, each plate is checked before it leaves, and the head cook writes down any change so the next shift can cook the same dish.

Let's see it in action. Mateo and his colleague Valentina are producing twelve images for Sunbird Juice, a fictional juice bar in Cartagena, Colombia. Their tested style block uses bright coral, mango yellow and palm green.

In batch one, the checklist finds a straw that bends through a glass, and a sign with broken letters. Mateo fixes the straw with generative fill, and removes the sign lettering, to add real text later in Canva.

In batch two, the light direction drifts in two images. Valentina adds the hero image from batch one as a reference, and the next results match. Because they checked after the batch, only two images needed fixing. They log every final prompt, including the edits.

In Canva, they add the headline Fresh from the coast in exact brand colours, then make story and banner versions. They check each size by eye, so the headline never covers a face or a glass.

In the final review, they notice that all five images with people show only young adults. So they regenerate two shots, an older couple sharing juice at a beach café, and a father and daughter at the counter. None of them shows an identifiable real person.

Finally, they record the tool terms they checked, with dates. And they add a line to the style guide. All images created with AI, and disclosed in campaign captions.

A common mistake is to do all the checking at the very end. By then, a drift in batch one has been copied into eleven images. Check after every batch. Another mistake is a style guide that lists only prompts. A useful guide also records settings, references, edits and the terms you checked, so another person can repeat your work.

Let's recap. First, generate in small batches with a fixed style block, and check for drift after every batch. Second, keep a prompt log with prompts, settings, references and edits. It is both your evidence and your guide. Third, finish every image in a design tool, then review the full set for consistency, brand fit, rights and disclosure.

Congratulations, you have reached the end of the course. Your last step is to produce and finish all twelve images, and write your one-page style guide. It takes about an hour. Then submit your capstone on the course page, where the rubric shows how it is assessed. Good style guides let someone else make a thirteenth image in your style. Well done.
```
