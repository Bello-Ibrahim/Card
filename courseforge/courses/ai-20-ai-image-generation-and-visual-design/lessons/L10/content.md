# L10 Copyright, Licences and Ethics

Course: AI-20 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Your image looks great and the client loves it. But can they put it on a billboard? Can they stop a competitor from copying it? Does anyone need to know it was made with AI? Before you publish, you need to ask the right questions.

## Explanation
This lesson teaches principles, not legal advice. The rules are different in different countries and they are still changing. For a real client campaign, ask a qualified legal adviser in your country.

**1. Ownership and copyright.** In many places, copyright protects work created by people. Whether an image made mostly by an AI tool can be protected, and who may own it, differs by country and is under discussion. [VERIFY] [REGION] Human contributions such as selecting, arranging and editing images may matter in some places. [VERIFY] [REGION] Keep records of your prompts and edits; they show your own creative work.

**2. Tool licence terms.** Each tool has its own terms of use. These say whether you may use images commercially, what rights you give the tool company, and whether free and paid plans have different rules. Terms change, so read them before each client project. [VERSION] [VERIFY] Some companies also make claims about how their models were trained, such as using licensed images. Treat these as company statements and read the current official terms yourself. [VERIFY]

**3. People's likeness.** Do not create images that look like real, identifiable people without their consent, and never create images that could mislead viewers about what a real person said or did. Many tools block this, and the law in many places protects a person's image. [REGION]

**4. Trademarks and characters.** Do not put other companies' logos, brand names or copyrighted characters into your prompts or images.

**5. Bias in who is shown.** Models can repeat stereotypes from their training data, for example showing only one gender as doctors or one skin colour as "professional". Review who appears in your set and prompt for the variety your audience really has.

**6. Disclosure and labels.** Some tools add signals that show an image was made or edited with AI. Google describes an invisible watermark called SynthID for some of its AI images. [VERIFY] Adobe describes Content Credentials, which are based on an open standard called C2PA and attach information about how an image was made. [VERIFY] Some platforms and some countries ask for AI images to be labelled. [VERIFY] [REGION] Do not try to remove these signals.

**Analogy:** Publishing an AI image is like using a rented car for business. You can drive it, but the rental agreement says where you may go and for what purpose. Before a long business trip, you read the agreement, and if something is unclear, you ask.

## Worked Example
This is a hypothetical case. Tomasz is a freelance designer in Warsaw, Poland. A client, a small organic juice company, asks for 10 AI images for a national poster campaign. Before starting, Tomasz does the following:

- He opens the terms of use for the tool he plans to use and finds the section about commercial use. He notes whether his free plan allows commercial use and whether the rules differ for paid plans. [VERSION] [VERIFY]
- He tells the client that the images were made with AI, and asks whether the client needs exclusive rights. He explains that he cannot promise copyright protection for the images. [VERIFY] [REGION]
- He removes a prompt line that described a famous athlete, and uses a generic "runner in her forties" instead.
- He reviews the draft set for bias. All four "family" images show the same type of family, so he adds variety.
- He writes down the questions he cannot answer and books a short call with a legal adviser.

## Common Mistake
Many creators think that "the tool let me make it, so I can use it however I want". A tool's ability to create an image says nothing about whether you may publish it, whether it can be protected, or whether it copies protected material. Another mistake is to trust a summary on a blog or social post. Always read the current official terms yourself.

## Key Takeaways
1. Copyright and ownership of AI images differ by country and are changing, so treat them as questions to check, not settled facts.
2. Read each tool's current terms for commercial use before a client project, and keep records of your prompts and edits.
3. Avoid real people's likeness, logos and characters, check your set for bias, and do not remove AI labels or content credentials.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Find and read the commercial-use section of the terms for one tool you use, then write 3 questions you would ask a legal adviser before a client campaign.
**Tools:** The official terms of use page for Gemini, Adobe Firefly or Canva; a notes app. [VERSION]
**Steps:**
1. Choose one tool you used in this course.
2. Open its official terms of use or generative AI terms from the company's own website. Do not rely on third-party summaries. [VERIFY]
3. Find the section about commercial use, ownership of outputs, or restrictions.
4. Write 3 to 5 bullet points in your own words: what it allows, what it restricts, and anything that differs between free and paid plans.
5. Note the date you read the terms.
6. Write 3 questions for a legal adviser, for example about ownership in your country or about a client's need for exclusive rights.
**What good looks like:** A short, dated summary of the terms in plain words, taken from the official source, and 3 clear questions that show you understand what is uncertain. No legal conclusions are stated as fact.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
