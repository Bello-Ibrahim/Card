# L02 Health Data: Types, Quality and Privacy

Course: AI-25 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O4 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
An AI tool is only as good as the data it learned from. If blood pressure was missing for half of the older patients in the training data, what has the tool really learned about older patients?

## Explanation
Health AI uses six main types of data. Each has typical strengths and problems.

- **Electronic health records (EHR):** diagnoses, medicines, notes, vital signs. Rich, but often incomplete and recorded for care or billing, not for research.
- **Images:** X-rays, scans, photographs of skin or the eye. Quality depends on the device, settings and the person taking the image.
- **Laboratory results:** usually well structured, but units, reference ranges and test methods differ between laboratories.
- **Wearables and home devices:** step counts, heart rate, glucose readings. Continuous, but the people who own these devices are often younger and wealthier than the general population.
- **Claims and billing data:** large and consistent, but they show what was paid for, not always what happened clinically.
- **Surveys:** they reach people who do not use health services, but depend on memory and honest answers.

Three quality problems appear again and again:

1. **Missing values.** Data is rarely missing at random. A test may be missing because the clinician thought it was not needed, which is itself information.
2. **Different coding systems.** One hospital records a diagnosis with one code set, another uses a different version or free text. Combining them without careful mapping creates errors.
3. **Unrepresentative samples.** If the data comes mostly from one type of hospital, region or group of patients, a model may work less well for everyone else. You will study this in L09.

Health data also needs extra privacy protection. It can reveal illness, pregnancy, mental health, HIV status or genetic information, and exposure can lead to discrimination, stigma or loss of work. Removing names is not enough: a rare diagnosis plus a village and an age may identify a person.

**Analogy:** A patient record is like a diary. In the hands of the right reader, such as the patient's own care team, it helps. In the wrong hands, it can cause real harm. Treat every dataset as someone's diary, even when it looks like a table of numbers.

In this course you only use **public** or **synthetic** data. Synthetic data is generated by a computer to look realistic but describes no real person. Public aggregated sources, such as the WHO Global Health Observatory, publish country-level indicators, not individual records [VERIFY] [VERSION]. Never paste identifiable patient data into a public AI tool.

## Worked Example
Nguyen Thi Lan is a public health researcher in Vietnam. She is given a hypothetical synthetic dataset of 500 adult clinic patients to plan an analysis of blood pressure control. Before any modelling, she explores it.

She lists the variables: age, sex, district, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, smoking status, HbA1c and date of last visit.

She then counts missing values:

| Variable | Missing |
|---|---|
| Smoking status | 31% |
| HbA1c | 58% |
| BMI | 12% |
| Systolic blood pressure | 4% |

HbA1c is missing for more than half of patients. Lan checks and sees that it is recorded mostly for patients who already have a diabetes diagnosis. If she trained a model on this data, it might learn "HbA1c present" as a sign of diabetes, which is a pattern in the recording process, not in the patients.

She also sees that 80% of rows come from two urban districts. She writes a note: "Results may not apply to rural districts."

Finally, she asks the privacy question: if this data were real, could a person be identified? District, exact age and a rare diagnosis together could point to one person in a small district. Her plan for real data would be to group ages into bands, remove exact dates and follow her institution's data protection rules.

## Common Mistake
Many people believe that data is anonymous once names and ID numbers are removed. In health data this is often false. Combinations of age, location, dates and rare conditions can identify people, especially in small communities. Treat "de-identified" data as still sensitive, keep it in approved systems and follow your organisation's data protection officer's advice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Health AI uses EHRs, images, laboratory results, wearables, claims and surveys, and each type has its own typical gaps and biases.
2. Missing values, different coding systems and unrepresentative samples can teach a model patterns that come from the recording process, not from patients.
3. Health data needs extra privacy protection; removing names is not enough, and identifiable patient data must never go into public AI tools.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Explore a public health dataset or the course's synthetic patient dataset. List its variables, note where data is missing, and describe one privacy risk if the data were real.
**Tools:** The course's synthetic patient dataset (CSV) opened in Google Sheets, Excel or LibreOffice Calc (free), or a public aggregated source such as the WHO Global Health Observatory [VERIFY] [VERSION].
**Steps:**
1. Open the dataset and write down every variable name and what it measures.
2. For each variable, count or estimate how many values are missing (for example with a COUNTBLANK formula).
3. For the two variables with the most missing data, write one possible reason why they are missing.
4. Note who seems to be over-represented or under-represented, for example by age, sex or location.
5. Write one sentence describing a privacy risk if this data were about real people, and one step that would reduce it.
**What good looks like:** A complete variable list, missing-data counts, a reasoned explanation for at least two gaps, one note on representativeness, and a specific privacy risk (such as re-identification through location and age) with a realistic protective step.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
