# L06 AI for Hospital and Clinic Operations | Presenter Script

Course: AI-25 · Video: 5 min · Words: 686

## Hook
Every missed appointment is a lost chance to care for someone, and an empty slot another patient could have used. A model can predict who is likely to miss a visit. The harder question is what you do with that prediction.

## Explain
Welcome to week two. Last week, we learned how clinical AI is built and judged. Now we turn to operations. Operational AI supports how a hospital or clinic runs. Common uses are scheduling, predicting missed appointments, managing beds, planning supplies, and drafting documents that clinicians review and sign.

These tools do not make clinical decisions, but they still affect patients. Suppose a model finds that people who live far away miss more appointments. One clinic might overbook their slots, so if they do arrive, they wait longer or are sent home. Another clinic offers transport support or a phone consultation. Same prediction, opposite effect on equity.

There is a second point. A factor linked with missed appointments is not proof that it causes them. Distance may stand in for cost, work hours or poor transport. Staff who know the community should interpret the pattern before choosing any action.

Here is a simple way to picture it. A missed-appointment model is like a weather forecast. A forecast of rain does not tell you to cancel the market. It tells you to bring shelter. A good clinic uses a risk forecast to prepare support, not to shut people out.

## Demonstrate
Let's see this on screen. Rosa Quispe manages a hypothetical outpatient clinic in Cusco, Peru. Many patients miss follow-up visits. She asks a data analyst to explore a synthetic dataset of two thousand appointments, shaped like her clinic's data.

We open the course notebook in Google Colab and sign in with a Google account. The first text cell says that all the data is generated by the code, and no real patient data is uploaded. Then we choose Runtime, and Run all.

We scroll to the Step two output. The code groups patients into distance bands, then prints the missed rate for each distance band, for reminder sent or not, and for each age group. Remember, these are synthetic numbers. Overall, about thirty percent of appointments were missed.

Look at distance. Patients living more than thirty kilometres away missed fifty-six percent of visits, compared with twenty-two percent for those within five kilometres. Patients who received a reminder missed twenty-one percent, compared with forty percent without one. And age group made almost no difference.

Finally, the notebook shows a simple logistic regression. Positive values go with more missed appointments, and negative values with fewer. Distance, days of waiting and previous missed visits are positive. Sending a reminder is clearly negative. These are associations in synthetic data, not causes.

Rosa reads the results with her team, and they talk about what the numbers might mean for their patients. She rejects the idea of overbooking distant patients. Instead, she proposes reminders for every booking, in Spanish or Quechua as the patient prefers, and a phone or video option for follow-up visits for patients who live far away, with transport support when an in-person visit is needed.

She plans to measure missed-visit rates by distance band after three months. A common mistake is to treat a risk score as a decision, and automatically overbook or cancel. Decide the action with staff and patients, and check whether it reduces differences between groups.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, operational AI supports scheduling, missed-appointment prediction, bed and supply planning, and documentation that clinicians review. Second, a prediction is not a cause, and it is not a decision. Staff who know the community should interpret it. Third, use predictions to offer support, not to penalise groups who already face barriers.
