## L06 Safety Risks and Human Oversight

- **Filename:** `ai-29-ai-ethics-bias-and-safety_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (676 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A human is in the loop, the vendor says. But what if that human has four hundred AI decisions to approve before lunch, and no power to change any of them? Is that really human control?

Welcome to week two. In AI, safety means avoiding physical, financial, emotional or social harm. Four kinds of safety risk appear again and again. The first is harmful advice, such as a wrong medicine dose or a risky money tip. The second is unsafe instructions, such as how to mix dangerous chemicals.

The third is over-trust. People accept the AI's answer without checking, because it is fast, confident and usually right. When it is wrong, nobody notices. The fourth is high-stakes use without checks, in areas like health, credit, hiring or public services.

Many AI tools have safety filters, but filters are not perfect. The strongest protection is often human oversight. That means a person can question and change the AI's decision. For this to be real, and not just a signature, the person needs three things. Time, knowledge and authority. Authority means the power to change or stop the decision, without being punished for it.

Where should a person review the output? A simple rule. The higher the impact of a mistake, the closer the human review. A blog title may need only spot checks. A loan rejection needs review before it takes effect, or an easy way to appeal.

Think of an aeroplane autopilot. It is very useful, and it flies smoothly for hours. But the pilot is still trained, alert and able to take control at any moment, especially at take-off, landing and in bad weather. Human oversight of AI works the same way. The system does the routine work, and a capable person stays ready to step in when it matters most.

Let's look at an example. Youssef Benali runs customer support for a chain of pharmacies in Morocco. The company launches a website chatbot to answer questions about opening hours, stock and deliveries. In the first month, Youssef reviews a sample of chats.

He finds three safety risks. A customer asks how many pain tablets they can take in a day, and the chatbot gives a number without asking about age or other medicines. That is harmful advice.

Another customer asks if two medicines can be taken together, and the chatbot says yes, with nobody checking. That is high-stakes use without checks. And staff copy the chatbot's answers into emails without reading them, because it is usually right. That is over-trust.

Youssef decides where oversight is needed. For opening hours, stock and deliveries, the chatbot answers alone, and staff review a weekly sample. For any question about doses, side effects or combining medicines, the chatbot does not answer. It passes the question to a pharmacist on duty.

The pharmacist has time in their schedule for this, the training to answer, and the authority to contact the customer directly. And a new rule says staff must read every AI-drafted reply before sending it.

A common mistake is to believe that adding a person anywhere solves the problem. A person with no time, training or power only approves what the machine already decided. The correction is to check that the reviewer has enough time, knowledge and authority, and to place the review where a mistake would hurt most.

Let's recap. First, key AI safety risks include harmful advice, unsafe instructions, over-trust in automated decisions, and high-stakes use without checks. Second, human oversight is real only when the reviewer has enough time, knowledge and authority. Third, the higher the impact of a possible mistake, the closer and earlier the human review should be.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will look at three uses of AI, an essay grader in Egypt, a bank chatbot in India and a crop-disease app in Ghana, and decide where a human should review the output. It takes about fifteen minutes. In the next lesson, we will look at responsible AI frameworks in practice. See you there.
```

## L07 Responsible AI Frameworks in Practice

- **Filename:** `ai-29-ai-ethics-bias-and-safety_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (676 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Governments, international organisations and companies have written many documents about responsible AI. They have different names and lengths. But read them side by side, and you find the same few ideas again and again. Those ideas can fit on a card on your desk.

In the last lesson, we looked at safety and human oversight. Now we bring the course together. A responsible AI framework is a set of principles that describes how AI should be built and used. Some are written by international organisations, some by governments, and many by companies for their own staff.

Two well-known international examples come from the OECD and from UNESCO. In this beginner course, we do not study their exact wording. Instead, we look at the ideas that they and many company frameworks share.

Six core principles appear in most frameworks. Fairness, so AI does not treat people unjustly. Transparency, so people know when AI is used. Privacy, so personal and confidential data is protected. Safety, so AI does not cause harm, and outputs are checked. Accountability, so a named person is responsible. And human oversight, so a capable person can question and change decisions.

Frameworks can feel far away from daily work. So the practical step is to turn each principle into one question you ask yourself, before, during or after you use an AI tool. That is a responsible-use checklist. It does not replace your organisation's rules. Legal duties are covered in course AI thirty.

Before take-off, pilots use a short checklist, even after thousands of flights. It does not teach them to fly. It makes sure they do not forget something important when they are busy. Your checklist does the same for AI. It gives you a few quick questions that stop you from skipping a step on a busy day.

Let's see one in use. Katarzyna Nowak works at a marketing agency in Poland. Her team uses a chatbot to draft social media posts and client reports. After this course, she writes six questions, one for each principle.

For example. For privacy, have I removed all personal and client-confidential details? For safety, have I checked every fact and number against a trusted source? For fairness, could this output use stereotypes? And for human oversight, did I read and edit the output myself? Transparency and accountability get a question too.

She uses it on a real task, an AI-drafted post for a client's new product. Her prompt included the client's unreleased price, so she replaces it with a placeholder. The draft claims the product reduces energy use by forty percent. She cannot find this in the client's material, so she deletes it. And the draft shows only young people, so she widens the description.

Her agency's rule is to tell clients when AI helped, so she adds a note to her email. She edits the final text herself, and she signs off the post as account manager. The whole checklist takes about five minutes, and it catches three real problems that would otherwise have reached the client.

A common mistake is to read a framework once and file it away. Principles only help if they change what you do, so use your questions every time. Write each question so you can answer yes or no for a real task. And always check official sources before quoting a named framework.

Let's recap. First, many international and company frameworks share the same core principles: fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, accountability and human oversight. Second, principles become useful when you turn each one into a short question for a real task. Third, a responsible-use checklist supports, but does not replace, your organisation's rules and the law.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will build your own six-question checklist and use it on one real task you did with an AI tool this month. Keep it, because you will use it again in your capstone audit. In the next lesson, we will start that capstone by testing an AI tool for bias. See you there.
```

## L08 Testing an AI Tool for Bias

- **Filename:** `ai-29-ai-ethics-bias-and-safety_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (692 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Two people have the same CV, word for word. The only difference is the name at the top. If you ask a chatbot to write a job reference for each of them, will it describe them in the same way?

In the last lesson, you built a responsible-use checklist. Now we start your capstone. You cannot see inside a chatbot, but you can test what it writes. The simplest structured test is a paired prompt. You send the same request twice, and change only one detail, such as a name, age, gender, country or disability. Then you compare the answers.

Good testing follows five rules. Change one detail only. Start a new chat for every prompt. Repeat each pair at least three times, because answers vary. Record the exact prompts and answers. And describe your findings carefully, because a few prompts are a limited test, not proof of bias.

Always follow the tool's terms of use, and never use real personal data. Invented names and CVs only. And remember that chatbots change between versions, so your results show one version at one time.

Think of a fair taste test. You give people the same drink in the same kind of cup, and change only the label. If they rate the drinks differently, the label is the likely reason. If you also change the cup, you learn nothing.

Let's run a test on screen. Tomás Herrera is an HR officer in Chile. Before his team drafts references with a chatbot, he wants to test it. We will follow his steps in a free chatbot and a spreadsheet.

First, open a spreadsheet with these columns. Pair number, detail changed, prompt A, prompt B, run, answer A, answer B, and differences noticed. Next, write an invented CV. A project coordinator with five years' experience, who managed a team of six and delivered twelve projects on time.

Now write prompt A. Write a short job reference for Amina, a project coordinator with this CV. Then copy it and change only the name to Lucas. That is prompt B. Check carefully that nothing else has changed.

Open a new chat, paste prompt A, and copy the full answer into the spreadsheet. Open another new chat, paste prompt B, and copy that answer too. Then repeat both, two more times, so each prompt has three runs.

Now compare. Highlight describing words, such as caring, supportive, confident or decisive. Note the length, the skills and any leadership words. Here is what you might see. Perhaps leadership words appear in one of three Amina answers, and in three of three Lucas answers. Your results may be different.

Finally, write one careful sentence. For example, in this limited test of three runs, the Lucas references used more leadership words, and more tests are needed. Notice what it does not say. It does not say the tool is biased.

A common mistake is to run one pair, see a difference, and say the tool is biased. One difference may be random. The opposite mistake is also common. One pair looks the same, so people say the tool is fair. Repeat each pair, test several details, and record everything.

For your notes, here is a short case with the same structure. A bank tested its chatbot with twenty pairs, changing only the customer's age. It saw a pattern, called it a limited test, asked its vendor to investigate, and repeated the test each month.

Let's recap. First, a paired prompt sends the same request twice and changes only one detail. Second, use a new chat for each prompt, repeat each pair several times, and record the exact prompts and answers. Third, follow the tool's terms, never use real personal data, and describe findings as a limited test, not proof of bias.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone. Choose a real AI tool, run at least five pairs of prompts that each change one detail, and record the differences in a table. It takes about forty minutes. Keep your table, because in the next lesson, we will rate and prioritise the risks you find. See you there.
```

## L09 Rating and Prioritising Risks

- **Filename:** `ai-29-ai-ethics-bias-and-safety_M2_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (679 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
After a good audit, you may have a list of ten risks. You cannot fix all ten this week. So which two do you fix first, and how do you explain that choice to your manager?

In the last lesson, you tested a tool with paired prompts. Now you need to decide what matters most. Not all risks are equal. A useful way to compare them is a risk matrix, which rates each risk on two scales. Likelihood asks how often this could happen. Impact asks how badly it could hurt someone. Each is rated low, medium or high.

Place each risk in a three by three grid. Risks in the top-right corner, high likelihood and high impact, are fixed first. And a high-impact risk usually deserves attention even when it is rare, because the harm is serious. Always ask who is harmed. A risk that falls mainly on one group is also a fairness problem.

Your ratings are judgements, not exact measurements. So write a short reason for each one, so others can understand and question it.

Then choose ways to reduce the risks. There are five common options. Better data, which includes the missing groups. Clear limits, which stop the tool from handling some tasks. Human review before outputs are used. User warnings about what the tool can and cannot do. And monitoring, because tools and uses change. Choose options the team can do in weeks, not only in an ideal world.

Think of a hospital emergency room deciding who to see first. A small cut that happens often is less urgent than a rare but serious chest pain. Staff treat the most urgent first. Nobody is ignored, but not everyone is seen at the same time.

Let's rate some real-looking risks. Dewi Lestari leads quality at a telecom company in Indonesia. Its customer-service chatbot answers questions about bills, data plans and network problems. Her team has tested it and found four risks.

Risk one. The chatbot gives wrong prices for data plans. That is high likelihood, medium impact. Risk two. Customers type their full ID number into the chat, and it is stored. That is medium likelihood, but high impact, because stored numbers could be misused.

Risk three. The chatbot understands regional languages less well, so those customers get weaker answers. That is high likelihood, medium impact, and also a fairness problem. Risk four. It tells a customer who reports a phone fire to restart the device. It was seen only once, but the impact is high, because it is a physical danger.

Dewi's team fixes two first. For the ID numbers, the chatbot warns customers not to share them, hides long number sequences, and the IT team checks what is stored. For the fire risk, a clear limit. Any message about fire, smoke or injury gets a fixed safety message and passes to a human agent.

Risks one and three are planned for next month, with better price data and weekly checks, more testing in regional languages, and a talk to a person button. Dewi records all four, so none is forgotten.

A common mistake is to fix the most frequent risk first, because it is the most visible. But a rare, high-impact risk can matter more. Always rate both scales. Another mistake is to choose unrealistic fixes, such as rebuild the model. Pick actions the team can take soon.

Let's recap. First, a risk matrix rates each risk by likelihood and impact, and the highest combinations are fixed first. Second, serious, high-impact risks need attention even when they are rare, and risks that fall mainly on one group are also fairness problems. Third, realistic ways to reduce risk are better data, clear limits, human review, user warnings and monitoring.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step two of your capstone. List at least five risks for the tool you tested, place each one on the matrix, and pick your top three. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next and final lesson, we will write your audit and recommendations. See you there.
```

## L10 Writing Your Audit and Recommendations

- **Filename:** `ai-29-ai-ethics-bias-and-safety_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (691 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have tested a tool and rated its risks. Now imagine your manager has five minutes to read your work. What must they understand, and what should they do next?

In the last lesson, you rated and prioritised risks. Now you turn your tests and ratings into decisions, with an audit report. A good report is short and clear. It answers four questions. What did you test? What did you find? How serious is it? And what should change?

For your capstone, use a template with seven headings, and keep it to one or two pages. Scope, test design, paired prompts, findings, severity, limitations and recommendations. Limitations matter. They say what your test could not show, such as few runs, one tool version, or invented cases only.

Scope says the tool, the plan, the date, and the uses you looked at. Test design says how you tested. The paired prompts section gives a short table of your key pairs, with the full table in an appendix. Findings are three to five clear statements, each with evidence, including what worked well. Severity comes from your risk matrix.

Good recommendations are specific, realistic and assigned. Compare these. A weak one says, make the tool fairer. A strong one says, the HR team reviews every AI-drafted reference before sending, and checks for differences in describing words, from next month.

And write carefully. Say in four of five runs, not the tool is biased. This is honest, and it makes your report harder to dismiss.

Think of a car inspection report. The mechanic does not write a novel. They list what they checked, what is wrong, how urgent each problem is, and what to repair first. They also say what they could not check. Then the owner can make a clear decision.

Let's look at a short audit. Rania Haddad works in student services at a university in Jordan. Her team plans to use a free chatbot to draft replies to student questions. First, she writes her scope and test design.

She tested replies about fees, deadlines and student support, but not questions in other languages. She ran six paired prompts, changing name, nationality, age or disability, three runs each. She also did five fact checks against the university website, and a privacy review.

Her findings. The chatbot gave a wrong deadline in two of five fact checks. Replies to international students added visa warnings nobody asked for, in three of three runs. Staff would need to share student ID numbers for some tasks. And the tone was polite and clear in every test.

Wrong deadlines are the highest priority, because students could miss one. Student ID sharing and the visa warnings are high priority. Her limitations are honest. A small number of runs, one tool version, and invented cases only.

Then she writes three recommendations. Staff check every date and fee on the university website, owned by the team lead. Staff use placeholders instead of names or ID numbers, owned by Rania. And the paired tests are repeated each term, owned by the quality officer.

A common mistake is a long report with strong claims. Keep it short, lead with the most serious findings, and state your limitations. Make every recommendation specific, realistic and assigned. This is a practical audit, not a legal review. Legal questions belong to course AI thirty and to your organisation's legal team.

Let's recap. First, a good audit report is short, and says what you tested, what you found, how serious it is and what should change. Second, use the template: scope, test design, paired prompts, findings, severity, limitations and recommendations. Third, recommendations should be specific, realistic and assigned, and findings should be described as limited tests.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is the final step of your capstone. Write a one to two page audit report on your chosen tool, with your test results, your risk ratings and at least three recommendations. Check it against the capstone rubric, then submit it.

Congratulations on finishing AI Ethics, Bias and Safety. You now have the skills to question, test and improve the AI tools around you. Well done.
```
