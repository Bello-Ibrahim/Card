# L07 Responsible AI Frameworks in Practice | Presenter Script

Course: AI-29 · Video: 5 min · Words: 683

## Hook
Governments, international organisations and companies have written many documents about responsible AI. They have different names and lengths. But read them side by side, and you find the same few ideas again and again. Those ideas can fit on a card on your desk.

## Explain
In the last lesson, we looked at safety and human oversight. Now we bring the course together. A responsible AI framework is a set of principles that describes how AI should be built and used. Some are written by international organisations, some by governments, and many by companies for their own staff.

Two well-known international examples come from the OECD and from UNESCO. In this beginner course, we do not study their exact wording. Instead, we look at the ideas that they and many company frameworks share.

Six core principles appear in most frameworks. Fairness, so AI does not treat people unjustly. Transparency, so people know when AI is used. Privacy, so personal and confidential data is protected. Safety, so AI does not cause harm, and outputs are checked. Accountability, so a named person is responsible. And human oversight, so a capable person can question and change decisions.

Frameworks can feel far away from daily work. So the practical step is to turn each principle into one question you ask yourself, before, during or after you use an AI tool. That is a responsible-use checklist. It does not replace your organisation's rules. Legal duties are covered in course AI thirty.

Before take-off, pilots use a short checklist, even after thousands of flights. It does not teach them to fly. It makes sure they do not forget something important when they are busy. Your checklist does the same for AI. It gives you a few quick questions that stop you from skipping a step on a busy day.

## Demonstrate
Let's see one in use. Katarzyna Nowak works at a marketing agency in Poland. Her team uses a chatbot to draft social media posts and client reports. After this course, she writes six questions, one for each principle.

For example. For privacy, have I removed all personal and client-confidential details? For safety, have I checked every fact and number against a trusted source? For fairness, could this output use stereotypes? And for human oversight, did I read and edit the output myself? Transparency and accountability get a question too.

She uses it on a real task, an AI-drafted post for a client's new product. Her prompt included the client's unreleased price, so she replaces it with a placeholder. The draft claims the product reduces energy use by forty percent. She cannot find this in the client's material, so she deletes it. And the draft shows only young people, so she widens the description.

Her agency's rule is to tell clients when AI helped, so she adds a note to her email. She edits the final text herself, and she signs off the post as account manager. The whole checklist takes about five minutes, and it catches three real problems that would otherwise have reached the client.

A common mistake is to read a framework once and file it away. Principles only help if they change what you do, so use your questions every time. Write each question so you can answer yes or no for a real task. And always check official sources before quoting a named framework.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, many international and company frameworks share the same core principles: fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, accountability and human oversight. Second, principles become useful when you turn each one into a short question for a real task. Third, a responsible-use checklist supports, but does not replace, your organisation's rules and the law.
