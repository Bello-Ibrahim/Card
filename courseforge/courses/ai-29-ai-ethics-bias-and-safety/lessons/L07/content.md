# L07 Responsible AI Frameworks in Practice

Course: AI-29 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O4 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Governments, international organisations and companies have written many documents about "responsible AI". They have different names and different lengths. But if you read them side by side, you find the same few ideas again and again. Those ideas can fit on a card on your desk.

## Explanation
A **responsible AI framework** is a set of principles that describes how AI should be built and used. Some are written by international organisations, some by governments and many by companies for their own staff.

Two well-known international examples are the **OECD AI Principles** [VERIFY] and the **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence** [VERIFY]. Both were agreed by many countries, and both describe, at a high level, how AI should respect people and society. In this beginner course we do not study their exact wording. Instead, we look at the ideas that they and many company frameworks share.

Six core principles appear in most frameworks:

1. **Fairness:** AI should not treat people or groups unjustly. (L02, L03)
2. **Transparency:** people should know when AI is used and understand, in simple terms, how it reaches results. (L01)
3. **Privacy:** personal and confidential data should be protected. (L05)
4. **Safety:** AI should not cause physical, financial, emotional or social harm, and outputs should be checked. (L04, L06)
5. **Accountability:** a named person or team is responsible for the results. (L01)
6. **Human oversight:** a capable person can question and change the AI's decisions. (L06)

Frameworks can feel far away from daily work. The practical step is to turn each principle into **one question you ask yourself** before, during or after you use an AI tool. That is a **responsible-use checklist**.

Legal duties, such as data protection laws and AI laws, are a separate topic and are covered in course AI-30. A checklist does not replace your organisation's rules. It helps you follow good practice in everyday tasks.

**Analogy:** Before take-off, pilots use a short checklist, even after thousands of flights. The checklist does not teach them to fly. It makes sure they do not forget something important when they are busy. A responsible-use checklist does the same for AI: a few quick questions that stop you from skipping a step on a busy day.

## Worked Example
Katarzyna Nowak works at a hypothetical marketing agency in Poland. Her team uses a chatbot to draft social media posts and client reports. After this course, she writes a six-question checklist, one question per principle:

| Principle | My question |
|---|---|
| Privacy | Have I removed all personal and client-confidential details from my prompt? |
| Safety | Have I checked every fact, number and source against a trusted source? |
| Fairness | Could this output treat any group unfairly or use stereotypes? |
| Transparency | Does the reader need to know that AI helped create this, and have I said so where our rules require it? |
| Human oversight | Did I read and edit the output myself before it was used? |
| Accountability | If this output causes a problem, do I know who is responsible and how to fix it? |

She uses it on a real task: an AI-drafted post for a client's new product.
- **Privacy:** her prompt included the client's unreleased price. She removes it and uses a placeholder.
- **Safety:** the draft claims the product "reduces energy use by 40%". She cannot find this figure in the client's material, so she deletes it.
- **Fairness:** the draft shows only young people using the product. She widens the description.
- **Transparency:** her agency's rule is to tell clients when AI helped. She adds a note to her email.
- **Human oversight:** she edits the final text herself.
- **Accountability:** she, as account manager, signs off the post.

The checklist takes her about five minutes and catches three real problems.

## Common Mistake
Many people think a framework is a document to read once and file away. Principles only help if they change what you do. The correction: turn each principle into a short, specific question that you can answer "yes" or "no" for a real task, and use it every time. A second mistake is to copy a framework's wording and assume it is exact. Always check official sources before quoting a named framework.

## Key Takeaways
1. Many international and company frameworks share the same core principles: fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, accountability and human oversight.
2. Principles become useful when you turn each one into a short question you can answer for a real task.
3. A responsible-use checklist supports, but does not replace, your organisation's rules and the law.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Build a personal 6-question responsible-use checklist from the shared principles, then use it on one real task you did with an AI tool this month.
**Tools:** A notes app, a document or paper. Optional: Claude or ChatGPT (free versions) to suggest wording, without sharing any personal or confidential details.
**Steps:**
1. Write the six principles: fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, accountability and human oversight.
2. For each one, write one question you can answer "yes" or "no" about a real task. Use your own words and your own type of work.
3. Choose one real task you did with an AI tool this month, such as an email, a summary or a report.
4. Answer each question for that task. Where the answer is "no" or "not sure", write what you would do differently.
5. Keep your checklist. You will use it again in your capstone.
**What good looks like:** Six clear questions, one per principle, written for your own work. Your answers on the real task are honest, and at least one answer leads to a specific change, such as "check the two figures against the official report".
**Time:** about 15 minutes
