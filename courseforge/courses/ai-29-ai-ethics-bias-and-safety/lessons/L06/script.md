# L06 Safety Risks and Human Oversight | Presenter Script

Course: AI-29 · Video: 5 min · Words: 685

## Hook
A human is in the loop, the vendor says. But what if that human has four hundred AI decisions to approve before lunch, and no power to change any of them? Is that really human control?

## Explain
Welcome to week two. In AI, safety means avoiding physical, financial, emotional or social harm. Four kinds of safety risk appear again and again. The first is harmful advice, such as a wrong medicine dose or a risky money tip. The second is unsafe instructions, such as how to mix dangerous chemicals.

The third is over-trust. People accept the AI's answer without checking, because it is fast, confident and usually right. When it is wrong, nobody notices. The fourth is high-stakes use without checks, in areas like health, credit, hiring or public services.

Many AI tools have safety filters, but filters are not perfect. The strongest protection is often human oversight. That means a person can question and change the AI's decision. For this to be real, and not just a signature, the person needs three things. Time, knowledge and authority. Authority means the power to change or stop the decision, without being punished for it.

Where should a person review the output? A simple rule. The higher the impact of a mistake, the closer the human review. A blog title may need only spot checks. A loan rejection needs review before it takes effect, or an easy way to appeal.

Think of an aeroplane autopilot. It is very useful, and it flies smoothly for hours. But the pilot is still trained, alert and able to take control at any moment, especially at take-off, landing and in bad weather. Human oversight of AI works the same way. The system does the routine work, and a capable person stays ready to step in when it matters most.

## Demonstrate
Let's look at an example. Youssef Benali runs customer support for a chain of pharmacies in Morocco. The company launches a website chatbot to answer questions about opening hours, stock and deliveries. In the first month, Youssef reviews a sample of chats.

He finds three safety risks. A customer asks how many pain tablets they can take in a day, and the chatbot gives a number without asking about age or other medicines. That is harmful advice.

Another customer asks if two medicines can be taken together, and the chatbot says yes, with nobody checking. That is high-stakes use without checks. And staff copy the chatbot's answers into emails without reading them, because it is usually right. That is over-trust.

Youssef decides where oversight is needed. For opening hours, stock and deliveries, the chatbot answers alone, and staff review a weekly sample. For any question about doses, side effects or combining medicines, the chatbot does not answer. It passes the question to a pharmacist on duty.

The pharmacist has time in their schedule for this, the training to answer, and the authority to contact the customer directly. And a new rule says staff must read every AI-drafted reply before sending it.

A common mistake is to believe that adding a person anywhere solves the problem. A person with no time, training or power only approves what the machine already decided. The correction is to check that the reviewer has enough time, knowledge and authority, and to place the review where a mistake would hurt most.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, key AI safety risks include harmful advice, unsafe instructions, over-trust in automated decisions, and high-stakes use without checks. Second, human oversight is real only when the reviewer has enough time, knowledge and authority. Third, the higher the impact of a possible mistake, the closer and earlier the human review should be.
