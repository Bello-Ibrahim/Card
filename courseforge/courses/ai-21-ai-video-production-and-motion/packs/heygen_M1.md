## L01 The AI Video Toolkit and Workflow

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (698 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A sixty-second video used to need a camera, a crew, actors and several days of editing. Today, one person with a laptop can make something similar in a few evenings. But that person still makes most of the important decisions.

Hi, and welcome to AI Video Production and Motion. In this first lesson, you will see which tools do which job, and which jobs stay with you.

AI video tools fall into four main kinds, and each one solves a different problem. The first is text-to-video. You write a description, and the model generates a short clip. It is useful for scenes you cannot film, like a drone shot over a forest, or a city at night. In this course, you use Google Flow, which runs Google's Veo models.

The second is image-to-video. You upload a still image, and the model adds motion to it. Because the image already fixes the look, the results are easier to control. In this course, you use Kling for this.

The third is avatar video. You type a script, choose a digital presenter and a voice, and the tool produces a person speaking to the camera. You will use HeyGen, with stock avatars and stock voices only.

The fourth is AI-assisted editing. An editor places your clips on a timeline, and AI features help with jobs like automatic captions, background removal and audio clean-up. In this course, you use CapCut.

These tools fit into a workflow with six stages. Idea, script, shot list, generation, edit, and finally export and review, where you check quality, disclosure, consent and rights.

Most beginners want to start at stage four, because generation is the exciting part. But a clip without a plan is usually a clip you cannot use. The first three stages cost almost nothing, and they save many hours of generation credits later.

Here is a way to picture it. Think of the tools as a small film crew. The text-to-video model is a camera operator who can film anywhere. The image-to-video model is an animator who brings your drawings to life.

The avatar tool is a presenter who reads your lines. The editor cuts the film together. But none of them knows the story. You are the director. You decide what each crew member does, and you check their work.

Let's see this in practice. Lindiwe runs a small bakery in Durban, South Africa. She wants a sixty-second video for her website about her new sourdough bread.

Her opening shot is a slow move across a wheat field at sunrise. She cannot film that herself, so it is a job for text-to-video.

Next, she has her own photo of the bread on a wooden board. She wants gentle steam rising from it. She already has the perfect still, so that is image-to-video.

A presenter explains the three-day fermentation process. Lindiwe does not want to appear on camera, so she uses a stock avatar, not a copy of herself or of any real person. Then captions, music and the final cut happen in the editor.

Before she opens any tool, Lindiwe writes one sentence. Our sourdough takes three days, and you can taste the difference. Every shot must support that sentence. A beautiful shot of a busy city street does not, so it stays out.

A common mistake is to think one tool can make the whole video from one long prompt. Current tools generate short clips, not finished films. A video that feels complete is many short clips, planned in advance and joined in an editor.

Let's recap. First, the four kinds of AI video tools are text-to-video, image-to-video, avatar video and AI-assisted editing. Second, the workflow has six stages: idea, script, shot list, generation, edit, and export and review. Third, the tools are your crew, but you are the director. Planning before generation saves time and credits.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will watch three short AI-assisted videos and guess which kind of tool made each part, with a clue for each guess. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, we start your own project with Writing a Short-Form Script. See you there.
```

## L02 Writing a Short-Form Script

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (695 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Most viewers decide in the first few seconds whether to keep watching. If your first line is, hello, and welcome to our company, many of them are already gone.

In the last lesson, you met the tools and the workflow. Now we start your project with stage two, the script. A good short script earns attention quickly, says one thing clearly, and tells the viewer what to do next.

A sixty to ninety second script has three parts. First, the hook, in the first three to five seconds. It is a question, a problem or a surprising picture. For example, still waiting three days for a bank transfer?

Second, one clear message in the middle. One idea, supported by two or three points. Not five features. One idea. Third, the call to action, in the last five to ten seconds. One thing the viewer should do, like download the app today.

Next, write for the ear. Viewers hear your script only once. So use short sentences, common words, and one idea per sentence. Avoid lists of numbers. One number per sentence is enough.

And write for the screen at the same time. Every line happens while the viewer is looking at something. A useful habit is a two-column script. On the left, what the viewer sees. On the right, what they hear. If a line has nothing good to show, it may not belong in the video.

How long is sixty seconds of script? Do not guess from the word count alone. A calm voice is slower, and pauses for visuals take time. Read it aloud with a timer. Your timer is the final judge.

A free chatbot can help you write a first draft quickly. But the draft is where your work starts, not where it ends. Chatbots often write too many words, use general phrases, and repeat ideas. Always edit by hand, and never paste confidential plans or personal data into a chatbot.

Think of a short script as a small suitcase. You cannot bring everything. So you choose the few things you really need, and you put the most important one where you can reach it first.

Let's see an example. Chukwuemeka is a marketer at a mobile payment app in Lagos, Nigeria. The app is made up for this lesson. He needs a sixty-second explainer for market traders who use cash today.

He asks a free chatbot for a draft. You can see his prompt on screen. It names the audience, the structure with a hook, one message and a call to action, a word limit, and a friendly, clear tone.

The draft has a hundred and ninety words and five features. Payments, savings, loans, airtime and bill payment. So he edits by hand. He keeps one message. Get paid instantly, without cash. He removes loans and savings. They can have their own videos.

He replaces a phrase like seamless financial experience with money arrives in seconds. Then he adds a visual note to each line. A trader counts coins while a customer waits. The voice asks, still counting change while customers walk away?

He reads it aloud with a timer. Seventy-one seconds. Too long. He cuts one example and a repeated sentence. Second reading, fifty-nine seconds, and a hundred and forty-one words.

The most common mistake is trying to say everything, and then speaking fast to make it fit. The result feels rushed, and nobody remembers anything. If your script is too long, do not speak faster. Cut ideas until one clear message remains.

Let's recap. First, a short script has a hook in the first seconds, one clear message, and one call to action. Second, a timed read-aloud is the real test of length. Third, write for the ear and the screen at the same time, and always edit a chatbot draft by hand.

Now it is your turn. Choose a real or fictional product that you will use for the whole course. Write a sixty-second, two-column script, read it aloud with a timer, and cut it to length. Save both versions. It takes about thirty minutes. Next, we turn your script into pictures in Storyboards and Shot Lists.
```

## L03 Storyboards and Shot Lists

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (671 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have a script. Now imagine sending it to a video model line by line. What should the first shot look like? How long should it last? If you cannot answer, the model will answer for you, and you may not like its answers.

In the last lesson, you wrote a short script. Now we turn that script into a plan the tools can follow.

A storyboard is a row of rough pictures that shows the video shot by shot. A shot list is the same plan as a table. For AI video, the shot list is more useful, because every row becomes a prompt later.

A simple shot list has seven columns. The shot number. The time where it starts. Its duration. The shot type, such as wide, medium or close-up. What the viewer sees, in one specific line. The narration at the same time. And the source, like text-to-video, image-to-video, an avatar, your own footage, or a simple graphic.

For short-form video, three to eight seconds per shot is common. Generated clips are often short, so a longer shot needs a plan, such as two clips, or a slower edit.

How do you build it? Take your two-column script. Split it where the picture should change, usually every one or two sentences. Read each piece aloud to get its duration. Then check that the durations add up to your target length.

Think about rhythm, too. Three wide shots in a row feel slow. A wide shot followed by a close-up feels like the story is moving. Put your strongest image in the hook. And quick stick-figure sketches still help you see whether shots work together before you spend credits.

A shot list is like a recipe card. The script tells you what dish you are cooking. The shot list gives each step, in order, with its time. Without a recipe, it is hard to cook the same dish twice, or to find what went wrong.

Let's look at an example. Valeria is a freelance creator in Oaxaca, Mexico. A small coffee brand asks her for a forty-five second video. Here are her first shots.

Shot one lasts four seconds. It is a wide shot of misty mountain slopes with coffee plants at sunrise. The narration says, our coffee starts in the clouds. The source is text-to-video.

Then come close-ups and medium shots. Hands picking red coffee cherries. Beans drying in the sun. Beans turning brown in a roaster. The bag on a kitchen table. Coffee pouring into a clay cup. Two friends smiling over coffee. Notice how the shot type changes almost every time, so the video never feels slow.

Notice that each viewer-sees line names a subject, an action and a setting. That makes the next lesson much easier. The durations add up to exactly forty-five seconds.

Shot eight is the logo and the website address, for eight seconds. It needs no AI at all. Text generated inside video is often unreadable, so Valeria adds logos and web addresses in the editor.

A common mistake is vague rows, like nice coffee shot, or happy people. They give you nothing to prompt with. Make every row specific. Who or what, doing what, and where. If you cannot picture the shot, the model cannot either.

Let's recap. First, a shot list turns your script into rows with time, duration, shot type, what the viewer sees, narration and source. Second, durations must add up to your target length, and most short-form shots last about three to eight seconds. Third, specific rows become good prompts later, and text such as logos is best added in the editor.

Now it is your turn. Turn your script into a shot list of eight to twelve shots, with duration, shot type and one specific line for each. Check the total, and vary your shot types. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we learn how to describe each shot to a video model, in Speaking the Language of the Camera. See you there.
```

## L04 Speaking the Language of the Camera

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (721 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask a video model for a woman in a café, and you get a random camera, random light and random movement. Ask for a slow dolly-in on a woman reading in warm window light, and the result looks planned.

The difference is the language of the camera. In the last lesson, you built a shot list. Now you learn the words that turn each row into a clear prompt.

First, a quick recap from AI Image Generation. A good image prompt names the subject, the setting, the lighting and the style, and it is specific. All of that still applies. Video adds two things: movement and time.

Shot types describe how much the viewer sees. A wide shot shows the whole scene, so we know where we are. A medium shot shows a person from about the waist up, so we see what they are doing. A close-up shows a face, hands or a small object. It shows detail and emotion.

Camera movements describe how the view changes. A pan turns the camera left or right from one spot. A tilt turns it up or down. A dolly moves the camera towards the subject, or away from it.

An orbit moves the camera in a circle around the subject. Handheld adds small, natural shaking, as if a person holds the camera. And static means the camera does not move at all.

Pacing words tell the model about speed. Slow, gentle, steady, fast, time-lapse, slow motion. Here is an important rule. Use one movement per shot. Two or three movements in a short clip often produce confused results.

Now put it together. The video prompt formula in this course has seven parts. Subject, action, setting, camera movement, lighting, style and duration. On screen, you can see an example: a young fisherman pulls a net onto a small boat, with a slow dolly-in to his hands, in soft overcast light.

Some tools set the duration in a menu, not in the prompt. Keep it in your notes anyway, so your plan and your settings match. And different models respond differently to the same words. So treat the formula as a checklist, not a guarantee.

Think of a prompt as the notes a director gives a camera operator before a take. Start wide, move in slowly, keep the light soft. A good operator follows short, clear notes. Long, mixed-up notes produce a confused take.

Let's see an example. Hana runs a small skincare brand in Busan, South Korea. Her shot list row says: close-up, hand applies cream, bathroom, morning.

She turns that short row into a full prompt. You can see it on screen. A woman's hand gently spreads white cream on her other hand, in a bright, minimal bathroom. A static close-up with a very slight push-in, soft morning light from the left, a clean commercial style, and five seconds.

This is the kind of clip a prompt like that can produce. Nothing surprising happens. The camera does exactly what Hana asked for.

Then she checks it against the formula. The subject is a woman's hand. The action is spreading cream. The setting is a bright bathroom. The camera is a static close-up with a slight push-in. Then the lighting, the style and the duration. All seven parts are there.

Her first draft was different. It said the camera orbits around her while zooming in and panning to the window. That is three movements in five seconds. They usually look unnatural, so she removed them.

A common mistake is to describe the scene carefully and forget the camera. The model then chooses a movement for you, often a slow drift that does not match the next shot. So always choose one clear movement, or static.

Let's recap. First, shot types control how much the viewer sees, and camera movements control how the view changes. Second, the formula is subject, action, setting, camera movement, lighting, style and duration. Third, use one movement per short shot, and treat the formula as a checklist.

Now it is your turn. Choose four rows from your shot list, including a close-up and a wide shot, and rewrite them as full prompts with all seven parts. Check each one has exactly one movement. It takes about twenty-five minutes. Next lesson: Keyframes, From Still Image to Motion. See you there.
```

## L05 Keyframes: From Still Image to Motion

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (699 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You generate a beautiful clip. Then the next clip has different colours, a different room and a different person. Many creators solve this before they generate any motion at all. They start with still images.

In the last lesson, you learned to write video prompts. Today, you learn a way to fix the look of your video before anything moves.

A keyframe is a still image that shows exactly how a shot should look. You create it with an image generator and check it. Only then do you add motion, with an image-to-video tool.

Why start from a still? There are three reasons. Control, because you fix the composition, colours and characters first. Cost, because images are usually quicker to try, so you can test many versions. And consistency. When all keyframes share one look, the clips made from them usually share it too.

The key to consistent keyframes is a style block. This is a fixed piece of text that you paste, unchanged, at the end of every image prompt. It describes the look, not the content. You can see an example on screen, with warm cinematic light and a terracotta and teal palette.

So every prompt has two parts. The first part changes each time. It says the subject, the action and the setting. The second part is the style block, and it never changes.

The same idea works for people. If a person appears in more than one shot, write one fixed character description, and reuse it word for word. Include an age range, hair, clothing and one or two clear details. Use fictional characters only. Do not create keyframes of real, identifiable people.

One more thing. Generate keyframes in the aspect ratio of your final video. That is usually sixteen by nine for horizontal, or nine by sixteen for vertical. If you change it later, you have to crop.

Keyframes are like the costume and set photos a film team takes before shooting. Everyone agrees on the look from the photos. So on the day of filming, nobody needs to guess what the room or the actor should look like.

Let's see an example. Youssef is a marketing assistant at a riad hotel in Marrakech, Morocco. The hotel is made up for this lesson. He needs three shots that feel like one place.

First, he writes his style block. Warm travel photography, late afternoon sun, and a palette of terracotta, cream and deep green. He will paste it into every prompt.

Then a character description for the third shot. A woman in her thirties with curly black hair tied back, a loose white linen shirt and a thin gold bracelet.

Now his three prompts. Keyframe one is a wide view of a tiled courtyard with a small fountain and orange trees. Keyframe two is a close-up of mint tea poured from a silver teapot into a small glass.

Keyframe three is a medium shot of his character reading on a rooftop terrace, with the city soft behind her. Each prompt ends with the same style block.

He generates four versions of each keyframe and chooses the best. Keyframe two has a warped teapot handle, so he regenerates it. Then he puts the three images side by side. The colours and light match. They are ready for Kling, in lesson seven.

A common mistake is changing the style words a little each time. Warm light in one prompt, sunset glow in the next. Each change moves the look. Copy and paste the exact same style block every time.

Let's recap. First, a keyframe is a still image that fixes the look of a shot before motion is added. Second, a style block pasted unchanged into every prompt, and a fixed character description, keep your images consistent. Third, use your final aspect ratio, check the images side by side, and use fictional people only.

Now it is your turn. Choose three shots you plan to animate. Write one style block, and a character description if you need one. Then generate three keyframes with any image generator, and check them side by side. It takes about thirty minutes. Next, we start generating motion in Text-to-Video in Google Flow.
```
