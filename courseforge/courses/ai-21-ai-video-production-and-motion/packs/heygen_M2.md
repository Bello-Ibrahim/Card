## L06 Text-to-Video in Google Flow (Veo)

```text
Today you generate your first real clips. But the goal is not one lucky result. The goal is a method. Change one thing at a time, and learn what each change does.

In the last lesson, you created keyframes. Now we turn to text-to-video, where you start from words alone.

Google Flow is a web tool for AI filmmaking. It uses Google's Veo video models. You write a prompt, choose settings, generate clips, and you can extend a scene. Models, features and credit costs change often, so always check the live tool.

Flow is not available in every country. If you cannot use it, try the text-to-video mode in Kling, which you meet in the next lesson. The method you learn today works in any text-to-video tool.

Before you start, sign in with a personal Google account, and check the age and country rules on the Flow page. Check how many credits you have and what one generation costs. Have your prompts from lesson four ready. And never type personal or confidential information into a prompt.

Now the method. When a clip is not right, do not rewrite the whole prompt. Change one element, like the camera, the lighting or the action, and generate again. Keep a simple log. Attempt number, what you changed, and what happened.

Think of a scientist testing a plant. If she changes the water, the light and the soil at once, and the plant grows better, she does not know why. If she changes only the light, she learns something she can use again.

Let's try it. Ingrid makes videos for an outdoor-gear shop in Tromsø, Norway. The shop is made up for this lesson. Her shot list needs a wide shot of a hiker on a snowy ridge at dawn. Button names in your version may look different.

She chooses the text-to-video mode and opens the settings. She picks the model, a sixteen by nine aspect ratio, and the number of outputs. She also notes the credit cost shown.

Then she pastes her prompt and clicks generate. You can see it on screen. A hiker in a red jacket walks along a snowy ridge, with a slow pan, soft dawn light, a realistic cinematic style and eight seconds.

She plays each clip twice. Once for the whole picture, and once for details like feet, snow and the horizon. In attempt one, the hiker's legs blur at the end. She writes that in her log.

In attempt two, she changes only the action, to walks slowly and steadily. The legs are clearer. In attempt three, she changes only the camera, to a static wide shot. It is calmer, but it feels flat. So she keeps the pan from attempt two.

Next, she uses extend on the best clip, to add a few seconds where the hiker stops and looks at the view.

Finally, she downloads the clip and checks the resolution and any watermark. She names the file with the shot number, and saves the prompt with it. Her log shows that the word slowly did more for quality than any style word.

One more rule. Never try to remove a watermark. Watermarks help viewers know that content is AI-generated. Lesson thirteen covers disclosure in more detail.

A common mistake is pressing generate again and again with the same prompt, hoping for luck. It spends credits and teaches you nothing. Others change five things at once, and then cannot repeat a good result.

Let's recap. First, Flow generates clips from text with Veo models, but features, credits and availability change, so check the live tool and have a fallback. Second, change one element per attempt, and log what happened. Third, name files by shot number, save each prompt with its clip, and never remove watermarks.

Now it is your turn. Generate two shots from your shot list in Google Flow, or in Kling if Flow is not available to you. Log at least four attempts, one change each. Then save the best clips with their prompts. It takes about thirty-five minutes. Next lesson: Image-to-Video and Motion Control in Kling.
```

## L07 Image-to-Video and Motion Control in Kling

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (668 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your keyframe already looks right. Now you only need it to move. With image-to-video, your prompt becomes much shorter, because the picture already answers most of the questions.

In the last lesson, you generated clips from text alone. Today, you bring your keyframes from lesson five to life.

In image-to-video, you upload a still image and describe only the motion. The subject, the setting, the lighting and the style are already in the image. If you describe them again, the model may try to change the image to match your words.

So a good image-to-video prompt answers two questions. What moves in the scene? For example, steam rises, or a person turns their head. And what does the camera do? A slow dolly-in, a gentle pan, or static.

Kling offers settings that help you control motion. The names change, but they usually include camera movement controls, a setting for how closely the result follows your prompt, duration and quality, and a negative prompt for things to avoid. Longer or higher-quality clips often cost more credits.

Free credits, daily limits, watermarks and export options also change often. So check what your account has before you start, and generate the shots that matter most to your video first.

Here is an important principle. Small motion is safer. Large movements, like a person walking across the frame, give the model more chances to distort faces, hands or objects. For products and places, a slow camera move is often enough.

Think of a puppeteer. The puppet is already built and painted. You do not describe its face or clothes again. You only say how it should move. Raise the left arm slowly, and turn the head towards the window.

Let's try it. Pim runs a small street-food tour company in Bangkok, Thailand. She has a keyframe of a market stall at night, with coloured lamps, a cook behind a wok and steam in the air. She wants a slow dolly-in.

She checks her credit balance first. Then she opens the image-to-video option and uploads her keyframe. She checks the preview, to make sure nothing is cropped. A cropped preview can cut off the edges of the composition she planned so carefully.

Now the prompt. It describes only motion. A slow dolly-in towards the cook, steam rising gently, lamps swaying slightly, and the cook stirring the wok. She adds a negative prompt for distorted hands, flicker and text.

She chooses the duration and mode, and notes the credit cost. She leaves the camera controls on default, because the prompt already describes the camera. Then she clicks generate.

The camera moves smoothly, but the cook's hands blur. Pim writes this down. In her second attempt, she removes the stirring and keeps only the steam and the lamps moving. The hands stay still, and the shot is clean.

She downloads the clip, checks the resolution and any watermark, and names it by shot number.

Pim's lesson is simple. Less subject movement gave a cleaner result, and the camera movement alone made the shot feel alive. For your own shots, start with a camera move, and add subject movement only when the shot really needs it.

A common mistake is to paste your full text-to-video prompt into image-to-video, with the setting, lighting and style. The model tries to follow both the image and the text, and the look shifts. Describe only what moves, and what the camera does.

Let's recap. First, in image-to-video the image fixes the look, so your prompt describes only subject motion and camera motion. Second, motion controls, duration and negative prompts help, but names and costs change, so check the live tool. Third, small, slow motion is safer, especially for hands and faces.

Now it is your turn. Animate two of your keyframes in Kling. Give one a camera movement only, and the other subject movement only, with a static camera. Log any problems and reduce motion if something distorts. It takes about thirty minutes. Next lesson: Keeping Characters and Scenes Consistent.
```

## L08 Keeping Characters and Scenes Consistent

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (684 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
In shot two, your character wears a blue scarf. In shot three, the scarf is green. In shot four, her hair is shorter. Viewers may not name the problem, but they feel that something is wrong.

In the last two lessons, you generated your first clips. Today, you learn why clips drift apart, and five ways to keep them together.

Why do clips drift? A video model does not remember your previous clip. Each generation starts again from your prompt, and sometimes an image. Words like a woman in a scarf allow many possible results, so the model chooses a slightly different one each time.

The common changes are faces, clothes, colours and light, props, and the setting. A cup becomes a glass. One shot is warm and the next is cool. A window moves to the other wall.

So how do you reduce drift? The strongest technique for beginners is to reuse keyframes. Start clips from keyframes that already match, as you learned in lesson five. Then use a fixed character description, and paste the same words every time.

Some tools also let you give a reference image of a character or object to reuse. Use only fictional characters, or images you have the rights to. And keep your style block fixed in every prompt.

The last technique happens in the edit. Cut from a wide shot to a close-up of hands, so a small face change is less visible. Keep shots of the same character short. And avoid placing two very similar shots next to each other.

Perfect consistency is not always possible with current tools. Your job is to make the changes small enough that viewers do not notice them.

Film crews have a continuity supervisor. This person photographs every actor and set before each take, so the coffee cup is still half full, and the collar is still open, in the next shot. With AI video, you are your own continuity supervisor.

Let's see an example. Tomás runs a bookshop in Córdoba, Argentina. The shop is made up for this lesson. He wants a friendly bookseller character in three shots.

He writes a fixed description. A man in his fifties with short grey hair, a neat grey beard, round black glasses, and a dark green apron over a white shirt.

He generates three clips from text only. The man takes a book from a shelf, reads at a counter, and hands a paper bag to a customer.

Then he puts them side by side and makes a continuity list. In shot two, the glasses are thinner and gold. In shot three, the apron is dark blue, not green, and the beard is longer. And the counter is wood in one shot, and white in the next.

Now his fixes. He generates one keyframe of the bookseller, and uses it as the starting image for all three shots. He adds wooden counter to every prompt. For shot three, he changes the framing to a close-up of hands and the paper bag, so the face is not needed.

The new version has only one small change. He accepts it, because a viewer watching at normal speed is very unlikely to notice it.

A common mistake is to check each clip on its own and decide it is good. Continuity problems only appear when clips are next to each other. So always review clips in order, before you decide which ones to keep.

Let's recap. First, clips drift because each generation starts again, so faces, clothes, colours, props and settings can change. Second, reused keyframes, a fixed character description, reference features and a fixed style block all reduce drift. Third, review clips in order, and use editing choices to hide small changes you cannot fix.

Now it is your turn. Generate three clips of the same fictional character, in Google Flow or Kling. Put them in order, and list every change you notice, and whether a viewer would notice it. Then choose one technique from this lesson to fix the biggest change. It takes about thirty minutes. Next lesson: Avatar Presenters in HeyGen.
```

## L10 Diagnosing Artifacts: Regenerate, Edit or Replace?

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (699 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You now have a folder full of clips. Some are excellent, some are strange, and some are almost right. The skill today is not generating more clips. It is deciding, quickly and with reasons, what to do with the clips you have.

In the last lesson, you added an avatar presenter. Now it is time to review everything you have made so far.

An artifact is a visual error caused by the generation process. There are five common types. Warped hands and faces. Melting or morphing objects. Flicker. Unreadable text. And physics that looks wrong.

Let's look closer. Hands may have extra fingers, or fingers that merge. A cup changes shape, or a bicycle wheel bends. Flicker is when light, colours or textures jump from frame to frame.

Unreadable text means signs and labels with letters that do not form words. And wrong physics means water flowing upwards, people walking without moving forward, or objects passing through each other.

How do you review? Watch each clip at full size, at least twice. Then step through the problem areas slowly, or pause at several points. Many artifacts appear only for a moment, but viewers notice them on a large screen.

Now the decision guide. For each clip, choose one of four actions. Keep it, when there are no visible problems at normal speed. Trim it, when the problem is only at the start or end, and the good part is long enough.

Regenerate it, when the problem runs through the clip and a prompt change could fix it, like less motion or a simpler action. And replace it when several attempts have failed, or the shot needs readable text. Use a graphic, a still with a slow zoom, a licensed stock clip, or your own footage.

You can also crop. Reframing a clip in the editor can remove a problem at the edge of the picture, if the quality stays good enough. And set a limit, such as three regenerations per shot. After that, replacing is usually faster and cheaper.

Think of a doctor at a clinic. Some patients need nothing. Some need a small treatment, some need a second test, and some need a specialist. The doctor does not keep repeating a test that already failed three times.

Let's see an example. Priya is producing a seventy-second explainer about rooftop solar panels for a small installer in Pune, India. The installer is made up for this lesson. She reviews six clips.

Shot one, a wide city at sunrise, is clean, so she keeps it. In shot two, a technician's hand has six fingers for about one second in the middle. She will regenerate, with the hands out of frame and the focus on the panel.

In shot three, the panels flicker in the last two seconds. The first four seconds are clean, so she trims it. In shot four, an electricity meter shows random symbols. Readable numbers need a graphic, so she replaces it with a simple animation made in the editor.

In shot five, a child's arm passes through a railing. After three failed attempts, she uses a still keyframe with a slow zoom. In shot six, a bird near the edge melts. A slight crop removes it.

Now Priya knows exactly which shots need more work, and which ones are ready for the edit.

A common mistake is to keep every clip because you spent credits on it. Another is to regenerate every problem again and again, hoping for perfection. Both waste time. A simple still or graphic is often the best choice.

Let's recap. First, common artifacts are warped hands and faces, melting objects, flicker, unreadable text and wrong physics. Second, for each clip, decide to keep, trim, regenerate or replace, and write a reason. Third, set a regeneration limit, and replace shots that need readable text or keep failing.

Now it is your turn. Put all your clips in one folder, and review each one at full size. Mark it keep, trim, regenerate or replace, with a reason. Keep your table, because you will need it for your capstone production note. It takes about twenty-five minutes. Next lesson: Editing in CapCut, Assembly, Pacing and Captions.
```
