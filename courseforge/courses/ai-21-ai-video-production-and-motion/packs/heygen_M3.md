## L11 Editing in CapCut: Assembly, Pacing and Captions

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (675 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your clips, your avatar segment and your script are ready. On their own, they are pieces. In the editor, they become a video. Today, you build your rough cut. This is step one of your capstone.

In the last lesson, you decided which clips to keep. Welcome to week three, where everything comes together into one finished video.

CapCut is a video editor with a free option and AI-assisted features, like automatic captions. It comes as a desktop app, a web version and a mobile app. But features and availability differ by version and by country.

If CapCut is not available to you, use a free editor such as DaVinci Resolve, Shotcut or Clipchamp. The steps in this lesson are the same in any timeline editor.

A rough cut is the first full version of your video. Every shot is in order, with the narration, at about the right length. It does not need music, colour work or perfect timing yet. Music and sound come in the next lesson.

Here are three rules. First, build from the voice. Put the narration on the timeline first, because the voice sets the timing. Second, trim for pace. Start each clip when something is already happening. Third, use few transitions. A simple cut is right for most shots.

A word about transitions. Use a short cross-fade only where time or place changes. Many different transition effects make a video look less professional, not more. And a shot that stays after its words have finished feels slow.

Captions matter, because many viewers watch without sound. Automatic captions save time, but they make mistakes with names, brands, numbers and accents. Always read and correct every line.

A rough cut is like the first fitting of a tailored suit. The pieces are sewn together loosely, so you can see the shape. You adjust the length and fit before you add the final buttons.

Let's build one. Kwame is making a seventy-five second explainer for a cocoa farmers' cooperative in Kumasi, Ghana. The cooperative is made up for this lesson. He has a voice-over, a HeyGen introduction and nine clips.

He imports all his files. Then he puts the avatar segment at the start, and the voice-over on the audio track after it. The voice is on the timeline first.

Now he places each clip above the voice, where its words begin, following his shot list. Shot two goes where its line starts, then shot three, and so on.

Next, he trims. He cuts the first second of shot four, where the camera had not started moving. When he plays the timeline, shot six stays on screen after its line ends. So he trims two seconds.

He adds one short cross-fade, only where the video moves from the farm scenes to the city market. Everywhere else, he uses plain cuts.

Then he generates automatic captions and reads every line. Kumasi is spelled wrongly, and cocoa has become cocoon. He corrects both. He chooses a plain, high-contrast style in the lower third of the screen.

Finally, he plays the whole rough cut and notes the length. Seventy-eight seconds, inside his target. He saves the project.

A common mistake is to place clips first, and then try to fit the voice under them. The pictures stop matching the words. Put the voice down first, then place pictures against it. And never trust automatic captions without reading them. One wrong brand name in a caption looks careless to viewers.

Let's recap. First, a rough cut is the first full version of your video, with every shot in order and the narration. Second, build from the voice, trim each clip for pace, and use very few transitions. Third, generate automatic captions, then read and correct every line.

Now it is your turn, with capstone step one. Put the voice on the timeline first, place and trim your clips, use cuts, and add corrected captions. Your rough cut should be sixty to ninety seconds long. It takes about forty minutes. Next lesson: Sound, Music and Export Settings.
```

## L12 Sound, Music and Export Settings

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (675 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Viewers often forgive a small visual problem. They rarely forgive a voice they cannot hear. Sound is half of your video, and the export is the version people actually watch. This is capstone step two.

In the last lesson, you built your rough cut. Now you make it sound right, and export it for the places people will watch it.

Most short videos have three layers of sound. The voice, from your narration or avatar, is the most important. Music sets the mood and the pace. And sound effects, like a door, waves or a coffee pour, make generated clips feel real.

The voice must always be clear. So set the voice first, loud and clear without distortion. Then bring the music in well below it, and adjust by ear. If your editor can lower music automatically under speech, use it.

Then listen twice, on phone speakers and on headphones. A mix that sounds good on headphones can sound unclear on a phone.

Now music. Only use music whose licence allows your use. Royalty-free does not always mean free for any use. Ask three questions. Can you use it commercially? On which platforms? Do you need to credit the creator?

Editors, stock libraries and AI music tools each have their own terms, and they change. Save a copy of the licence with your project. And never use a popular song just because you own a copy of it.

Next, export. Plan two versions. A horizontal version, sixteen by nine, for websites and presentations. And a vertical version, nine by sixteen, for short-form social apps. Platform requirements change, so check each platform's current guidance.

On vertical apps, buttons and text cover parts of the screen, especially the bottom and the right side. So keep captions and important details inside the central safe area.

Mixing sound is like a dinner conversation in a restaurant. The music is the background noise of the room. If it becomes too loud, nobody at the table can hear the person speaking, however interesting they are.

Let's see an example. Lucas runs a surf school in Florianópolis, Brazil. The school is made up for this lesson. His eighty-second video is edited. Now he works on sound and export.

First, he makes the voice clear and steady, and removes a short hum with noise reduction. Then he chooses a calm acoustic track. He reads the licence. For his track, commercial use is allowed and credit is optional. He saves a copy in his project folder.

He places the music under the whole video, well below the voice. He raises it slightly only in the four-second opening, where there is no narration. He adds two sound effects. Waves on the beach shot, and a board hitting the water.

Then he listens on his phone. The music hides the word beginners. So he lowers the music further, until every word is clear.

He exports a sixteen by nine version at ten eighty p. Then he duplicates the project, changes it to nine by sixteen, and reframes each clip, so the surfer stays in the centre. He moves the captions up into the safe area, and exports the vertical version.

A common mistake is loud music that sounds exciting on headphones, but hides the voice on a phone. Another is changing the aspect ratio without reframing, so subjects are cut off and captions sit under app buttons.

Let's recap. First, the voice comes first. Music and sound effects sit clearly below it, and you test the mix on phone speakers. Second, use only music whose licence allows your use, and save a copy of the licence. Third, export a horizontal and a vertical version, reframe each shot, and keep captions inside the safe area.

Now it is your turn, with capstone step two. Add music and one to three sound effects, balance the levels on a phone and on headphones, and save the music licence. Then export one horizontal and one vertical version. It takes about thirty-five minutes. Next lesson: Publishing Responsibly, Disclosure, Consent and Rights.
```

## L13 Publishing Responsibly: Disclosure, Consent and Rights

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M3_L13_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (677 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your video looks finished. Before you press publish, ask four questions. Would a viewer be misled? Did every real person agree? Do you have the right to use every piece? Does the platform allow it?

In the last lesson, you exported your video. Today, you check it before anyone else sees it. This is capstone step three. A few minutes of checking can prevent a problem that is very hard to fix after publication.

This lesson gives principles, not legal advice. Rules for AI content differ by country and by platform, and they change often. Always check the current rules that apply to you.

The first check is disclosure. As a principle, if a viewer could think an AI-generated scene or person is real, label it. A short line in the description or on screen is a good habit, even where no rule requires it. And never remove watermarks from AI tools.

The second check is consent. Every real person whose face or voice appears must agree to this use. That includes your own footage of staff or customers. An avatar or voice of a real person needs their clear, recorded consent. Stock avatars and fictional characters avoid this problem.

The third check is rights. Does your music licence allow this use? Do the terms of each AI tool allow commercial use on your plan? Free plans can have different terms from paid ones. And are the images, logos and footage you uploaded yours, or licensed?

The fourth check is misleading content. Avoid realistic scenes that could deceive. A fake customer testimonial, a product doing something it cannot do, or a real person saying words they never said. Even with a label, these can harm viewers and your brand.

Keep records, too. Save your prompts, tool names, licences and consent forms in your project folder. Think of a pilot before take-off. She has flown many times, but she checks the list every time, because a missed item is much harder to fix in the air.

Let's look at three made-up cases. Case one. Anna, a marketer in Kraków, Poland, makes a video for a dental clinic. She generates a realistic patient saying the treatment was painless. But no patient said it. It looks like a real testimonial.

Her fix is to remove it. She uses a stock avatar, clearly presented as the clinic's presenter, with an AI disclosure line. Nothing on screen now pretends to be a real patient.

Case two. Samir, a freelancer in Amman, Jordan, uses a popular song in a hotel video, because it is only twenty seconds. The problem is that he has no licence for it, and he cannot assume that a short clip is allowed. So he replaces it with a royalty-free track whose licence allows commercial use, and saves the licence.

Case three. Mei Lin, a content creator in Penang, Malaysia, films her colleague, and wants to turn the footage into an avatar. But the colleague agreed to be filmed, not to have an avatar made.

So Mei Lin uses a stock avatar. If her colleague later wants an avatar, Mei Lin will get clear, recorded consent for that specific use, and check the local rules first.

A common mistake is to think a label solves every problem. A label does not replace consent, and it does not make a misleading scene acceptable. Use all four checks together: disclosure, consent, rights and misleading content.

Let's recap. First, label AI-generated or altered content where a viewer could think it is real, and check the current rules, because they differ and change. Second, every real person's face or voice needs their clear consent. Third, check music licences and tool terms, avoid misleading realistic scenes, and keep records.

Now it is your turn, with capstone step three. Complete a pre-publish checklist for your video. List every AI-generated part, plan your disclosure, confirm consent, and check your music licence and tool terms. Save the evidence in your project folder. It takes about thirty minutes. Next, the final lesson: Capstone, Finishing and Presenting Your Video.
```

## L14 Capstone: Finishing and Presenting Your Video

- **Filename:** `ai-21-ai-video-production-and-motion_M3_L14_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (663 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have a script, a shot list, clips, an avatar, an edit, sound, two exports and a checklist. The last step is not about adding more. It is about checking carefully, fixing what matters, and explaining how you made it.

In the last lesson, you completed your pre-publish checklist. This is the final lesson, and capstone step four.

Here is the capstone brief. Produce a sixty to ninety second AI-assisted brand or explainer video, from script to final export. Add a half-page production note that lists your tools, key prompts and pre-publish checks. The rubric on the course page shows how it is assessed.

If you want to rebuild or check your plan, the lesson page has two templates. A two-column script template, and a shot-list template with a status column. The status is keep, trim, regenerate or replace, as in lesson ten.

Now the final quality pass. Watch the whole video four times, each time for one thing. First, artifacts, like hands, faces, melting objects and flicker. Second, continuity of characters, clothes, colours and settings.

Third, audio. Every word must be clear over the music, on phone speakers. Fourth, captions. Every line must be correct and inside the safe area. Fix the most visible problems first. Then do the final export of both versions.

Then write the production note. Half a page, with four parts. The tools you used and what each did. Two or three key prompts, shown exactly. The main problems and how you solved them. And your pre-publish checks.

The production note is like the notes an engineer leaves with a finished machine. Someone else, or you in six months, can see what was used, how it was built and what was checked.

Let's see an example. Lan is a content creator in Da Nang, Vietnam. Her capstone is a seventy-two second explainer for a bicycle-rental app. The app is made up for this lesson.

In her final pass, she finds four problems. A wheel bends for half a second in shot seven, so she trims it. The rider's helmet changes from white to grey between shots four and five, so she replaces shot five with a close-up of the phone screen.

The music is too loud under the call to action, so she lowers it. And the caption Da Nang is split badly over two lines, so she edits it.

Here is one of her shots. A young woman in a white helmet rides a blue bicycle along a seaside road, with a slow tracking shot beside her.

Now her production note. Her tools were Flow for four text-to-video shots, Kling for three image-to-video shots from keyframes, a HeyGen stock avatar for the intro, and CapCut for the edit, captions and export.

She shows her key prompt exactly, as you can see on screen. She explains the problems she solved, like the helmet drift, and a hands artifact she fixed by adding hands on handlebars, steady ride. And she lists her checks. A disclosure line, a stock avatar only, no real people, a saved music licence, and tool terms checked for her plan.

A common mistake is to spend the last hours generating new clips, and then run out of time for the quality pass and the note. A simpler video that is checked and well documented is stronger. Stop generating early.

Let's recap. First, the capstone is a sixty to ninety second video from script to final export, plus a half-page production note. Second, do the final quality pass in four viewings: artifacts, continuity, audio and captions. Third, the note lists your tools, key prompts, problems solved and checks.

Congratulations. You have gone from an idea to a finished video, and you directed every step. Now complete capstone step four. Do your final pass, export both versions, and write your production note. It takes about forty-five minutes. Then submit your video, the note, your shot list and your checklist. Well done, and good luck with your capstone.
```
