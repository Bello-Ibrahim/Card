# L01 The AI Video Toolkit and Workflow

Course: AI-21 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
A 60-second video used to need a camera, a crew, actors and several days of editing. Today one person with a laptop can make something similar in a few evenings. But the person still makes most of the important decisions. This lesson shows you which tools do which job, and which jobs stay with you.

## Explanation
AI video tools fall into four main kinds. Each one solves a different problem.

**1. Text-to-video.** You write a description, and the model generates a short clip, usually a few seconds long [VERSION]. It is useful for scenes you cannot film: a drone shot over a forest, a product floating in space, a city at night. In this course you use Google Flow, which runs Google's Veo models, for this [VERSION].

**2. Image-to-video.** You upload a still image, and the model adds motion to it. Because the image already fixes the look, the results are easier to control. In this course you use Kling for this [VERSION].

**3. Avatar video.** You type a script, choose a digital presenter and a voice, and the tool produces a person speaking to the camera. In this course you use HeyGen with stock avatars and stock voices only [VERSION].

**4. AI-assisted editing.** An editor places your clips on a timeline, and AI features help with jobs such as automatic captions, background removal and audio clean-up. In this course you use CapCut [VERSION].

These tools fit into a workflow with six stages:

1. **Idea:** who the video is for and the one message you want them to remember.
2. **Script:** the words the viewer hears and reads.
3. **Shot list:** what the viewer sees, shot by shot, with durations.
4. **Generation:** creating clips, keyframes and the avatar segment.
5. **Edit:** putting everything together with sound, music and captions.
6. **Export and review:** checking quality, disclosure, consent and rights, then exporting for each platform.

Most beginners want to start at stage 4, because generation is the exciting part. But a clip without a plan is usually a clip you cannot use. Stages 1 to 3 cost almost nothing, and they save many hours of generation credits later.

**Analogy:** Think of the tools as a small film crew. The text-to-video model is a camera operator who can film anywhere. The image-to-video model is an animator who brings your drawings to life. The avatar tool is a presenter who reads your lines. The editor is the person who cuts the film together. None of them knows the story. You are the director: you decide what each crew member does, and you check their work.

## Worked Example
Lindiwe runs a small bakery in Durban, South Africa. She wants a 60-second video for her website about her new sourdough bread. Here is how the tool kinds map to her plan:

- **Opening shot**, a slow move across a wheat field at sunrise: text-to-video, because she cannot film it herself.
- **Her own photo of the bread on a wooden board** with gentle steam rising: image-to-video, because she already has the perfect still image.
- **A presenter explaining the three-day fermentation process**: a stock avatar, because Lindiwe does not want to appear on camera. She does not use a clone of herself or of any real person.
- **Captions, music and the final cut**: the AI-assisted editor.

Before she opens any tool, Lindiwe writes one sentence: "Our sourdough takes three days, and you can taste the difference." Every shot must support that sentence. A shot of a busy city street, however beautiful, does not, so it stays out.

## Common Mistake
Many learners think one tool can make the whole video from one long prompt. Current tools generate short clips, not finished films [VERSION]. A video that feels complete is almost always many short clips, planned in advance and joined in an editor. If you try to describe a whole story in one prompt, you usually get one confused clip. Plan first, then generate shot by shot.

## Key Takeaways
1. The four kinds of AI video tools are text-to-video, image-to-video, avatar video and AI-assisted editing, and each solves a different problem.
2. The workflow has six stages: idea, script, shot list, generation, edit, and export and review.
3. The tools are your crew, but you are the director: planning before generation saves time and credits.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Watch 3 short AI-assisted videos from the course list, and name which kind of tool probably made each part.
**Tools:** The video list on the course page; pen and paper or any notes app.
**Steps:**
1. Open the first video from the list on the course page.
2. Watch it once without stopping, and write the one message you think it wants you to remember.
3. Watch it again. Pause at each new shot and write: what you see, and which tool kind probably made it (text-to-video, image-to-video, avatar or editing feature).
4. Add one clue for each guess, for example "the camera moves but the product never changes shape, so it probably started from a still image".
5. Repeat for the other two videos.
6. Write which stage of the workflow you think each video's creator spent the most time on, and why.
**What good looks like:** A short table for each video with at least 4 shots, a tool guess for each and a clue that supports the guess. You do not need to be certain; a reasoned guess is the goal.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
