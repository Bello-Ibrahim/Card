# L08 Keeping Characters and Scenes Consistent | Presenter Script

Course: AI-21 · Video: 5 min · Words: 686

## Hook
In shot two, your character wears a blue scarf. In shot three, the scarf is green. In shot four, her hair is shorter. Viewers may not name the problem, but they feel that something is wrong.

## Explain
In the last two lessons, you generated your first clips. Today, you learn why clips drift apart, and five ways to keep them together.

Why do clips drift? A video model does not remember your previous clip. Each generation starts again from your prompt, and sometimes an image. Words like a woman in a scarf allow many possible results, so the model chooses a slightly different one each time.

The common changes are faces, clothes, colours and light, props, and the setting. A cup becomes a glass. One shot is warm and the next is cool. A window moves to the other wall.

So how do you reduce drift? The strongest technique for beginners is to reuse keyframes. Start clips from keyframes that already match, as you learned in lesson five. Then use a fixed character description, and paste the same words every time.

Some tools also let you give a reference image of a character or object to reuse. Use only fictional characters, or images you have the rights to. And keep your style block fixed in every prompt.

The last technique happens in the edit. Cut from a wide shot to a close-up of hands, so a small face change is less visible. Keep shots of the same character short. And avoid placing two very similar shots next to each other.

Perfect consistency is not always possible with current tools. Your job is to make the changes small enough that viewers do not notice them.

Film crews have a continuity supervisor. This person photographs every actor and set before each take, so the coffee cup is still half full, and the collar is still open, in the next shot. With AI video, you are your own continuity supervisor.

## Demonstrate
Let's see an example. Tomás runs a bookshop in Córdoba, Argentina. The shop is made up for this lesson. He wants a friendly bookseller character in three shots.

He writes a fixed description. A man in his fifties with short grey hair, a neat grey beard, round black glasses, and a dark green apron over a white shirt.

He generates three clips from text only. The man takes a book from a shelf, reads at a counter, and hands a paper bag to a customer.

Then he puts them side by side and makes a continuity list. In shot two, the glasses are thinner and gold. In shot three, the apron is dark blue, not green, and the beard is longer. And the counter is wood in one shot, and white in the next.

Now his fixes. He generates one keyframe of the bookseller, and uses it as the starting image for all three shots. He adds wooden counter to every prompt. For shot three, he changes the framing to a close-up of hands and the paper bag, so the face is not needed.

The new version has only one small change. He accepts it, because a viewer watching at normal speed is very unlikely to notice it.

A common mistake is to check each clip on its own and decide it is good. Continuity problems only appear when clips are next to each other. So always review clips in order, before you decide which ones to keep.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, clips drift because each generation starts again, so faces, clothes, colours, props and settings can change. Second, reused keyframes, a fixed character description, reference features and a fixed style block all reduce drift. Third, review clips in order, and use editing choices to hide small changes you cannot fix.
