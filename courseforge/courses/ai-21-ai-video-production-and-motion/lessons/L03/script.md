# L03 Storyboards and Shot Lists | Presenter Script

Course: AI-21 · Video: 5 min · Words: 687

## Hook
You have a script. Now imagine sending it to a video model line by line. What should the first shot look like? How long should it last? If you cannot answer, the model will answer for you, and you may not like its answers.

## Explain
In the last lesson, you wrote a short script. Now we turn that script into a plan the tools can follow.

A storyboard is a row of rough pictures that shows the video shot by shot. A shot list is the same plan as a table. For AI video, the shot list is more useful, because every row becomes a prompt later.

A simple shot list has seven columns. The shot number. The time where it starts. Its duration. The shot type, such as wide, medium or close-up. What the viewer sees, in one specific line. The narration at the same time. And the source, like text-to-video, image-to-video, an avatar, your own footage, or a simple graphic.

For short-form video, three to eight seconds per shot is common. Generated clips are often short, so a longer shot needs a plan, such as two clips, or a slower edit.

How do you build it? Take your two-column script. Split it where the picture should change, usually every one or two sentences. Read each piece aloud to get its duration. Then check that the durations add up to your target length.

Think about rhythm, too. Three wide shots in a row feel slow. A wide shot followed by a close-up feels like the story is moving. Put your strongest image in the hook. And quick stick-figure sketches still help you see whether shots work together before you spend credits.

A shot list is like a recipe card. The script tells you what dish you are cooking. The shot list gives each step, in order, with its time. Without a recipe, it is hard to cook the same dish twice, or to find what went wrong.

## Demonstrate
Let's look at an example. Valeria is a freelance creator in Oaxaca, Mexico. A small coffee brand asks her for a forty-five second video. Here are her first shots.

Shot one lasts four seconds. It is a wide shot of misty mountain slopes with coffee plants at sunrise. The narration says, our coffee starts in the clouds. The source is text-to-video.

Then come close-ups and medium shots. Hands picking red coffee cherries. Beans drying in the sun. Beans turning brown in a roaster. The bag on a kitchen table. Coffee pouring into a clay cup. Two friends smiling over coffee. Notice how the shot type changes almost every time, so the video never feels slow.

Notice that each viewer-sees line names a subject, an action and a setting. That makes the next lesson much easier. The durations add up to exactly forty-five seconds.

Shot eight is the logo and the website address, for eight seconds. It needs no AI at all. Text generated inside video is often unreadable, so Valeria adds logos and web addresses in the editor.

A common mistake is vague rows, like nice coffee shot, or happy people. They give you nothing to prompt with. Make every row specific. Who or what, doing what, and where. If you cannot picture the shot, the model cannot either.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a shot list turns your script into rows with time, duration, shot type, what the viewer sees, narration and source. Second, durations must add up to your target length, and most short-form shots last about three to eight seconds. Third, specific rows become good prompts later, and text such as logos is best added in the editor.

## CTA
