# L07 Image-to-Video and Motion Control in Kling | Presenter Script

Course: AI-21 · Video: 5 min · Words: 687

## Hook
Your keyframe already looks right. Now you only need it to move. With image-to-video, your prompt becomes much shorter, because the picture already answers most of the questions.

## Explain
In the last lesson, you generated clips from text alone. Today, you bring your keyframes from lesson five to life.

In image-to-video, you upload a still image and describe only the motion. The subject, the setting, the lighting and the style are already in the image. If you describe them again, the model may try to change the image to match your words.

So a good image-to-video prompt answers two questions. What moves in the scene? For example, steam rises, or a person turns their head. And what does the camera do? A slow dolly-in, a gentle pan, or static.

Kling offers settings that help you control motion. The names change, but they usually include camera movement controls, a setting for how closely the result follows your prompt, duration and quality, and a negative prompt for things to avoid. Longer or higher-quality clips often cost more credits.

Free credits, daily limits, watermarks and export options also change often. So check what your account has before you start, and generate the shots that matter most to your video first.

Here is an important principle. Small motion is safer. Large movements, like a person walking across the frame, give the model more chances to distort faces, hands or objects. For products and places, a slow camera move is often enough.

Think of a puppeteer. The puppet is already built and painted. You do not describe its face or clothes again. You only say how it should move. Raise the left arm slowly, and turn the head towards the window.

## Demonstrate
Let's try it. Pim runs a small street-food tour company in Bangkok, Thailand. She has a keyframe of a market stall at night, with coloured lamps, a cook behind a wok and steam in the air. She wants a slow dolly-in.

She checks her credit balance first. Then she opens the image-to-video option and uploads her keyframe. She checks the preview, to make sure nothing is cropped. A cropped preview can cut off the edges of the composition she planned so carefully.

Now the prompt. It describes only motion. A slow dolly-in towards the cook, steam rising gently, lamps swaying slightly, and the cook stirring the wok. She adds a negative prompt for distorted hands, flicker and text.

She chooses the duration and mode, and notes the credit cost. She leaves the camera controls on default, because the prompt already describes the camera. Then she clicks generate.

The camera moves smoothly, but the cook's hands blur. Pim writes this down. In her second attempt, she removes the stirring and keeps only the steam and the lamps moving. The hands stay still, and the shot is clean.

She downloads the clip, checks the resolution and any watermark, and names it by shot number.

Pim's lesson is simple. Less subject movement gave a cleaner result, and the camera movement alone made the shot feel alive. For your own shots, start with a camera move, and add subject movement only when the shot really needs it.

A common mistake is to paste your full text-to-video prompt into image-to-video, with the setting, lighting and style. The model tries to follow both the image and the text, and the look shifts. Describe only what moves, and what the camera does.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, in image-to-video the image fixes the look, so your prompt describes only subject motion and camera motion. Second, motion controls, duration and negative prompts help, but names and costs change, so check the live tool. Third, small, slow motion is safer, especially for hands and faces.
