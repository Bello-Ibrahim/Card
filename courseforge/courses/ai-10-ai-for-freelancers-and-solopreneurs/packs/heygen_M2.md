## L05 Onboarding Clients Smoothly

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M2_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (683 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
The client said yes. Three weeks later, you find they expected a feature you never discussed, and they never sent the files you needed on day one. Many project problems do not start in the middle. They start in the first week.

Welcome to week two. Your proposal won the work. Now let's make sure the project starts well, with a small pack you build once and reuse with every client.

Onboarding is everything between yes and the start of the real work. A good onboarding process does three things. It welcomes the client and confirms what happens next. It collects the information and files you need, all at once. And it sets expectations, such as how you communicate, how feedback works, and what is not included.

Most freelancers do this by hand, again and again. That makes it a perfect task for AI. You create three reusable pieces once. A welcome email with the next steps and your working hours. A questionnaire of eight to ten questions. And a project checklist, from kick-off to handover, with who does what.

Why does this matter? A clear start reduces misunderstandings later. When the client knows what happens next, what you need from them, and what is included, there are fewer surprises in week three, for both of you.

AI helps in two ways. It drafts the pieces quickly. But more usefully, it can find gaps. Ask it, what questions do freelancers in my trade often forget to ask at the start? Then you decide which suggestions are relevant.

Keep the templates in one folder, with blanks such as client name and start date. And never paste a client's completed questionnaire into an AI tool if it contains personal or confidential information.

Think of onboarding as giving a guest clear directions before they visit your home. Tell them the address, where to park and which door to use, and they arrive calm and on time. If you do not, they phone you three times from the street.

Here is an example. Nadia is a freelance online maths tutor in Kraków, Poland. She works with parents of secondary school students. She used to start every new student with a long email thread about times, topics and exam dates.

She asks Claude for a reusable onboarding pack. A warm welcome email to a parent, under one hundred and fifty words, with blanks. A questionnaire of ten questions. And a checklist from the first lesson to the first progress report.

The first draft is useful. The email is warm and short, and the checklist covers every step. But she does not stop there. Then she asks what tutors often forget. The AI suggests the exam board, learning support needs, and the cancellation policy. She adds the exam question. She adds a polite question on learning support, worded so the parent shares only what they want.

But she writes the cancellation policy herself, because it is her business rule, not something AI should decide. She saves all three pieces in one folder. Starting with a new student now takes her about fifteen minutes, instead of emails over a whole week.

A common mistake is to let AI create a questionnaire of twenty-five or thirty questions, because it looks professional. Clients do not finish long forms, or they answer badly. Keep only what you need to start, about eight to ten questions. Ask the rest later.

Let's recap. First, onboarding welcomes the client, collects everything you need at once, and sets clear expectations. Second, use AI to draft a reusable welcome email, questionnaire and checklist, and to suggest questions you forgot. Third, keep your own business rules in your hands, and never paste clients' personal answers into AI tools.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, adapt Nadia's prompt to your own trade and build your onboarding pack. Cut the questionnaire to eight to ten questions, add your own rules, and save everything in one folder. You will connect that questionnaire to an automation later in the course. In the next lesson, we deliver work faster with prompt templates.
```

## L06 Delivering Work Faster with Prompt Templates

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (676 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
If you type a new prompt every time you need a report, you get a different result every time. The freelancers who save the most time do not write better prompts each day. They write good prompts once, and reuse them.

Last time, you built an onboarding pack. Today, we move to the delivery stage, and make your routine client work faster and more consistent.

A prompt template is a saved prompt with fixed instructions and blanks that you fill in for each client. A good template has five parts. A role and context. The task. The inputs, which are the blanks you fill in. The format, such as length, headings and tone. And rules, such as do not invent figures.

Start with your most common deliverables. First drafts, research notes, design briefs or monthly reports. Test each template three times with different example inputs, and improve it when the output is weak.

How much time will you save? It varies a lot by task, by trade, and by how good your inputs are. So do not rely on any general figure. Measure it on your own work, including the time you spend checking. What stays with you is the part clients pay for. The expert decisions, the judgement about what matters, and the final check.

Think of a recipe card in a busy restaurant kitchen. The chef does not invent the sauce again every evening. The recipe gives the same good base each time. But the chef still tastes it and adjusts it before the plate leaves the kitchen.

Keep your templates in one document, each with a name, the date you last changed it, and an example of good output. And never put real client secrets or personal data into the inputs. Use summaries or anonymised figures instead.

Let's look at an example. Mateus is a social media consultant in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Every month, he writes a performance report for each of his six small business clients. Each report used to take him about two hours.

His template tells the AI it is an assistant to a social media consultant. The inputs are this month's figures, last month's figures, and his own notes. The format is a three-sentence summary, a comparison table, three observations, and two recommendations based only on his notes.

The rules are the most important part. Use only the figures I give. Do not calculate percentages unless I ask. Mark anything uncertain with the word check. And no marketing jargon. He removes client names and account details, and pastes only his exported figures and his own notes.

He records his times. The first draft takes about ten minutes. Then he spends about thirty-five minutes checking every figure against his source, rewriting the recommendations in his own words, and adding one idea for each client. That is his own measurement, on his own work.

Notice this. The AI never decides what to recommend. It organises what Mateus already concluded. His expertise is in the observations and the recommendations. That is the part his clients pay for, and it stays with him.

A common mistake is to stop checking once the first few results are good. Templates reduce variation, but AI can still invent a figure or change a number. Keep the use only my inputs rule in every template, and check every number, every time.

Let's recap. First, a prompt template has five parts. Role and context, task, inputs, format and rules. Second, templates give you a consistent first draft for routine work. How much time they save depends on your task, so measure it yourself. Third, you keep the expert decisions and the final check, and you never put confidential client data into the inputs.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, choose your three most common deliverables and write a template for each, with all five parts. Test each one three times with example data. Then time one task with and without the template, including checking time. In the next lesson, we cover quality checks and client confidentiality.
```

## L07 Quality Checks and Client Confidentiality

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (674 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Imagine a client finds a sentence from their confidential plan in someone else's work. Or a wrong figure in a report you sent. As a freelancer, your reputation is your business. One careless paste can cost you a client relationship you spent years building.

In the last lesson, you built prompt templates. Today is about the checks that keep that work safe. Using AI in client work brings three risks, and each one needs a rule.

Risk one is confidentiality. When you paste text into an AI tool, it leaves your computer. Depending on the tool, your plan and your settings, the provider may store it, and in some cases may use it to improve its models. These settings change often, so check your own tool's current settings yourself.

So here is the safe rule. Do not paste client secrets, personal data or unpublished work into an AI tool, unless the client agrees and the tool's settings are suitable. Otherwise, remove names, figures and details first, or describe the task in general terms.

Risk two is accuracy. AI can invent facts, figures and sources, and it can quietly change numbers. So every AI-assisted deliverable needs a quality check. Check every fact, figure, name and date against your source. Read it as the client would. Check the tone for their audience. And make sure it is your work, shaped by your judgement.

Risk three is disclosure. Should you tell clients you use AI? There is no single answer, and some clients, contracts or countries may require it. But it is safer to decide your rule now than to explain later. A common honest approach is to say in your proposal how you use AI, and to ask before using it on sensitive material.

Think of client material as the keys to a client's office. You may use them to do your job. But you do not hand copies to a stranger, however helpful that stranger is. And before you leave, you check that every door is locked. That is your quality check.

Let's see how this works. Kofi is a freelance copywriter in Accra, Ghana. A start-up client sends him an internal document about a product that has not launched yet, and asks for website text.

Kofi wants AI help with structure. But instead of pasting the document, he writes a short, general summary. A mobile app that helps small traders track daily sales. It works offline and in local languages. He leaves out the product name, the launch date, the prices and the partner names.

He uses the summary to get a structure and some headline ideas. Then he writes the final text himself, using the real details from the document. His quality check finds one AI headline that promises zero fees. That is not in the brief, so he removes it.

And disclosure was already done. His proposal said that he sometimes uses AI for early drafts and structure, that he never shares confidential material with it, and that he writes and checks all final work. The client agreed at the start.

A common mistake is to think a paid plan, or a setting you changed once, makes it safe to paste anything. Settings and terms change, and your client may not have agreed to any outside tool. Make confidentiality your default habit, and ask the client when you are not sure.

Let's recap. First, do not paste client secrets, personal data or unpublished work into AI tools, unless the client agrees and the settings are suitable. Remove identifying details instead. Second, check every AI-assisted deliverable for facts, figures, fit and originality. Third, decide your disclosure rule in advance, and state it clearly in your proposal.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, write your personal AI-use policy on one page. What you will paste, what you will never paste, your quality checks, and your disclosure rule. Check your tool's data settings and write the date next to your notes. In the next lesson, we look at contracts, invoices and admin help.
```

## L08 Contracts, Invoices and Admin Help

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (688 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Writing to a client who has not paid is uncomfortable. Knowing what must appear on an invoice is confusing. AI can help with the blank page. But there is one line it cannot cross. It is not your lawyer, and it is not your tax adviser.

Last time, you wrote your AI-use policy. Now we finish week two with the admin that every freelancer has to do.

Admin is repeated and text-heavy, so AI can help a lot. It can draft a contract checklist, which lists the topics your agreement should cover. It can write clear invoice text. It can draft polite payment reminders, from friendly to firm. And it can turn your bullet points into short client updates.

What AI cannot do is tell you reliably what the law or tax rules require in your situation. These rules differ by country, and sometimes by region or type of business. What must appear on an invoice, whether you charge sales tax, and what late fees you may add all depend on local rules.

AI answers can sound confident but be out of date, from another country, or simply invented. So here is the rule. AI drafts are not legal or tax advice.

Use AI for structure and wording. Then check the legal and tax parts with an official source, such as your national tax authority's website, or with a qualified professional, such as an accountant or a lawyer. And never paste a signed contract, bank details or a tax number into AI. Use a blank or anonymised template.

Think of AI as a helpful friend who has read many contracts from many countries. The friend can remind you what to include and make your wording clearer. But you would not let that friend decide what your country's tax law says. For that, you ask the official office or a professional.

Let's see an example. Ingrid is a freelance illustrator in Bergen, Norway. A publishing client is fourteen days late with a payment. She wants to stay polite, keep the relationship, and still get paid.

She asks ChatGPT for three reminder emails. A friendly first reminder, a firmer second one, and a final notice, each under one hundred words, with blanks for the invoice number, amount and due date. She adds one more instruction. Do not mention any legal penalties or interest, because she does not yet know what she may charge.

She edits the first reminder so it sounds like her. Then she asks for an invoice template. The AI adds a line for a tax registration number and a tax rate. Ingrid does not know whether these apply to her, so she does not guess. Notice that she never asks AI what the rules are. She asks it for a draft, and she keeps the rules as questions.

Instead, she writes three points to check. Which details must appear on a freelancer's invoice in her country. Whether she must charge value-added tax on this service. And whether she may add interest or a fee to late payments. She checks them on the official tax authority website, and plans to ask an accountant about the tax question.

A common mistake is to ask AI what an invoice must include, or whether a clause is legal, and trust the answer. It may describe another country's rules, or rules that have changed. Use AI to prepare good questions and a clear draft. Then confirm the rules locally.

Let's recap. First, AI can quickly draft contract checklists, invoice text, payment reminders and client updates. Second, AI drafts are not legal or tax advice. The rules differ by country, so check them with an official source or a qualified professional. Third, never paste signed contracts, bank details or tax numbers into AI tools.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, adapt Ingrid's prompt to draft a payment reminder and an invoice template, with placeholders only. Then list three points you must check in your country, and find the official website for each. Save both for the capstone, which starts next week. In the next lesson, we begin mapping your client workflow.
```
