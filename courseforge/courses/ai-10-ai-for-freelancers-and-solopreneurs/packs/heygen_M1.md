## L01 Your AI-Assisted Freelance Business

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (716 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
When you work alone, you are the salesperson, the expert, the project manager and the accounts department. The client work is often not the hard part. The hard part is everything around it. What if some of that work could start from a draft?

Hi, and welcome to AI for Freelancers and Solopreneurs. In this first lesson, we look at your whole business, and find the places where AI can really help.

Every freelance business, whatever the trade, moves through the same cycle. You find clients. You win the work with a proposal and a price. You onboard the client. You deliver the work. You send the invoice. And you follow up for feedback or the next project.

AI chat tools, such as Claude or ChatGPT, can help at every stage. But mostly they help with two kinds of work. Drafting, which means first versions of messages, proposals and templates. And organising, which means turning messy notes into lists, tables and summaries. Later in the course, you will also meet automation tools that move information between apps for you.

What AI does not replace is the reason clients hire you. Your expertise, your judgement and your relationship with them. A client pays a translator for correct meaning, a designer for good taste, and a bookkeeper for accurate numbers.

Here is a simple way to picture it. Think of AI as a very fast assistant who has just joined your business. The assistant writes a first draft in seconds and never gets tired. But the assistant does not know your clients, sometimes makes things up, and must never send work without your approval. The assistant works for you, not in place of you.

So how do you find good tasks for AI? Look for tasks that are repeated, mostly text, and low risk if the first draft is not perfect. Writing the fifth welcome email of the month is a good candidate. Deciding a client's tax question is not.

And one safety rule applies for the whole course. Do not paste client secrets, personal data or confidential documents into an AI tool. Use example data, or remove names and details first.

Let's see this in practice. Leila is a freelance bookkeeper in Casablanca, Morocco. She works with eight small shops and cafés. Last week, she wrote down every task she did, and how long each one took.

Her list has replies to new enquiries, a quote for a bakery, reminders about missing receipts, monthly summary notes, invoices, and posts on a professional network. The biggest task is entering and checking figures, at about twelve hours.

For each task, she asks the three questions. Is it repeated? Is it mostly text? Is a first draft low risk? Her five best candidates are the summary notes, the receipt reminders, the enquiry replies, the invoice reminders and the quote. Together, they take about nine hours a week.

Notice what she expects. She does not expect AI to remove all nine hours. She expects to start each task from a good draft and check it, instead of starting from nothing. And her core work, checking the figures, stays with her.

A common mistake is to start with the most impressive task, like letting AI do the client work itself. That usually fails, because the core work needs the expertise the client pays for. Start with the small, repeated tasks around the work. Emails, templates, summaries and checklists. The risk is low, and you learn fast.

Let's recap. First, a freelance business has six stages, and AI can help with drafting and organising in each one. Second, the best first tasks for AI are repeated, mostly text and low risk. Your expertise and client relationships stay human. Third, AI is a fast assistant that works for you. You check everything before a client sees it, and you never paste confidential client data into it.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will list every task from your last week, or from one typical project if you are just starting. Then you will mark the five that take the most time and could get AI help. Keep your list for later in the course. In the next lesson, we will sharpen your offer and profile. See you there.
```

## L02 Sharpen Your Offer and Profile

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (702 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
I do translation. I do graphic design. I do bookkeeping. Thousands of people could say the same sentence. A potential client reads it and has no reason to choose you. Today, you will use AI to turn that sentence into a clear offer.

In the last lesson, you mapped your business. Now we start at the first stage, finding clients, with the words you use to describe yourself.

A clear offer answers three questions in one sentence. Who do you help? A specific type of client, not everyone. What problem do you solve? Something the client already worries about. And what result do you deliver? The change the client gets, not only the task you do.

Most people find this hard to write about themselves. AI helps here, not because it knows your business, but because it can ask you good questions and then organise your answers. If you just ask it to write your offer, you get general text that could describe anyone.

Think of it like a good magazine interviewer. The interviewer does not know your story. But the right questions help you say things you already knew and never put into words. The final article is still about you, and you approve it.

From your offer, you can build two short pieces. First, a profile of sixty to one hundred words for your website or your professional network. Second, a one-page service sheet. It shows your offer, who it is for, what is included, how the process works, a starting price, and how to contact you.

One warning before we start. AI often adds impressive phrases, like award-winning or ten years of experience, that are simply not true. Remove anything you cannot prove.

Let's watch this work. Joon-ho is a freelance Korean to English translator in Busan, South Korea. His old profile said only, I translate documents from Korean to English.

He opens Claude and asks it to interview him. The prompt asks for eight questions, one at a time, and to wait for each answer. Only after the last answer should it write three one-sentence offers and an eighty-word profile.

The questions ask about his best past clients, the work he enjoys and the problems clients mention. A pattern appears. His favourite clients are small export companies that need product and packaging text for English-speaking markets. They worry about sounding unprofessional abroad.

One draft says, I help small Korean export companies sell abroad with clear, natural English product text and packaging copy. Another draft promises guaranteed higher sales. He cannot promise that, so he removes it. Check every claim the AI writes.

Now the service sheet. In Canva, he searches the templates for a one-page service sheet or flyer, and chooses a simple design with space for a heading and a few short sections. He replaces the heading with his new offer.

He adds five sections. Who this is for. What you get. How it works, in three steps. A from price. And contact details. Then he changes the colours and fonts to match his own brand, and adds his own photo.

Finally, he downloads the sheet as a PDF and opens it on a phone, to check that it is easy to read on a small screen.

A common mistake is to ask AI to make your profile sound more professional, and accept the result. The text fills with general words like passionate and innovative, and loses the details that build trust. Keep it short and specific.

Let's recap. First, a clear offer says who you help, what problem you solve and what result you deliver, in one sentence. Second, ask AI to interview you first, because drafts without your real information are general and forgettable. Third, remove any claim you cannot prove, then turn your offer into a short profile and a one-page service sheet.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, paste the interview prompt into Claude or ChatGPT and answer all eight questions honestly, without real client names. Choose and edit your offer, write your profile, and build your service sheet in Canva. Then ask a friend who it is for. In the next lesson, we look at finding clients and writing outreach.
```

## L03 Finding Clients and Writing Outreach

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (700 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
With AI, you could write five hundred outreach emails before lunch. That is exactly why so many people now get messages that say nothing about them. In this lesson, you will use AI to write fewer messages that are much better.

Last time, you wrote a clear offer. Now let's use it to reach the right people. Good outreach has three steps, and AI can help with each one.

Step one. Decide which clients to contact. Start from your offer, and ask AI to list types of organisations that have the problem you solve, and the public signs that show they have it. Then you look for real examples yourself.

Step two. Research one company from public information, such as its own website or recent posts. You can paste a short About us page into AI and ask for a summary. But never ask AI to find private information about people. And remember that AI can invent facts about companies, so check anything important on their own pages.

Step three. Draft a short, personal message. A good first message is under one hundred and twenty words, and has four parts. One specific detail about them. The problem you noticed. One sentence on how you help. And a small, easy next step, like a fifteen-minute call.

Here is a way to picture it. Mass outreach is like handing the same flyer to everyone at a train station. Personal outreach is like knocking on one neighbour's door, because you noticed their gate is broken, and you repair gates. It takes longer, but people listen.

Generic mass messages damage your reputation, and they can also break the law. Rules on marketing messages and contact data differ from country to country. So check the official guidance in your country and in your client's country before you start.

As general good practice, contact a small number of relevant people. Say clearly who you are. Give an easy way to say no thanks, and stop if they ask. And do not upload lists of personal contact details into AI tools. Research companies, not private lives.

Let's see it in action. Tomás is a freelance product photographer in Valparaíso, Chile. His offer is, I help small food and drink brands sell online with clear, natural product photos.

He asks ChatGPT for types of ideal clients. The list includes small coffee roasters, craft breweries and jam makers with their own online shops. He finds three example businesses, and reads their websites himself. For one coffee roaster, he notices the shop photos are dark and taken on different backgrounds.

His prompt asks for a first email under one hundred and twenty words. It mentions their new range, offers one idea for consistent photos, and ends with a question about a short call. He asks for a friendly tone, and no exaggerated claims.

The draft is good, but one line is generic. I love your brand. Tomás replaces it with a detail only he could add. He tried their Andean blend at a market in Viña del Mar, and the bag design stood out. He also adds a last line. If this is not useful, just tell me, and I will not write again.

The common mistake is to ask AI for one perfect message, and send it to hundreds of people with only the name changed. People spot these quickly. Do the opposite. A small list, real research, one true detail, and a clear way to say no.

Let's recap. First, use AI to list types of ideal clients, summarise public company information and draft short messages, but check the facts yourself. Second, a strong message is short, includes one specific true detail, and ends with a small next step. Third, marketing rules differ by country. Check yours, contact few people, and always offer an easy way to say no.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, choose three real or example businesses and read their websites yourself. Use AI to draft a message for each, then add one true detail only you could know. Finally, write one line about the rules you need to check. In the next lesson, we write proposals and quotes that win work.
```

## L04 Proposals and Quotes That Win Work

- **Filename:** `ai-10-ai-for-freelancers-and-solopreneurs_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (694 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You had a great call. The client said, send me a proposal. Now it is ten at night, your notes are messy, and the page is blank. AI can give you a strong first draft in minutes. But only you can check what it promises.

In the last lesson, you started conversations with potential clients. Now we turn a good conversation into a proposal that wins the work.

A clear proposal has six parts. The client's problem, in their words, which shows you listened. Your approach, in three to five steps. The deliverables, with exact quantities. The timeline, including what you need from the client. The price and payment terms. And one clear next step.

AI is very good at turning messy notes into this structure. But it does not know your real prices, your free time, or what the client actually agreed. So it often fills gaps with invented details. An extra deliverable. A faster timeline. A round number for the price. And each one becomes a promise once the client reads it.

So here is the method. You provide the facts. AI provides the structure. And you check every promise. Before you send, read the proposal once only for deliverables, dates and prices. Ask, can I really deliver this, at this price, by this date?

Think of a builder's plan drawn by an assistant from your sketch. It may look professional. But if the assistant added an extra room you never discussed, you will have to build it at the agreed price. Check the plan before the client signs.

And protect your client. Before you paste notes into AI, write the client instead of their name, and leave out financial or personal details you do not need.

Let's see an example. Aisha is a freelance web designer in Dubai. She has just had a call with a small yoga studio. The old site is slow and does not work well on phones.

Her notes say the studio wants a class timetable and a link to the booking app it already uses. Five pages, in Arabic and English. Launch in six weeks. And some extra photos, maybe. She removes the studio name, and asks Claude for a two-page proposal with the six sections, placeholders for price and date, and nothing added that is not in her notes.

The draft is well organised. But when she checks it, she finds three problems. It promises a custom booking system, but the client already has one. She only adds a link. It includes professional photography, which she does not offer. And it offers unlimited revisions.

She fixes each one. A link to the existing booking app. Advice on which extra photos to take. And two rounds of changes per page. Then she adds her own price, a six-week timeline with the client's content deadlines, and the deposit term she normally uses. Every promise is now one she can keep.

Notice one more thing. Every deliverable in her final proposal comes from a line in her notes. If she cannot point to where a promise came from, it does not go in.

The common mistake is to trust a draft because it looks professional. Watch for invented deliverables, unrealistic timelines and vague phrases like ongoing support with no limit. The fix is simple. Tell the AI not to add anything outside your notes, use placeholders for prices and dates, and always do a final check that looks only at promises.

Let's recap. First, a clear proposal has six parts. Problem, approach, deliverables, timeline, price and next step. Second, you provide the facts, and AI provides the structure. Tell it not to add anything outside your notes. Third, check every deliverable, date and price before you send, because each one becomes a promise you must keep.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise, write notes from a real or example call, without names, and ask AI for a draft. Then read it twice. Once for structure, and once only for promises. Save the final version as your proposal template, because you will reuse it in the capstone. Next week begins with onboarding clients smoothly. See you there.
```
