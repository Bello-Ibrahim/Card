# L11 What to Automate and What to Keep Human

Course: AI-10 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
You now know how to automate a step and how to get AI to draft almost anything. So why not automate everything? Because a client who receives an automatic message at the worst possible moment may never work with you again. The skill now is not building automations. It is deciding.

## Explanation
For every step in your workflow that uses AI or automation, ask four questions. Together they form a **decision checklist**.

1. **How often does the task happen?** A task that happens once a year is rarely worth automating. The setup and maintenance time may be more than the time saved. A task that happens for every client, every week, is a stronger candidate.
2. **What happens if it goes wrong?** Rate the damage: low (a small delay you can fix), medium (confusion or extra work), or high (a lost client, a wrong figure in advice, a privacy problem). The higher the risk, the more human checking you need.
3. **Does the client expect a personal touch?** Confirmations and reminders can be automatic. Advice, bad news, apologies and negotiations should come from you, personally.
4. **What does it cost in money and time?** Include the price of the tool or plan, the time to set it up, the time to check and fix it, and the risk of sharing client data with another service. [VERSION] Compare this with the time you save.

For each step, the checklist gives one of three decisions:

- **Keep** the step as it is.
- **Change** it, for example from "Automation" to "AI draft that I check", or add a human check before sending.
- **Remove** it and do the task by hand.

Write a one-line reason for each decision. That reason is what makes your workflow trustworthy, both to you and to a client who asks how you work.

**Analogy:** Deciding what to automate is like deciding which household jobs to give to a washing machine. It is excellent for the weekly laundry. You would not put your best silk shirt or a child's drawing in it. The machine is not bad; some things simply need careful hands.

## Worked Example
Chidi is a freelance accountant in Enugu, Nigeria. He serves about 30 small-business clients. He applies the checklist to three steps in his workflow:

**Appointment reminders** (sent the day before each meeting). Frequency: many times a week. Risk if wrong: low; a client might miss a reminder, but the meeting is in their calendar. Personal touch: not expected. Cost: included in a free tier he already uses. [VERSION] **Decision: keep as automation.** Reason: repeated, rule-based and low-risk.

**Advice messages** (for example, what a client should do about a tax question). Frequency: weekly. Risk if wrong: high; wrong advice could cost the client money. Personal touch: clients expect Chidi's own professional judgement. **Decision: keep fully human.** He writes these himself. He may use AI only to tidy the wording of a message he has already written, with names and figures removed.

**Monthly summary notes** (a short summary of each client's figures). Frequency: monthly for every client. Risk if wrong: medium to high. Personal touch: partly. **Decision: change** from "AI draft sent automatically" to "AI draft from anonymised figures, checked and sent by Chidi". Reason: saves drafting time, but every figure needs his check.

His workflow is now faster where it is safe, and personal where it matters.

## Common Mistake
Many freelancers judge only by time saved: "It saves 20 minutes, so I will automate it." They forget the cost of setup, maintenance and mistakes, and the value of personal contact. A step that saves 20 minutes but occasionally sends a wrong or cold message to an important client is not a saving. Always ask all four questions, not only the first.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge each AI or automation step with four questions: how often, what if it goes wrong, is a personal touch expected, and what does it cost in money, time and risk.
2. Repeated, low-risk, impersonal steps suit automation; advice, bad news and high-risk work stay human.
3. For each step, decide to keep, change or remove it, and write a reason.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Capstone step 3: use the checklist to judge each AI or automation step in your workflow. Keep, change or remove each step and write a reason.
**Tools:** Your workflow map from L09 and your automation from L10; a spreadsheet (free).
**Steps:**
1. Add five columns to your workflow map: "How often", "Risk if wrong (low/medium/high)", "Personal touch expected? (yes/no)", "Cost (money, time, data risk)" and "Decision and reason".
2. Fill in the columns for every step labelled "AI draft" or "Automation".
3. For each step, decide: keep, change or remove. Write a one-line reason.
4. For every high-risk step you keep, add the human check you will do before anything reaches the client.
5. Look at your "Human only" steps. Is there any that could get safe AI drafting help? Note it, but do not change it unless the checklist supports it.
6. Update your workflow map with the new labels.
**What good looks like:** Every AI or automation step has answers to all four questions, a keep, change or remove decision, and a clear reason. High-risk steps have a named human check, and at least one step was changed or removed as a result of the checklist.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
