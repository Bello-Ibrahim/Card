# L07 Quality Checks and Client Confidentiality

Course: AI-10 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O2, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Imagine a client finds a sentence from their confidential business plan in someone else's work, or discovers a wrong figure in a report you sent. As a freelancer, your reputation is your business. One careless paste or one unchecked draft can cost you a client you spent years building a relationship with.

## Explanation
Using AI in client work brings three risks. Each needs a rule.

**1. Confidentiality.** When you paste text into an AI tool, it leaves your computer and is processed by the tool's provider. Depending on the tool, your plan and your settings, the provider may store it, and in some cases may use it to improve its models. [VERSION] These settings differ between free, paid and business plans and change often, so check the current data and privacy settings of your tool yourself. [VERSION] Your contract with a client may also forbid sharing their material with other services. [REGION]

The safe rule: **do not paste client secrets, personal data or unpublished work into an AI tool unless the client agrees and the tool's data settings are suitable.** When you need AI help, remove names, figures and identifying details first, or describe the task in general terms.

**2. Accuracy.** AI can invent facts, figures, quotes and sources, and it can make small changes to numbers without telling you. Every AI-assisted deliverable needs a **quality check** before the client sees it:

- Check every fact, figure, name and date against your source.
- Read the whole piece as the client would read it: does it answer what they asked?
- Check the tone and language for their audience.
- Check originality: it should be your work, shaped by your judgement, not general text. If a phrase seems copied from somewhere, rewrite it.

**3. Disclosure.** Should you tell clients you use AI? There is no single answer, but it is safer to decide your rule now than to explain later. Some clients or contracts require disclosure, and rules may apply in some countries or sectors. [REGION] A common honest approach is to say in your proposal or terms how you use AI (for example, "for first drafts and organising notes; all work is checked and finished by me") and to ask before using it on sensitive material.

**Analogy:** Think of client material as the keys to a client's office. You may use them to do your job, but you do not hand copies to a stranger, however helpful that stranger is. And before you leave, you check that every door is locked: that is your quality check.

## Worked Example
Kofi is a freelance copywriter in Accra, Ghana. A client, a hypothetical fintech start-up, sends him an internal document about a product that has not launched yet, and asks for website text.

Kofi wants AI help with structure. Instead of pasting the document, he writes a short, general summary: "A mobile app that helps small traders track daily sales. Audience: market traders. Key benefits: simple, works offline, local languages." He leaves out the product name, launch date, prices and partner names. He uses this summary to get a draft structure and headline ideas.

He writes the final text himself, using the details from the real document. Then he runs his quality check. He finds one AI headline idea that promises "zero fees", which is not in the brief. He removes it.

In his proposal, Kofi had already written: "I sometimes use AI tools for early drafts and structure. I never share your confidential material with them, and all final work is written and checked by me." The client agreed to this at the start.

## Common Mistake
Many freelancers believe that a paid plan, or a setting they changed once, makes it safe to paste anything. Settings, terms and plans change, and your client may not have agreed to any external tool at all. The fix is to treat confidentiality as your default: remove identifying details by habit, check your tool's current settings regularly, and ask the client when you are not sure.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do not paste client secrets, personal data or unpublished work into AI tools unless the client agrees and the tool's current data settings are suitable; remove identifying details instead.
2. Check every AI-assisted deliverable for facts, figures, fit and originality before the client sees it.
3. Decide your disclosure rule in advance and state it clearly in your proposal or terms.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Write your personal AI-use policy for client work: what you will and will not paste into AI tools, how you check work, and what you tell clients. Keep it to one page.
**Tools:** A word processor (free); the current privacy or data settings page of the AI tool you use. [VERSION] Optional: Claude or ChatGPT to suggest a structure.
**Steps:**
1. Open the settings and privacy information of your AI tool. Write down, in your own words, what it says about storing your conversations and using them for training on your plan. [VERSION]
2. Write a section "I will paste": for example public information, my own notes, anonymised summaries.
3. Write a section "I will never paste": for example client names with confidential details, personal data, passwords, unpublished work without permission.
4. Write your quality checklist: 4 to 6 checks you do on every AI-assisted deliverable.
5. Write your disclosure rule in one or two sentences you could put in a proposal.
6. Add a line about what you will do if you are unsure: "I ask the client first."
**What good looks like:** A one-page policy with four clear sections (paste, never paste, quality checks, disclosure), written in plain language you could show a client. The data-settings notes are dated, because settings change.
**Time:** about 30 minutes

## Review Flags
- [VERSION] Data storage, retention and model-training settings for Claude and ChatGPT differ by plan (free, paid, business) and change often; they must be checked against current terms before scripting.
- [VERSION] The location and names of privacy and data controls in each tool must be checked against the live interface.
- [REGION] Whether client contracts, sector rules or national laws require freelancers to disclose AI use, or restrict sharing client material with third-party services, differs by country and sector.
