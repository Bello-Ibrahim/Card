# L03 Finding Clients and Writing Outreach

Course: AI-10 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O3, O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
With AI, you could write 500 outreach emails before lunch. That is exactly why so many people now receive messages that start "I hope this finds you well" and say nothing about them. In this lesson, you will use AI to write fewer messages that are much better.

## Explanation
Good outreach has three steps, and AI can help with each.

**1. Decide which clients to contact.** Start from the offer you wrote in L02. Ask AI to list types of organisations that have the problem you solve, and the signs that show they have it. For example: "Which types of small businesses need product photos, and what public signs show they need better ones?" You then look for real examples yourself.

**2. Research one company from public information.** Read the company's own website, recent posts or public news. You can paste short public text, such as an "About us" page, into an AI tool and ask it to summarise what the company does and what challenges it might have. Do not ask the AI to find private information about people, and remember it can invent facts about companies. Check anything important on the company's own pages.

**3. Draft a short, personal message.** A good first message is under 120 words and has four parts: one specific detail about them, the problem you noticed, one sentence on how you help, and a small, easy next step, such as "Would a 15-minute call next week be useful?"

**Analogy:** Mass outreach is like handing out the same flyer to everyone who walks past a train station. Personal outreach is like knocking on one neighbour's door because you noticed their garden gate is broken and you repair gates. The second takes longer per person, but people actually listen.

**Rules and reputation.** Generic, mass-produced messages damage your reputation, and they can also break the law. Countries have different rules about sending unsolicited marketing emails and messages, about which contact details you may collect and store, and about how people must be able to opt out. [REGION] Some rules treat business contacts differently from private individuals. [REGION] Before you start, check the official guidance on marketing messages and personal data in your country and in your client's country. [REGION] As general good practice: contact a small number of relevant people, say clearly who you are, give an easy way to say "no thanks", and stop if they ask you to.

Do not upload lists of personal contact details into AI tools. Research companies, not private lives.

## Worked Example
Tomás is a freelance product photographer in Valparaíso, Chile. His offer: "I help small food and drink brands sell online with clear, natural product photos."

He asks ChatGPT for types of ideal clients. The list includes small coffee roasters, craft breweries and jam makers selling through their own online shops. He finds three example businesses and reads their websites himself.

For one hypothetical coffee roaster, he pastes the public "About us" text and asks for a summary. He notices their shop photos are dark and taken on different backgrounds. He asks the AI:

> "Write a first outreach email under 120 words to the owner of this coffee roaster. Mention that their new single-origin range launched this month. Offer one idea to make product photos consistent. End with a question about a short call. Friendly, not pushy. No exaggerated claims."

The draft is good but generic in one line: "I love your brand." Tomás replaces it with a detail only he could add: "I tried your Andean blend at a market in Viña del Mar last month, and the bag design stood out." He also adds a line at the end: "If this is not useful, just tell me and I will not write again."

## Common Mistake
Many learners ask AI to write one "perfect" message and then send it to hundreds of contacts with only the name changed. People recognise these messages quickly. Worse, sending many unsolicited messages can break local marketing rules. The fix is the opposite approach: a small list, real research, one true personal detail per message, and a clear way to say no.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use AI to list types of ideal clients, summarise public company information and draft short messages, but check the facts yourself.
2. A strong outreach message is short, includes one specific true detail, and ends with a small, easy next step.
3. Rules on unsolicited marketing and contact data differ by country, so check your local rules, contact few people, and always offer an easy way to say no.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Choose 3 real or example potential clients. Use AI to draft a personalised outreach message for each, then edit each message so it includes one specific detail that only you could add.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); the clients' public websites; a notes app.
**Steps:**
1. Using your L02 offer, ask AI for 5 types of ideal clients and the public signs that show they need you.
2. Choose 3 real businesses or invent 3 realistic example businesses. Read their public websites yourself.
3. For each, paste only short public text into the AI tool and ask for a draft message under 120 words, using the four parts from the Explanation.
4. Edit each draft. Replace general praise with one specific, true detail that only you could add.
5. Check each message: is it under 120 words, is every fact correct, does it offer an easy way to say no?
6. Write one line about the marketing-message rules you need to check in your country before sending.
**What good looks like:** Three different messages, each under 120 words, each with a true personal detail, a small next step and an opt-out line. No private contact data was pasted into the AI tool.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
