# L12 Your Complete Proposal-to-Invoice Workflow

Course: AI-10 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O6, O7 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Over three weeks you have built a proposal template, an onboarding pack, prompt templates, an AI-use policy, an invoice template and an automation. Separately, they are useful tools. Together, in one documented workflow, they become a system that lets you take on more work without working longer hours.

## Explanation
Your capstone brings everything into one **workflow document**. It has seven parts, and each part links to something you have already made:

1. **Workflow map** (L09, updated in L11): every step from proposal to invoice, with labels and reasons.
2. **Proposal template** (L04): with placeholders and your checking rule for promises.
3. **Onboarding pack** (L05): welcome email, questionnaire and checklist.
4. **Delivery prompt templates** (L06): your three tested templates.
5. **Quality checks and AI-use policy** (L07): what you paste, what you never paste, how you check, what you tell clients.
6. **Automation** (L10): what it does, screenshots, test results and your weekly check.
7. **Invoice template and payment reminder** (L08): with your list of points checked with an official source. [REGION]

Then you **run one test client** through the whole workflow, from proposal to invoice. Use a real client if you have one and they agree, or a realistic example client. For every step, record what you did, how long it took and what went wrong. This test is the most important part: a workflow only proves itself when it is used.

Finally, you **evaluate**: which steps worked well, which need changing, and what you would do differently. This is the same keep, change or remove thinking from L11, now based on real evidence.

Three final reminders. Keep confidential client information out of AI tools throughout the test. AI drafts of contracts, invoices and tax material are not legal or tax advice. [REGION] And check your tool plans and settings before relying on them, because they change. [VERSION]

**Analogy:** Documenting your workflow is like writing down a family recipe that you have cooked from memory for years. Once it is on paper, you can repeat it every time, notice where it can be better, and even hand it to a helper in the future.

## Worked Example
Yuki is a freelance UX writer in Osaka, Japan, who writes app and website text for small software companies. She builds her workflow document in one shared folder, with one page that links to each part.

She runs an example client, "a small language-learning app", through it:

- **Proposal**: her template and an AI draft from example call notes took 25 minutes. Her promise check removed "copy for all app screens" and replaced it with "up to 20 screens".
- **Onboarding**: the enquiry form triggered her automation, which added a spreadsheet row and sent her welcome email. She then sent the questionnaire.
- **Delivery**: her "screen copy" prompt template produced first drafts from anonymised notes. She rewrote the key messages herself and ran her quality checklist.
- **Invoice**: she filled her invoice template and noted which tax points she had confirmed on her national tax authority's website. [REGION]

Her evaluation found one problem: the automation sent the welcome email before she had read the enquiry, so a poor-fit enquiry received a warm welcome. She changed the action to "send a short thank-you; I will reply personally within one working day", and kept the full welcome email for after the discovery call.

## Common Mistake
Many learners submit a folder of separate templates and call it a workflow. Without the map linking the parts, and without a real test run, there is no evidence that the parts work together. The test client is where you find the gaps, such as Yuki's early welcome email. Run the full test, record the problems honestly, and show what you changed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Your workflow document links seven parts: map, proposal, onboarding, delivery prompts, quality checks and policy, automation, and invoice.
2. Running one test client from proposal to invoice is the proof that the workflow works, and it shows you what to fix.
3. Evaluate each step with evidence from the test, and keep confidential data out of AI tools and legal or tax decisions in qualified hands.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Capstone step 4: document your full AI-assisted workflow with templates, prompts, the automation and your AI-use policy, then run one test client (real or example) through it from proposal to invoice.
**Tools:** Your work from L04 to L11; a shared folder or document (free, such as Google Drive); Claude or ChatGPT; Zapier or n8n. [VERSION]
**Steps:**
1. Create one folder and one overview page that links to all seven parts.
2. Update your workflow map so every step names the template, prompt or automation it uses.
3. Choose a test client. If it is a real client, ask their permission and follow your AI-use policy. Otherwise use a realistic example.
4. Run the test client through every step from proposal to invoice. Record the time, tool and any problem for each step.
5. Write a one-page evaluation: what worked, what failed, and what you changed (keep, change or remove, with reasons).
6. Check your work against the capstone rubric and the submission checklist before you submit.
**What good looks like:** One organised folder with all seven parts, an updated workflow map, a complete test-run record from proposal to invoice, and an honest evaluation that shows at least one improvement.
**Time:** about 60 minutes

## Review Flags
- [REGION] Invoice, contract and tax requirements differ by country; the lesson repeats that AI drafts are not legal or tax advice and that learners must confirm these points with an official source or qualified professional.
- [VERSION] AI tool and automation tool plans, free-tier limits and settings change often and should be confirmed before scripting.
