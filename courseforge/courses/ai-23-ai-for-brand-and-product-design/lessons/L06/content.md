# L06 Naming and Verbal Identity

Course: AI-23 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O3, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Claude can give you 100 brand names in ten seconds. The hard part is the next step: finding the three that mean the right thing, sound right in every market and that your client can actually use.

## Explanation
Naming is one of the best tasks for AI in brand design, because it needs a lot of variation. It is also one of the riskiest, because a name must be meaningful, pronounceable and legally available.

**Step 1: generate from the brief.** Give Claude the key parts of your brief from L04: audience, markets, languages, personality and what the name must avoid. Ask for names in different categories, because this gives real variety:

- **Descriptive:** says what the business does.
- **Evocative:** suggests a feeling or image.
- **Invented:** a new word built from roots or sounds.
- **Compound:** two real words joined together.

Ask for a one-line reason for each name, so you can see the thinking and reject weak logic quickly. Never ask for names "like" an existing brand; describe qualities instead.

**Step 2: tone of voice.** A verbal identity is more than a name. Ask Claude for 3 short tone-of-voice directions (for example, "calm and expert", "playful and local", "direct and practical"), each with 3 sample lines such as a welcome message and an error message. Then choose one direction and edit the lines yourself.

**Step 3: filter.** Shortlist by checking each name for:

- **Meaning in other languages:** does the word, or a similar sound, mean something negative or rude in your markets? Ask Claude as a first screen, then confirm with native speakers or reliable dictionaries, because the model can miss slang and local meanings.
- **Pronunciation:** can people in each market say it after reading it once?
- **Availability:** are the main domain names and social handles free?

**What AI cannot do.** AI cannot confirm that a name is free to use. It does not have reliable access to every trademark register, and it cannot give a legal opinion. Trademark rules and search processes differ by country. [REGION] Before a name goes to launch, the client needs official trademark searches in each market and, usually, advice from a trademark professional. [VERIFY] Your job as a designer is to deliver a shortlist that is clearly marked "not yet cleared".

**Analogy:** AI naming is like a brainstorm with a very fast colleague who has read a lot of dictionaries but never visited a trademark office. Their ideas start the conversation. The legal check happens somewhere else.

## Worked Example
Thandi Mokoena is a brand designer in Johannesburg, South Africa. Her hypothetical client runs refill stations where customers bring their own containers for soap, oil and grains. The brand will launch in South Africa and later in neighbouring countries.

She asks Claude for 20 names in four categories, with a reason for each, and adds: "The name must be easy to say in English, isiZulu and Afrikaans. Avoid words that suggest luxury."

She shortlists three: "Refilla" (invented), "Full Circle Pantry" (compound) and "Gcwalisa" (an evocative name that Claude suggests means "fill up" in isiZulu).

Then she filters. For "Gcwalisa", she asks two isiZulu-speaking colleagues. They confirm the meaning, but say the "gc" click sound may be hard for customers in other markets. She keeps it as a local option. For "Refilla", she searches for existing businesses and domains and finds a similar name in a related field, so she marks it "high risk: similar name found". "Full Circle Pantry" has free domains in her basic check.

Her shortlist slide says: "These names have passed meaning and basic domain checks. None has been cleared as a trademark. The client should order official searches in each launch market before choosing." [VERIFY]

## Common Mistake
The most common mistake is to ask Claude, "Is this name trademarked?" and trust the answer. The model may say "I found no conflicts", but it has not searched the official registers in your markets. A second mistake is testing a name only in English. Always check meaning and sound in every language of your markets with real speakers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Generate names from the brief in several categories, with a reason for each, and develop tone of voice with sample lines you then edit.
2. Filter names by meaning in other languages, pronunciation and basic domain availability, confirming language checks with native speakers.
3. AI cannot clear a name; trademark searches and legal advice are still needed, and rules differ by country.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Generate 20 names for your capstone brand, shortlist 3, and check each for meaning in 2 other languages and basic domain availability.
**Tools:** Claude (free plan); a domain registrar's free search; online dictionaries or native speakers you know.
**Steps:**
1. Give Claude your brief summary: audience, markets, personality and words to avoid. Do not include confidential client details.
2. Ask for 20 names in four categories, each with a one-line reason.
3. Shortlist 3 names, with a sentence on why each fits the brief.
4. For each name, check its meaning and sound in 2 other languages relevant to your markets. Use Claude as a first screen, then confirm with a dictionary or a native speaker.
5. Check basic domain availability for each name.
6. Mark every name "not yet cleared as a trademark" and note any similar names you found.
**What good looks like:** A table of 3 names with category, reason, language checks with sources, domain results and a clear risk note. No name is described as legally safe.
**Time:** about 30 minutes

## Review Flags
- [REGION] Trademark rules, registers and search processes differ by country; the lesson gives no legal conclusion and tells learners that official searches are needed in each market.
- [VERIFY] Confirm the general statement that official trademark searches and professional advice are normally needed before launch, and that AI tools cannot reliably search trademark registers. The isiZulu meaning in the worked example is hypothetical and should be confirmed by a native speaker before recording.
