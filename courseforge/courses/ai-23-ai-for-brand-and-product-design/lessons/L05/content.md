# L05 Protecting Client Data and Confidentiality

Course: AI-23 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
You paste an unreleased product name into an AI tool to get tagline ideas. Two weeks later, the client asks: "Which tools have seen our launch plans?" Can you answer?

## Explanation
Designers see sensitive material every day: research recordings, customer lists, unreleased products, pricing and strategy. When you use AI tools, you share some of this material with a third-party service. That is not always wrong, but it must be a deliberate, permitted choice.

**What not to paste without permission:**

- **Personal data:** names, contact details, photos, voice recordings, health or financial details, and anything that identifies a research participant.
- **Unreleased work:** product names, launch dates, prototypes and campaign ideas.
- **Client secrets:** strategy, prices, contracts, sales figures and internal documents.

**Check each tool's settings.** AI tools differ in how they store your prompts and files, how long they keep them, and whether they may use them to improve their models. These settings can depend on your plan (free, paid, team or enterprise) and can change over time. [VERSION] Before you use a tool for client work, read its current privacy and data-use information and check your own account settings, such as any option about model training. [VERIFY] Do this for every tool, including Claude, Figma's AI features, plugins and image generators. Plugins are easy to forget: a plugin may send your content to its own service with its own terms. [VERSION]

**Anonymise research.** As in L02, replace names and identifying details with IDs and general terms before any AI step. Keep the key that links IDs to people outside AI tools, in the storage your client or organisation approves.

**Respect local rules.** Data-protection law differs by country and region. Some laws have strict rules about consent, sensitive categories of data, and sending data to other countries. [REGION] This course teaches principles, not legal advice. If you work with personal data, ask your client or a data-protection specialist which rules apply. [VERIFY]

**Agree AI use in writing.** The simplest protection is a clear statement in your proposal or contract: which tools you use, for what, what data you will not share, and how the client can say no.

**Analogy:** An AI tool is like a print shop outside your studio. You would not send a client's secret launch poster to any print shop without checking who can see it, how long they keep the files and whether the client agreed. AI tools need the same checks.

## Worked Example
Katarzyna Nowak runs a small design studio in Kraków, Poland. A hypothetical client, a physiotherapy clinic network, asks her to redesign its patient app. The work includes patient interviews.

Before starting, she lists the tools she plans to use: Claude for research synthesis and microcopy, Figma with its AI features, and a free image generator for moodboards. For each tool, she reads the current data-use information and checks her account settings. [VERSION] She notes that health data is especially sensitive, and asks the clinic's data-protection contact which rules apply to the interviews. [REGION]

She decides:

- Interview recordings and names never go into AI tools. She anonymises the notes before synthesis.
- The new service name stays out of AI prompts until the client approves it; she uses "[SERVICE]" as a placeholder.
- Moodboard prompts describe mood and materials, not the client's plans.

She adds this statement to her proposal:

> "We use AI tools (Claude, Figma AI features and an image generator) to speed up research synthesis, early concepts and draft copy. We never enter patient names, recordings, health details or unreleased product names into these tools. Research notes are anonymised first. All final designs are reviewed and finished by our designers. You can ask us not to use AI tools for any part of the project."

The client asks one question and signs.

## Common Mistake
Many designers assume a paid plan automatically protects client data, or that a free plan always uses their data for training. Neither assumption is safe; the settings depend on the tool, plan and account, and they change. [VERIFY] The correct approach is to check the current terms for each tool and to share less data by default. A second mistake is agreeing on AI use only in a conversation. Put it in writing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do not paste personal data, unreleased work or client secrets into AI tools without permission; anonymise research first.
2. Check each tool's current data-use and training settings for your plan, including plugins, because they differ and change.
3. Data-protection rules differ by country, so agree AI use with clients in writing and ask a specialist when personal data is involved.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Write a short AI-use statement you could add to a client proposal, covering which tools you use and how you protect their data.
**Tools:** A document editor; the current privacy pages of the tools you use (Claude, Figma, your image generator).
**Steps:**
1. List every AI tool and plugin you expect to use on your capstone or a typical project.
2. For each one, find its current data-use and training information and note the relevant setting on your plan. [VERSION]
3. Write what each tool is used for, in one line each.
4. Write what data you will never enter, and how you anonymise research.
5. Add a sentence that gives the client a clear way to refuse AI use.
6. Keep the statement to 80 to 150 words in plain language.
**What good looks like:** A short, specific statement that names the tools, their purpose, the data you exclude and the client's right to refuse, supported by your own notes on each tool's settings.
**Time:** about 25 minutes

## Review Flags
- [VERSION] Data-use, retention and model-training settings for Claude, Figma AI features, Figma plugins and free image generators depend on plan and change often; check the live terms and settings before scripting.
- [VERIFY] Confirm that the lesson's general statements about account settings (such as training options) match each tool's current privacy information, and that free and paid plans are not described incorrectly.
