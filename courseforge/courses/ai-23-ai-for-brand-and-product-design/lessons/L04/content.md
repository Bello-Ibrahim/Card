# L04 Writing a Design Brief with AI as a Sparring Partner

Course: AI-23 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Most projects that go wrong in week six were already going wrong in week one, in the brief. What if you could find the gaps in your brief before the client does?

## Explanation
A design brief is the agreement that guides every later decision: what problem you are solving, for whom, within which limits and how you will know it worked. A good one-page brief usually covers:

- **Background:** the organisation and why the project exists now.
- **Problem and goals:** what must change, in one or two sentences.
- **Audience:** who the work is for, based on research (L02 and L03).
- **Scope and deliverables:** what is included and what is not.
- **Constraints:** budget, timeline, existing assets, technical limits, languages and markets.
- **Success measures:** how the client and you will judge the result.

You can use Claude in two ways with a brief. The first is to ask it to **write** the brief. This is fast, but the result is generic, because Claude does not know the client, the politics or the budget. The second, more useful way is to ask it to **question** your draft, as a sparring partner. In this role, Claude looks for:

- **Missing constraints:** "Which markets and languages must the brand work in?"
- **Unclear success measures:** "What does 'more modern' mean, and how will you measure it?"
- **Conflicting goals:** "The brief asks for a premium look and the lowest possible packaging cost. Which has priority?"
- **Hidden assumptions:** "You assume customers know the family story. Is there evidence?"

A useful prompt sets the role and the limits: "You are a senior creative director reviewing this brief. Ask me the 5 most important critical questions. Do not rewrite the brief and do not suggest design solutions."

The last rule matters. You want questions, not answers. **The designer keeps the decisions.** You answer each question yourself, with the client when needed, and revise the brief. Some questions will not be relevant; you can reject them with a short reason.

Remember the data rules from L02: remove confidential numbers and client secrets unless you have permission, or replace them with ranges ("a small budget", "launch in the second quarter").

**Analogy:** Claude as a sparring partner is like a training partner in boxing. The partner does not fight your match for you. They test your defence so you find the weak points before the real fight. You decide what to change.

## Worked Example
Yasmine Trabelsi is a brand designer in Tunis, Tunisia. A hypothetical family-owned olive oil company wants a rebrand. Her draft brief says: "Modernise the brand for younger buyers and export markets, keep the family heritage, launch new labels in six months."

She pastes the brief into Claude, with the owner's name and revenue figures removed, and asks for 5 critical questions. Claude replies:

1. Which export markets, and does the label need to work in Arabic, French and English at the same time?
2. What does "younger buyers" mean in age range and buying channel (supermarket, online, speciality shops)?
3. How will you measure success after six months: sales, shelf recognition or distributor feedback?
4. "Modernise" and "keep heritage" can conflict. Which heritage elements are fixed, and which can change?
5. Are there legal label requirements in the export markets that limit the design?

Yasmine answers each one. After a call with the client, she adds: target markets (two European countries and the Gulf region), trilingual labels, "the family name and the olive tree must stay", and a success measure based on distributor listings. For question 5 she writes: "Label rules differ by country; the client's export agent will confirm them." She does not ask Claude to answer that question, because it needs an expert source. Her brief is now clearer, and every decision is hers and the client's.

## Common Mistake
Many designers ask Claude to "improve" the brief and accept a longer, more polished text. Polished is not the same as correct. The new text can add goals the client never agreed to. Ask for questions, not rewrites, and make every change yourself. Another mistake is to treat all AI questions as equally important; choose the ones that change decisions.

## Key Takeaways
1. A strong brief covers background, goals, audience, scope, constraints and success measures on one page.
2. Use Claude to question your draft, looking for missing constraints, unclear measures and conflicting goals, not to write the brief for you.
3. You and the client answer the questions and make every decision; expert questions, such as legal requirements, go to expert sources.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Draft a one-page brief for your capstone brand, ask Claude for 5 critical questions, and revise the brief to answer them.
**Tools:** Claude (free plan); a document, or a frame in Figma (free plan).
**Steps:**
1. Choose your capstone brand. It can be a real side project or a fictional business; do not use a real client's confidential details.
2. Write a one-page brief with the six parts from this lesson.
3. Paste it into Claude with a prompt that sets the reviewer role and asks for 5 critical questions only, with no rewrites or solutions.
4. For each question, write your answer, or write "not relevant" with a reason.
5. Revise the brief. Highlight every change.
6. Save both versions; you will use the brief in every later lesson.
**What good looks like:** A revised brief with at least 3 specific changes (for example, a measurable success measure or a clear priority between two goals), and a short note for each question showing your decision.
**Time:** about 30 minutes

## Review Flags
- None. The example is hypothetical, and questions about legal label rules are sent to an expert source rather than answered in the lesson.
