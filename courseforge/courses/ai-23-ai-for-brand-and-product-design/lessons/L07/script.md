# L07 Moodboards and Visual Direction with Image Generators | Presenter Script

Course: AI-23 · Video: 5 min · Words: 744

## Hook
A client says: we want it to feel warm but modern. What does that look like? In this lesson, you will see three different answers on screen, in less time than it takes to search a stock library.

## Explain
Last time, we worked with words. Now we move to images. This lesson is about moodboards and visual direction with image generators.

A moodboard sets the visual direction before any logo or screen exists. Image generators make moodboards faster and more specific, because you can create images for the exact mood in your brief.

The key is to prompt with visual qualities. Describe materials and textures, like raw clay or recycled paper. Colour, like muted terracotta and sage green. Light, like soft morning window light. Era and context. And composition, such as close-up, flat lay, or a lot of empty space.

Never prompt with the name of a living artist, a photographer or an existing brand. It is unfair to the people whose work is copied. It can create rights problems. And it makes your direction look borrowed. Qualities give you better control anyway.

Next, create contrast, not variations. One direction with eighteen images tells the client nothing. Three clearly different directions for the same brief start a real conversation. Change one or two big variables between directions, such as the light and the era, and keep the brief the same.

And know the tool's limits. Free image generators differ in daily limits, quality, whether images are public, and what their terms allow for commercial use. These things change often. So read the current terms before you show images to a client, and label every moodboard as AI-generated reference, not final artwork.

Think of a moodboard prompt as a recipe written by ingredient, not by restaurant. Tastes like that famous restaurant gives the cook nothing to work with, and copies someone else. Smoked paprika, charred peppers and lime describe the result you want. And the dish becomes yours.

## Demonstrate
Let's build one. Diego Hernández is a brand designer in Oaxaca, Mexico. His client is a small ceramics studio that sells tableware online. The brief says: handmade, calm, contemporary, for young professionals setting up their first home.

Diego opens Google Gemini in his browser and signs in. First, he checks the settings he needs, and chooses a square format for the moodboard tiles.

He types the prompt for direction A. Handmade ceramic bowls on a pale oak table, soft morning window light, muted terracotta and cream, a lot of empty space, a calm contemporary kitchen, close-up photograph. He generates a set, keeps the two strongest images, and notes why.

Then he varies the subject, but keeps the direction. Hands shaping raw clay, same light and palette. He keeps two more.

Direction B changes the light and the mood. Glazed plates in deep cobalt and white, hard midday shadows, a bright outdoor courtyard, bold geometric composition. Direction C goes the other way. Matte charcoal ceramics on dark slate, a single warm lamp at night, quiet and minimal, wide shot.

He generates six images for each direction and downloads them. In Figma, he creates three frames. A, Morning Studio. B, Courtyard Colour. C, Evening Quiet. He places six images in each frame.

Under each frame, he writes one sentence on the idea, and adds the label AI-generated reference. The client sees three clearly different answers to warm but modern, and chooses direction A, with the cobalt accent from B.

A common mistake is to generate dozens of images from one prompt and call it a moodboard. That shows one idea many times. Another is to prompt in the style of a well-known artist to get a quick result. Describe materials, colour, light and era instead.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, prompt with visual qualities, such as materials, colour, light, era and composition, never with the names of living artists or existing brands. Second, create three contrasting directions for the same brief, by changing one or two big variables. Third, free image generators change their limits and terms often, so check them, and label moodboard images as AI-generated references.
