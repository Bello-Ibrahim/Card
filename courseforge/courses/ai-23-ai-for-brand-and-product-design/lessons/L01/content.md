# L01 Where AI Fits in Brand and Product Design

Course: AI-23 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
An AI tool can give you forty logo ideas before your coffee is cold. It can also give you forty logo ideas that look like marks you have seen before, in colours that fail a contrast check. The skill is not using AI everywhere. The skill is knowing where it helps and where it hurts.

## Explanation
You already know the design process. Most brand and product projects move through similar stages: research, definition (insights, personas, the brief), ideation (names, moodboards, concepts), design and systems (identity, UI, components), prototyping and testing, and delivery (files, guidelines, handover). AI tools can take part in every stage, but they do not add the same value at each one.

AI adds the most value where the work needs **volume, speed or variation**:

- **Volume:** summarising 30 interview transcripts, drafting 50 name options or writing 20 versions of an error message.
- **Speed:** turning a rough idea into a first screen layout or a moodboard in minutes instead of hours.
- **Variation:** showing you directions you would not have tried, such as a warmer palette or a denser layout.

AI adds the most risk where the work needs **originality, accuracy or rights**:

- **Originality:** image generators learn from huge sets of existing images. Their output can drift towards familiar clichés or look close to existing marks.
- **Accuracy:** language models can invent quotes, overstate patterns in research or suggest a colour pair that "looks accessible" but is not.
- **Rights:** AI cannot tell you whether a name is free to use or whether an AI-assisted logo can be protected. Those questions need human checks and, in some cases, legal advice.

There is also a third category: **judgement**. Choosing which insight matters most, which direction fits the client and which trade-off to accept is the designer's job. AI can argue for options, but it does not carry responsibility for the result. You do.

**Analogy:** Think of AI as a fast junior assistant on your team. The assistant works quickly, never gets tired and produces many options. But the assistant has no memory of your client meetings, sometimes invents details with confidence and does not know the law. You would never send a junior's first draft to a client without a senior review. Treat every AI output the same way.

A simple way to plan AI use on a project is to mark each stage with one of three labels:

1. **AI drafts, I decide.** AI produces options; you select and refine. Good for ideation and microcopy.
2. **AI checks, I own.** You do the work; AI reviews it for gaps. Good for briefs and critique.
3. **No AI.** Work that involves confidential data you may not share, final legal decisions, or relationships with people.

## Worked Example
Mateo Rojas is a freelance product designer in Santiago, Chile. A hypothetical client, a small chain of bakeries, wants a new brand and a simple ordering app.

Mateo maps his usual process and marks each stage:

| Stage | AI role | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interviews | No AI during the interviews | Relationships and consent matter; he takes his own notes |
| Research synthesis | AI drafts, I decide | Claude groups anonymised notes into themes; Mateo checks every quote |
| Brief | AI checks, I own | Claude asks critical questions about the draft brief |
| Names and moodboards | AI drafts, I decide | High volume and variation help here |
| Logo final art | No AI for final art | He redraws the chosen concept by hand in Figma |
| UI screens | AI drafts, I decide | Figma AI features give a first layout; he rebuilds with his components |
| Trademark checks | No AI for decisions | Needs official searches and possibly a lawyer |

Mateo notices that his plan uses AI for more than half of the stages, but the decisions remain his at every stage. He writes this plan into his project notes so he can later explain his process to the client.

## Common Mistake
Many designers think the choice is "use AI" or "do not use AI" for the whole project. That leads either to a fully AI-made result that nobody can defend, or to missing real time savings. The better approach is stage by stage: decide the role of AI for each task, and decide in advance who reviews its output. A second mistake is to measure AI only by speed. A fast draft that needs three rounds of fixing is not faster.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI adds value where design work needs volume, speed or variation, such as synthesis, ideation and drafting.
2. AI adds risk where work needs originality, accuracy or rights, so those outputs always need human checks.
3. Plan AI use stage by stage with clear roles: "AI drafts, I decide", "AI checks, I own" or "No AI".

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Map your own current design process and mark 3 steps where AI could help and 1 step where you would not use it, with reasons.
**Tools:** Figma or FigJam (free plan), or pen and paper.
**Steps:**
1. List the stages of a recent real or practice project, from first client contact to handover. Aim for 8 to 12 steps.
2. For each step, write what you produce (for example, "interview notes" or "screen flow").
3. Mark 3 steps where AI could help. Label each "AI drafts, I decide" or "AI checks, I own", and write one sentence on the benefit (volume, speed or variation).
4. Mark 1 step where you would not use AI. Write one sentence on the risk (originality, accuracy, rights or confidentiality).
5. For each AI step, note who reviews the output and what they check.
**What good looks like:** A clear map with specific steps (not "design" as one step), 3 AI steps with a named benefit and a named reviewer, and 1 "No AI" step with a concrete reason.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
