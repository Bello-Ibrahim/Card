## L06 Naming and Verbal Identity

- **Filename:** `ai-23-ai-for-brand-and-product-design_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (704 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Pronunciation: 'Gcwalisa' contains a click consonant (gc). Get a native-speaker recording and set a HeyGen pronunciation override, or have the editor check the avatar's reading; do not publish a mispronounced version.

```text
Claude can give you a hundred brand names in ten seconds. The hard part is the next step. Finding the three that mean the right thing, sound right in every market, and that your client can actually use.

Welcome to module two, where we build a brand identity. We start with words. This lesson is about naming and verbal identity.

Naming is one of the best tasks for AI in brand design, because it needs a lot of variation. It is also one of the riskiest. A name must be meaningful, easy to say, and legally available.

Step one is to generate from the brief. Give Claude your audience, markets, languages, personality, and what the name must avoid. Then ask for names in four categories. Descriptive names say what the business does. Evocative names suggest a feeling. Invented names are new words. Compound names join two real words.

Ask for a one-line reason for each name, so you can reject weak logic quickly. And never ask for names like an existing brand. Describe qualities instead.

Step two is tone of voice, because a verbal identity is more than a name. Ask Claude for three short directions, like calm and expert, or playful and local. Ask for three sample lines each, such as a welcome message and an error message. Then choose one direction, and edit the lines yourself.

Step three is to filter. Check meaning in other languages. Does the word mean something negative in your markets? Claude can do a first screen, but confirm with native speakers, because models miss slang. Then check pronunciation, and whether the main domain names and social handles are free.

Now the most important point. AI cannot confirm that a name is free to use. It cannot give a legal opinion, and trademark rules differ by country. So your shortlist should be clearly marked, not yet cleared. Official trademark checks happen outside the AI tool.

Think of AI naming as a brainstorm with a very fast colleague who has read a lot of dictionaries, but never visited a trademark office. Their ideas start the conversation. The legal check happens somewhere else.

Let's follow a real naming round. Thandi Mokoena is a brand designer in Johannesburg, South Africa. Her client runs refill stations, where customers bring their own containers for soap, oil and grains.

She asks Claude for twenty names in four categories, with a reason for each. She adds two rules. The name must be easy to say in English, isiZulu and Afrikaans. And it must avoid words that suggest luxury.

She shortlists three. Refilla, an invented name. Full Circle Pantry, a compound. And Gcwalisa, an evocative name that Claude suggests means fill up in isiZulu.

Then she filters. Two isiZulu-speaking colleagues confirm the meaning of Gcwalisa, but say the click sound may be hard in other markets. So it stays a local option. For Refilla, she finds a similar business name in a related field, and marks it high risk. Full Circle Pantry has free domains in her basic check.

Her shortlist slide says it clearly. These names have passed meaning and basic domain checks. None has been cleared as a trademark. The client should order official searches in each launch market before choosing.

A common mistake is to ask Claude whether a name is trademarked, and trust the answer. It may say it found no conflicts, but it has not searched the official registers. Another mistake is testing a name only in English. Check every language with real speakers.

Let's recap. First, generate names from the brief in several categories, with a reason for each, and develop tone of voice with sample lines that you edit. Second, filter by meaning, pronunciation and basic domain availability, and confirm language checks with native speakers. Third, AI cannot clear a name. Trademark searches are still needed, and rules differ by country.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will generate twenty names for your capstone brand, shortlist three, and check each one for meaning in two other languages, and for basic domain availability. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we create moodboards and visual direction with image generators. See you there.
```

## L07 Moodboards and Visual Direction with Image Generators

- **Filename:** `ai-23-ai-for-brand-and-product-design_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (729 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** [VERSION] If Gemini has no aspect-ratio control, the presenter adds 'square format' to each prompt; the screen_steps say so.

```text
A client says: we want it to feel warm but modern. What does that look like? You will see three different answers on screen, in less time than it takes to search a stock library.

Last time, we worked with words. Now we move to images. This lesson is about moodboards and visual direction with image generators.

A moodboard sets the visual direction before any logo or screen exists. Image generators make moodboards faster and more specific, because you can create images for the exact mood in your brief.

The key is to prompt with visual qualities. Describe materials and textures, like raw clay or recycled paper. Colour, like muted terracotta and sage green. Light, like soft morning window light. Era and context. And composition, such as close-up, flat lay, or a lot of empty space.

Never prompt with the name of a living artist, a photographer or an existing brand. It is unfair to the people whose work is copied. It can create rights problems. And it makes your direction look borrowed. Qualities give you better control anyway.

Next, create contrast, not variations. One direction with eighteen images tells the client nothing. Three clearly different directions for the same brief start a real conversation. Change one or two big variables between directions, such as the light and the era, and keep the brief the same.

And know the tool's limits. Free image generators differ in daily limits, quality, whether images are public, and what their terms allow for commercial use. These things change often. So read the current terms before you show images to a client, and label every moodboard as AI-generated reference, not final artwork.

Think of a moodboard prompt as a recipe written by ingredient, not by restaurant. Tastes like that famous restaurant gives the cook nothing to work with, and copies someone else. Smoked paprika, charred peppers and lime describe the result you want. And the dish becomes yours.

Let's build one. Diego Hernández is a brand designer in Oaxaca, Mexico. His client is a small ceramics studio that sells tableware online. The brief says: handmade, calm, contemporary, for young professionals setting up their first home.

Diego opens Google Gemini in his browser and signs in. First, he checks the settings he needs, and chooses a square format for the moodboard tiles.

He types the prompt for direction A. Handmade ceramic bowls on a pale oak table, soft morning window light, muted terracotta and cream, a lot of empty space, a calm contemporary kitchen, close-up photograph. He generates a set, keeps the two strongest images, and notes why.

Then he varies the subject, but keeps the direction. Hands shaping raw clay, same light and palette. He keeps two more.

Direction B changes the light and the mood. Glazed plates in deep cobalt and white, hard midday shadows, a bright outdoor courtyard, bold geometric composition. Direction C goes the other way. Matte charcoal ceramics on dark slate, a single warm lamp at night, quiet and minimal, wide shot.

He generates six images for each direction and downloads them. In Figma, he creates three frames. A, Morning Studio. B, Courtyard Colour. C, Evening Quiet. He places six images in each frame.

Under each frame, he writes one sentence on the idea, and adds the label AI-generated reference. The client sees three clearly different answers to warm but modern, and chooses direction A, with the cobalt accent from B.

A common mistake is to generate dozens of images from one prompt and call it a moodboard. That shows one idea many times. Another is to prompt in the style of a well-known artist. Describe materials, colour, light and era instead.

Let's recap. First, prompt with visual qualities, such as materials, colour, light, era and composition, never with the names of living artists or existing brands. Second, create three contrasting directions for the same brief, by changing one or two big variables. Third, free image generators change their limits and terms often, so check them, and label moodboard images as AI-generated references.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will create three contrasting moodboards of six images each for your capstone brand, and write one sentence on the idea behind each. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we explore logos, using AI as a sketchbook, not final art. See you there.
```

## L08 Logo Exploration: AI as Sketchbook, Not Final Art

- **Filename:** `ai-23-ai-for-brand-and-product-design_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (728 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
An image generator gives you a logo that looks finished. Clean edges, nice colours and a clever shape. So why would a professional designer still redraw it from the beginning?

Last time, we built moodboards. Now we move to the mark itself. This lesson is about logo exploration, with AI as a sketchbook, not final art.

Image generators are useful early in logo design, when you explore many shapes, metaphors and compositions. Prompt with the idea and the form, not with existing logos. For example, a coffee bean that is also a sun rising over hills. Simple flat vector symbol, two colours, thick even lines, no text.

Change one variable at a time. Geometric or organic. Open or closed. Symmetric or not. Then choose a direction based on the brief, not on which image looks most polished.

But raw AI logos carry three risks. The first is similarity. Generators learn from huge image collections that include many logos, so a result can look close to an existing mark without you knowing. Use a reverse image search, and remember that official trademark checks are still needed before launch.

The second risk is weak scalability. Generated images are pixels, not vectors. Edges are uneven, details disappear at small sizes, and shapes do not follow a grid. A logo must work as a tiny browser icon, and on the side of a building.

The third risk is ownership. Copyright rules for work made mostly by AI are still unclear, and they differ by country. If the client cannot own their logo, a competitor might be able to use something very similar.

So, redraw. Choose the direction, then rebuild it yourself in Figma as a clean vector. Use a grid, make your own decisions about proportions, curves and weight, and simplify until it works small. That is better design, and clearly your own work. But it is good practice, not a legal guarantee.

Think of an AI concept as a photo reference for a painter. The photo helps you see the idea, but the painting comes from your own decisions with the brush. Copying the photo line by line is tracing. Interpreting it is design.

Let's see this in practice. Selam Tesfaye is a brand designer in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Her client is a small coffee roaster that sells to cafés. The brief asks for a mark that suggests origin, warmth and care.

She generates ten concepts with prompts like this one: a coffee cherry combined with a rising sun, as a simple flat symbol, with no text. She puts the ten in Figma and scores each against the brief. Meaning, simplicity, and difference from typical coffee logos.

She picks a direction where the sun's rays become the lines of a coffee bean. A reverse image search finds no close matches. But she writes a note: visual search is not a trademark clearance.

Then she redraws the mark in Figma. She sets up a forty-eight unit grid, and draws the bean with the ellipse and pen tools. She makes the rays equal width, with equal gaps. One ray disappears at sixteen pixels, so she removes it.

She converts the outlines into one clean vector shape, and tests it at sixteen, sixty-four and five hundred and twelve pixels. Her file notes that the concept was explored with an image generator, the final mark was redrawn by hand, and a trademark search and ownership advice are recommended before launch.

A common mistake is to auto-trace an AI image and call it redrawn. That keeps the uneven shapes, and adds no real design decisions. Another mistake is believing that redrawing makes a logo legally safe. It does not. Ownership and similarity still need proper checks.

Let's recap. First, use image generators as a sketchbook for fast logo exploration, prompting with concept and form, not existing logos. Second, raw AI logos are risky. They may look like existing marks, they scale poorly, and their copyright protection is uncertain. Third, redraw your chosen direction by hand in Figma, and still recommend trademark and legal checks.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will generate ten logo concepts, pick one direction, and redraw it as a vector mark in Figma at three sizes. It takes about forty minutes. In the next lesson, we build colour, type and accessible brand systems. See you there.
```

## L09 Colour, Type and Accessible Brand Systems

- **Filename:** `ai-23-ai-for-brand-and-product-design_M2_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (713 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Claude suggests a beautiful palette, and writes: these colours have good contrast. Do they? The only way to know is to measure.

Last time, we redrew a logo by hand. Now we turn it into a brand system. This lesson is about colour, type and accessible brand systems.

Claude can help here, because it can suggest options with reasons linked to your brief. For example, ask for three palettes of five colours, with hex codes, a role for each colour, and one reason. For type, ask for pairings that are free to use and support the scripts you need, such as Latin and Arabic.

Treat these suggestions as a starting point. Language models can give hex codes that do not match the colour they describe. They can claim contrast values without calculating them. And they can suggest fonts that do not exist, or that do not support your scripts. So you test everything yourself.

First, test colour contrast. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, or WCAG, set minimum contrast ratios between text and background, with a higher minimum for normal body text than for large text. Test every text and background pair you plan to use, with a contrast checker plugin in Figma or a free web checker.

If a pair fails, darken or lighten one colour until it passes. Then keep the original colour for decoration only.

Next, test type in real conditions. Set body text at small sizes, like twelve to fourteen pixels, and check that a capital I, a small l and the number one are clear. If your brand uses other scripts, such as Arabic or Devanagari, test real words in those scripts. Check the weights, the line height, and ask a reader of that script to review it.

Think of an AI palette as a paint chart from a shop. The colours look good on the card, but you still test them on your own wall, in your own light, before you paint the whole room.

Let's measure a real palette. Omar Haddad is a product designer in Amman, Jordan. His client is a money-transfer app for workers who send money between Jordan and Nepal. The brand must work in English, Arabic and Nepali, which uses the Devanagari script.

Claude suggests five colours: navy, saffron, cream, green and warm grey. It says that all of them work well for text. Omar does not accept that. He tests each text pair he plans to use.

Navy on cream measures ten point two two to one, and passes. But saffron on white measures only two point nine nine to one. Green on white and warm grey on cream also fail for body text. Saffron on navy works for large text only.

So he fixes the failures. He darkens saffron for text and buttons, and it now measures four point seven eight to one on white. He keeps the brighter saffron for illustrations. He also darkens the green and the warm grey until both pass.

For type, Claude suggested a pairing where one font had no Devanagari support. Omar chooses a free font family with Latin, Arabic and Devanagari versions. He tests transfer amounts and short messages at fourteen pixels in all three scripts, and asks two colleagues who read Arabic and Nepali to check the text.

A common mistake is to accept an AI statement like this palette is accessible, without measuring. Another is to test only the main brand colour on white, and forget grey helper text, button text or text on coloured cards. List every pair you will use, and test each one.

Let's recap. First, use Claude to suggest palettes and type pairings with roles and reasons, but treat every hex code, font and contrast claim as unverified. Second, test every text and background pair against WCAG contrast guidance, and fix failures by adjusting the colour. Third, test type at small sizes and in every script your brand needs, with real readers.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will build a palette of five colours and a type pairing for your brand, test all text colour pairs for contrast, and fix any that fail. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we build brand guidelines in Figma. See you there.
```

## L10 Building Brand Guidelines in Figma

- **Filename:** `ai-23-ai-for-brand-and-product-design_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (703 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A brand is only as consistent as the people who use it after you leave the project. What will they open when they need the right blue, or the correct space around the logo?

Last time, we tested colour and type. Now we collect all your brand decisions in one place. This lesson is about building brand guidelines in Figma, and it is also the first step of your capstone.

Brand guidelines turn your decisions into rules that other people can follow. One clear Figma page is enough to start. It covers four things. Logo usage, with versions, minimum size, clear space and examples of incorrect use. Colour, with names, roles, hex codes and tested text pairs. Type, with fonts, a size scale and line heights. And example applications.

Now, make the rules live in Figma. Instead of drawing swatches, set up colours and type as reusable styles or variables. Turn the logo versions into components. Then every example uses the real values. If you change a colour later, every example updates.

Writing rules is slow, so let Claude draft short usage notes from your decisions. Give it the facts, such as the minimum logo size, the clear space, and colours the logo must never sit on. Ask for short, direct rules in plain language.

Then edit. Remove anything you did not decide, check every number, and make the tone match the brand. The AI drafts. You set the rules.

Think of brand guidelines as the rules of a sport. Players do not need the history of the game. But they need to know the size of the field, the lines they cannot cross, and what counts as a foul. Clear examples of allowed and not allowed work better than long explanations.

And keep the page easy to scan, with headings, short rules and visual examples. A person should find the right answer in under a minute.

Let's build one. Sanne de Vries is a freelance designer in Utrecht, in the Netherlands. Her client is a cycling café, where people have coffee while their bike is repaired. She already has the logo, the palette and the type.

In Figma, she creates a page called Brand Guidelines, with a frame fourteen hundred and forty pixels wide. She creates colour styles for her five colours, named by role, such as brand primary and text default. Then text styles for two heading levels, body and caption.

She turns the logo versions into components: the full logo, the icon only, and a one-colour version. Then she builds the logo section, with a clear-space diagram and four examples of incorrect use. Stretched, recoloured, rotated, and placed on a busy photo.

Next, she asks Claude to draft usage notes from her facts. Most are good. Always use the logo on a plain background. Never add effects such as shadows. But Claude also adds a rule about black and white logos for newspapers. She never decided that, so she deletes it.

She builds two applications, a coffee cup sleeve and a repair-ticket screen, using only her styles and components. Then she tests the system. She changes the primary colour slightly, and both applications update. Now the client's staff can use the page without asking her every time.

A common mistake is to build guidelines as a picture, with colours drawn as rectangles and text set by hand. It looks correct, but nothing is connected, so later changes create inconsistency. Another mistake is pasting AI-drafted rules without checking them. The draft may include rules you never made.

Let's recap. First, a one-page guidelines frame covers logo usage, colour, type and example applications, and it should be easy to scan. Second, set up colours and type as styles or variables, and the logo as components, so everything updates together. Third, let Claude draft usage notes, then check every rule and number, and remove anything you did not decide.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone. You will build a one-page brand guidelines frame in Figma, with your logo, colours, type and two example applications. It takes about forty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we start module three, with generating UI drafts in Figma. See you there.
```
