# L09 Running a Pilot and Measuring Results

Course: AI-04 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O5, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Three months after launch, a manager reports that the new AI tool is "going well". You ask how much time it saves and how many errors it makes. Nobody knows, because nobody measured how things were before. Was it a success?

## Explanation
A pilot is a small, time-limited test of an AI use case in real work, designed to produce evidence for a decision. A good pilot has five parts.

**1. A clear scope.** One use case, one team, a limited set of tasks. Small scope keeps risk low and makes results easier to understand.

**2. A baseline.** Measure how the work performs before the pilot starts: time per task, error rate, cost, customer or staff satisfaction. Without a baseline, you cannot prove any change.

**3. Success measures.** Choose about three measures, agreed before the pilot starts. Include at least one measure of value, such as time or cost, one measure of quality or risk, such as error rate, and one measure of people, such as staff or customer satisfaction. Set a target for each.

**4. A fixed duration and a decision date.** Most pilots need enough time for staff to learn the tool and for results to settle. Put the decision date in the calendar on day one.

**5. A feedback plan.** Staff who use the tool see problems first. Plan regular short check-ins and a simple way to report issues.

At the decision date, choose one of three options: **go** (scale up), **adjust** (change and test again) or **stop**. Stopping is a valid outcome. A pilot that stops a weak idea early has saved money.

**Analogy:** A pilot is like a clinical trial for a new treatment. You record the patient's condition first, give the treatment for a fixed period, measure agreed outcomes and then decide whether to continue. You do not decide based on how the patient feels on day two.

## Worked Example
Carlos Herrera is head of customer service at a hypothetical insurance company in Mexico. He wants to test an AI tool that drafts replies to customer emails about claims status. Agents edit and send every reply.

**Scope:** one team of 12 agents, claims-status emails only, in Spanish.

**Baseline:** for four weeks before the pilot, the team records average handling time per email, the percentage of replies that need a second contact, and customer satisfaction from the existing short survey.

**Success measures:**
1. Average handling time falls by an agreed target.
2. The second-contact rate does not increase.
3. Agent satisfaction with the tool is at least 3.5 out of 5 in a short survey.

**Risks and controls:** agents check every draft; no draft is sent automatically; the tool is set up so customer data is not used to train the vendor's models, as agreed in the contract.

**Duration and decision date:** eight weeks, with a go, adjust or stop meeting in the ninth week.

**Feedback plan:** a 15-minute team check-in every Friday and a shared log for wrong or unhelpful drafts.

At the decision meeting, handling time has improved and the second-contact rate is stable, but agents report that drafts are weak for complex claims. Carlos decides to **adjust**: keep the tool for simple emails and exclude complex claims.

## Common Mistake
Many pilots measure success only by asking users whether they like the tool. Opinions matter, but they are not enough. Without a baseline and agreed measures, a pilot cannot show whether the tool improved the work, and decisions become a matter of who argues best.

## Key Takeaways
1. A good pilot is small, time-limited and measured against a baseline taken before it starts.
2. Agree about three success measures in advance, covering value, quality or risk, and people.
3. Set a decision date for go, adjust or stop, and treat stopping as a valid result.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Capstone step 1: choose one or two priority use cases and write a pilot plan with a baseline, three success measures, a duration and a decision date.
**Tools:** A document or spreadsheet; your scoring table from L04 and risk register from L06.
**Steps:**
1. Choose one or two use cases from your top two in L04.
2. Define the scope: team, tasks and any exclusions.
3. Describe how you will measure the baseline and for how long.
4. Write three success measures with targets: value, quality or risk, and people.
5. Copy the top two risks and controls from your risk register.
6. Set the duration, the decision date and who will attend the decision meeting.
7. Describe your staff feedback plan.
**What good looks like:** A one-page pilot plan with a clear scope, a measurable baseline, three measures with targets, controls for key risks, a fixed decision date and a simple feedback routine.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
