# L02 Where AI Creates Value

Course: AI-04 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Many organisations start with the question "Where can we use AI?" A better question is "Which of our tasks cost the most time, create the most errors or slow down our customers?" Value comes from the task, not from the technology.

## Explanation
In most organisations, AI creates value in three ways.

**1. Automating routine tasks.** AI takes over work that is repetitive, high in volume and low in judgement. Examples are sorting documents, extracting data from forms and routing requests to the right team. The value is saved time, faster service and fewer manual errors. People stay responsible for exceptions.

**2. Supporting people's decisions.** AI gives a person better information, but the person still decides. Examples are flagging unusual transactions, forecasting demand or summarising long reports. The value is better or faster decisions. This is often the safest place to start, because a human checks every important outcome.

**3. Enabling new products or services.** AI makes something possible that was not practical before, such as a service available at any hour, or advice tailored to each customer. The value is growth or new reach. It is also the most uncertain of the three, because it depends on customers accepting the new offer.

A useful task for AI usually has four features: it happens often, it follows a pattern, there is data about it, and a mistake can be caught and corrected. A task that is rare, needs deep human trust or has severe consequences for a single error is usually "not suitable for AI", or only suitable with strong human control.

Value also has a price. Every AI use case needs data, people who can use and check it, changes to the workflow, and ongoing cost. The next lesson covers these costs. For now, remember that a use case only creates value if the benefit is larger than all of them together.

**Analogy:** Think of AI as a new team member with a very specific skill set. You would not hire a person and then look for work to give them. You would first find the work that needs doing, then decide what kind of person fits. AI should be chosen the same way.

## Worked Example
Here are three hypothetical organisations, one for each type of value.

**Automate: a logistics firm in Poland.** Katarzyna Nowak runs operations at a mid-sized freight company. Her team types delivery details from hundreds of scanned delivery notes every day. An AI document tool reads the notes and fills in the system. Staff now check only the notes the tool marks as uncertain. The value is time saved and fewer typing errors. The control is a daily sample check.

**Support: a hospital administration team in Kenya.** Wanjiru Kamau leads administration at a public hospital. Her team plans staff rotas and struggles with sudden busy periods. A forecasting tool uses past patient arrival records to predict busy days one week ahead. Wanjiru's team still builds the rota, but with better information. Patient data stays inside the hospital's approved systems, and no individual patient decision is made by the tool.

**Enable: a retail bank in Brazil.** Rafael Souza is head of digital services at a regional bank. The bank wants to answer simple customer questions in Portuguese at any hour through a chat assistant. This is a new service, not only a faster old one. Because it speaks to customers directly, Rafael limits it to general questions, such as opening hours and how to reset a card, and hands any account-specific question to a human.

Notice that each leader chose the level of AI control based on the risk of a mistake, not on what the technology could do.

## Common Mistake
Leaders often jump to "enable new products" because it sounds strategic. New services carry the highest uncertainty and need the most change. Most organisations get faster, safer results by first automating or supporting existing tasks they already understand well, and then building on what they learn.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI creates value in three main ways: automating routine tasks, supporting people's decisions and enabling new products or services.
2. Good tasks for AI happen often, follow a pattern, have data and allow mistakes to be caught.
3. Start from the problem and its cost to the organisation, not from the technology.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** List ten tasks your team does every week and label each one "automate", "support" or "not suitable for AI", with a one-line reason.
**Tools:** A spreadsheet or table in a document. Optional: Claude or ChatGPT to challenge your labels, using general task descriptions only.
**Steps:**
1. Write down ten tasks your team does every week. Include both small and important tasks.
2. For each task, note roughly how often it happens and how many hours it takes per week.
3. Label each task "automate", "support" or "not suitable for AI".
4. Write one reason for each label, using the four features: frequent, patterned, has data, mistakes can be caught.
5. Mark the two tasks where you think value is highest.
**What good looks like:** Ten real tasks with honest labels, at least two tasks marked "not suitable for AI" with clear reasons, and a short note on the two highest-value tasks.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
