# L07 Governance, Policy and Regulation

Course: AI-04 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O4 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Some of your staff probably already use AI tools at work, with or without permission. The question is not whether AI is in your organisation. The question is whether anyone has set the rules.

## Explanation
AI governance is the set of rules, roles and reviews that make sure AI is used safely and in line with your organisation's goals. For most teams it rests on three basic elements.

**1. Clear rules for staff use of AI tools.** An acceptable-use policy tells people which tools are approved, what data they may and may not enter, and when they must check or declare AI output. A good policy is short, practical and written in plain language. Typical rules include:
- Use only approved tools and accounts for work.
- Do not paste personal data, client data or confidential information into tools that are not approved for it. Free plans may have different data terms from business plans. [VERSION]
- A person checks every AI output before it is sent outside the organisation or used in a decision.
- Tell your manager if an AI tool produces something harmful or clearly wrong.

**2. Human oversight for important decisions.** Decisions that affect people's jobs, money, health, rights or access to services need a named person who reviews and can change the AI's suggestion. The more serious the impact, the stronger the review.

**3. A named owner for each AI system.** Every AI tool in use needs one owner who knows what it does, what data it uses, how it performs and when it should be reviewed. A simple list of AI systems, with owners, is one of the most useful governance tools you can create.

**Regulation.** AI, data protection and sector rules differ between countries and are changing. [REGION] As a leader, you do not need to memorise laws. You need to ask your legal or compliance team the right questions:
- Which laws and sector rules apply to this use of AI in each country where we operate?
- Does this use involve personal data, and do we have a legal basis to use it this way?
- Does this use affect decisions about people in ways that need extra controls or disclosure?
- What do our contracts with clients and vendors say about data and AI?
- What must we document, and for how long?

**Analogy:** Governance is like the rules of the road. Traffic rules do not stop people from driving; they make it safe for many people to drive at the same time. A clear AI policy lets staff use AI confidently instead of guessing or hiding it.

## Worked Example
Kwame Asante is managing partner of a hypothetical accountancy firm in Ghana with 45 staff. He learns that several staff use free AI chat tools to draft client letters, and one has pasted a client's financial figures into a tool.

Kwame does not ban AI. He asks an AI assistant to draft a one-page policy, using only a general description of the firm. The draft is useful but too general. He edits it:
- He names one approved business account and says that client data may only be used there, after his IT provider has checked the data terms.
- He adds that every client letter drafted with AI must be checked and signed by a qualified accountant.
- He names the operations manager as owner of the list of AI tools in use.
- He asks the firm's legal adviser which data protection and professional rules apply to client data in AI tools. [REGION]

He shares the policy in a staff meeting, explains the reasons and invites questions. Staff say they feel more confident, because they now know what is allowed.

## Common Mistake
Some leaders respond to AI risk with a total ban. Bans are hard to enforce, and staff often keep using personal accounts where the organisation has no control. A clear policy with approved tools usually reduces risk more than a ban does.

## Key Takeaways
1. Good AI governance rests on clear rules for staff use, human oversight for important decisions and a named owner for each AI system.
2. An acceptable-use policy should be short, practical and specific about which data may go into which tools.
3. Laws differ between countries and change, so leaders should ask their legal team the right questions rather than rely on a list of laws.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Use Claude or ChatGPT to help draft a one-page acceptable-use policy for AI tools in your team, then edit it so every rule fits your organisation.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free plan), and a document editor.
**Steps:**
1. Describe your team in general terms only: sector, size and main tasks. Do not paste confidential information or internal documents. [VERSION]
2. Ask: "Draft a one-page acceptable-use policy for AI tools for this team, covering approved tools, data rules, human review and reporting problems."
3. Read the draft. Delete any rule that is vague or that you could not enforce.
4. Add rules that are specific to your team, such as named approved tools and data types that are never allowed.
5. Name an owner for the policy and a review date.
6. List three questions for your legal or compliance team. [REGION]
**What good looks like:** A one-page policy in plain language with specific data rules, a human-review rule, an owner and review date, and three clear legal questions.
**Time:** about 30 minutes
