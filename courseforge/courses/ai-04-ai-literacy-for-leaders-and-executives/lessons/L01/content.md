# L01 AI for Leaders: What You Need to Know

Course: AI-04 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Your team proposes an "AI solution". A vendor promises "machine learning". A board member asks whether you have a generative AI strategy. Are these three different things, or one thing with three names? A leader who cannot tell the difference will approve the wrong projects.

## Explanation
You do not need to build AI. You need to understand enough to ask good questions and make sound decisions. Five terms cover most conversations.

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** is the broad name for computer systems that perform tasks we usually link with human judgement, such as recognising images, understanding language or making recommendations. It is an umbrella term, so on its own it tells you very little.

**Machine learning** is the most common way to build AI today. Instead of people writing every rule, the system learns patterns from many past examples. A machine learning system that has seen thousands of past invoices can learn to flag unusual ones. It predicts or classifies; it does not "think".

**Training data** is the set of examples the system learns from. Its quality sets the limit on the system's quality. If the examples are old, incomplete or unbalanced, the system will repeat those weaknesses. For a leader, the first question about any AI system is: "What data was it trained on, and does it look like our situation?"

**A model** is the result of training: the file of learned patterns that makes predictions on new cases. When a vendor says "our model", they mean this learned engine. Models can become out of date when the world changes, so they need checking over time.

**Generative AI** is a type of machine learning that creates new content, such as text, images, code or audio. Chat assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT are examples. They produce fluent answers by predicting likely words. Fluent does not mean correct: they can state false information with confidence.

**Automation** is different from all of the above. It means a process runs without a person doing each step. Much automation uses simple fixed rules and no AI at all, for example "if the invoice is approved, send payment on day 30". AI becomes useful when the task has too much variety for fixed rules.

Why does the difference matter for decisions? Each type has a different cost, risk and data need. Rule-based automation is predictable and cheap to check. Machine learning needs good historical data and regular monitoring. Generative AI is flexible and fast to try, but needs human review of its outputs.

**Analogy:** A leader does not need to repair an engine, but needs to know what fuel it uses, what it can carry and when it can break down. For AI, the fuel is data, the load is the task you give it, and the breakdowns are wrong outputs, bias and outdated models.

## Worked Example
Nadia Rahman is chief executive of a hypothetical textile exporter in Bangladesh with about 400 staff. Her managers bring her three proposals in one week.

The finance manager wants "AI" to send payment reminders to customers. Nadia asks what decides when a reminder is sent. The answer is a fixed schedule. This is automation with simple rules, and the existing accounting software may already do it. No AI is needed.

The quality manager wants a camera system that spots fabric faults. Nadia asks what it learns from. The vendor needs thousands of labelled photos of good and faulty fabric. This is machine learning, and the real work is collecting and labelling the right training data from her own factory.

The sales director wants to use a generative AI assistant to draft replies to buyer emails. Nadia asks who checks the drafts and what information is typed into the tool. They agree that a person reviews every draft, and that prices and contract terms are never pasted into a free tool.

By using the five terms, Nadia turns three vague "AI projects" into three clear decisions with different costs and risks.

## Common Mistake
Many leaders treat "AI" as one product that is either good or bad. In reality, a rules-based workflow, a machine learning model and a generative AI assistant need different data, different checks and different skills. Ask your team which type they mean before you discuss budget or risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Machine learning learns patterns from training data; the resulting model makes predictions and is only as good as that data.
2. Generative AI creates new content that sounds fluent but can be wrong, so outputs need human review.
3. Automation does not always need AI; simple rules are often cheaper and more reliable for stable tasks.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Build a short glossary of five AI terms, explained with examples from your own industry, that you would be comfortable using with your board.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free plan), and a notes app or document.
**Steps:**
1. Open Claude or ChatGPT. Do not paste confidential company information or personal data. Free plans may have different data terms from business plans. [VERSION]
2. Ask: "Explain machine learning, generative AI, model, training data and automation in two sentences each, using examples from the [your industry] sector."
3. Read each answer. Mark any explanation that is wrong, too technical or does not fit your organisation.
4. Rewrite each definition in your own words, in no more than two sentences.
5. Add one question a board member might ask about each term, with your short answer.
**What good looks like:** Five plain definitions with real examples from your sector, at least one correction to the AI's first answer, and board questions that link each term to cost, data or risk.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
