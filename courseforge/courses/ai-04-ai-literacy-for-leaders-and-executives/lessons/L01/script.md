# L01 AI for Leaders: What You Need to Know | Presenter Script

Course: AI-04 · Video: 5 min · Words: 693

## Hook
Your team proposes an AI solution. A vendor promises machine learning. A board member asks about your generative AI strategy. Are these three different things, or one thing with three names? A leader who cannot tell the difference will approve the wrong projects.

## Explain
Hello, and welcome to AI Literacy for Leaders and Executives. You do not need to build AI. You need to understand enough to ask good questions and make sound decisions. Five terms cover most conversations.

The first is artificial intelligence, or AI. It is the broad name for systems that do tasks we link with human judgement, such as understanding language or making recommendations. It is an umbrella term, so on its own it tells you very little.

The second is machine learning, the most common way to build AI today. Instead of people writing every rule, the system learns patterns from past examples. Those examples are the third term, training data. A system trained on thousands of past invoices can learn to flag unusual ones.

The result of training is the fourth term, the model. It is the learned engine that makes predictions on new cases. A model is only as good as its data, and it can go out of date. So always ask, what was it trained on, and does it look like our situation?

The fifth term is generative AI. It creates new content, such as text, images or code. Chat assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT are examples. They write fluent answers by predicting likely words. But fluent does not mean correct.

Finally, automation is different from all of these. It means a process runs without a person doing each step, and it often uses simple fixed rules with no AI at all. For example, if the invoice is approved, send payment on day thirty.

Think of it this way. A leader does not need to repair an engine. But you do need to know what fuel it uses, what it can carry and when it can break down. For AI, the fuel is data, the load is the task, and the breakdowns are wrong outputs, bias and outdated models.

## Demonstrate
Let's see this in practice. Nadia Rahman is chief executive of a fictional textile exporter in Bangladesh, with about four hundred staff. In one week, her managers bring her three AI proposals.

The finance manager wants AI to send payment reminders. Nadia asks what decides when a reminder goes out. The answer is a fixed schedule. So this is simple automation, and their accounting software may already do it. No AI is needed.

The quality manager wants a camera system that spots fabric faults. Nadia asks what it learns from. The vendor needs thousands of labelled photos of good and faulty fabric. This is machine learning, and the real work is collecting that training data from her own factory.

The sales director wants a generative AI assistant to draft replies to buyer emails. Nadia asks who checks the drafts and what goes into the tool. They agree that a person reviews every draft, and that prices and contract terms never go into a free tool.

With five terms, Nadia turns three vague AI projects into three clear decisions, each with its own cost and risk. That is the common mistake to avoid. AI is not one product that is good or bad. Ask your team which type they mean before you discuss budget or risk.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, machine learning learns patterns from training data, and the model it produces is only as good as that data. Second, generative AI writes fluent content that can be wrong, so people must review it. Third, automation does not always need AI. Simple rules are often cheaper and more reliable for stable tasks.
