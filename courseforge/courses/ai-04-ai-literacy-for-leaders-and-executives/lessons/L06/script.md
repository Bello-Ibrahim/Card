# L06 AI Risks Every Leader Must Manage | Presenter Script

Course: AI-04 · Video: 5 min · Words: 685

## Hook
An AI tool saves your team many hours a week. Then one wrong output reaches a customer, a regulator or a journalist. Suddenly the conversation is not about time saved, but about trust lost. Which risks should you manage before that day comes?

## Explain
Welcome to week two. Last week, you found and prioritised your AI opportunities. Now we manage the risks. AI risk is manageable when you name it clearly, rate it and give it an owner. Six categories cover most situations.

The first is wrong or invented outputs. AI can give inaccurate predictions. Generative AI can invent facts, sources or numbers with confidence. The control is human review for anything that reaches customers or affects decisions, plus regular sample checks.

The second is bias and unfair treatment. A model trained on past decisions can repeat past unfairness towards certain groups. The control is to test results across groups before and after launch, and keep a person responsible for final decisions about people.

Third, privacy and data leaks. Staff may paste confidential data into tools that store or reuse it. Control it with clear data rules, approved business accounts and training. Fourth, security. Hidden instructions in a document or email can change a tool's behaviour. Limit what each tool can access, involve IT security and keep logs.

Fifth, legal and contractual exposure. AI use can conflict with data protection, employment or consumer rules, or with client contracts. These rules differ by country and change over time, so ask your legal team to review high-impact uses before launch.

Sixth, damage to reputation. Even a legal and accurate use can upset people if it feels hidden or unfair. The control is to be open about where you use AI, and to offer a way to reach a person.

Think of a building site. You do not stop building. You wear helmets, fence off dangerous areas and name a safety officer. Each hazard has a matching control, and a person who checks it. AI risk works the same way.

## Demonstrate
The tool for this is a risk register. It is a simple table. It lists each risk, rates likelihood and impact from one to five, and names a control and an owner. Multiply likelihood by impact to see which risks need attention first.

Here is an example. Priya Sandhu is HR director at a fictional engineering company in Canada. Her team wants an AI tool that screens job applications and ranks candidates. Before any pilot, she builds a risk register.

Her register has six risks. The tool might rank strong candidates low because of unusual CV formats. Applicant data might be reused by the vendor. Hidden text in a CV might change the ranking. Candidates might feel judged by a machine. Each risk gets a control and an owner, from the recruitment lead to legal counsel.

The highest score is bias, three times five, fifteen. The tool could favour groups that were hired more often in the past. So Priya sets a condition for the pilot. The tool may only sort applications for recruiters to review. It may not reject anyone automatically.

Notice who owns that risk. It is Priya, the HR director, not the IT team. That is the common mistake. Most AI risks are business risks, such as unfair treatment, broken trust and legal exposure. Technical staff help with controls, but a business leader must decide how much risk is acceptable.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, the main AI risks are wrong outputs, bias, privacy, security, legal exposure and damage to reputation. Second, a risk register rates each risk on likelihood and impact, and names one control and one owner. Third, business leaders, not only technical teams, must own AI risks.
