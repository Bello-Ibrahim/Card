## L01 AI for Leaders: What You Need to Know

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (693 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your team proposes an AI solution. A vendor promises machine learning. A board member asks about your generative AI strategy. Are these three different things, or one thing with three names? A leader who cannot tell the difference will approve the wrong projects.

Hello, and welcome to AI Literacy for Leaders and Executives. You do not need to build AI. You need to understand enough to ask good questions and make sound decisions. Five terms cover most conversations.

The first is artificial intelligence, or AI. It is the broad name for systems that do tasks we link with human judgement, such as understanding language or making recommendations. It is an umbrella term, so on its own it tells you very little.

The second is machine learning, the most common way to build AI today. Instead of people writing every rule, the system learns patterns from past examples. Those examples are the third term, training data. A system trained on thousands of past invoices can learn to flag unusual ones.

The result of training is the fourth term, the model. It is the learned engine that makes predictions on new cases. A model is only as good as its data, and it can go out of date. So always ask, what was it trained on, and does it look like our situation?

The fifth term is generative AI. It creates new content, such as text, images or code. Chat assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT are examples. They write fluent answers by predicting likely words. But fluent does not mean correct.

Finally, automation is different from all of these. It means a process runs without a person doing each step, and it often uses simple fixed rules with no AI at all. For example, if the invoice is approved, send payment on day thirty.

Think of it this way. A leader does not need to repair an engine. But you do need to know what fuel it uses, what it can carry and when it can break down. For AI, the fuel is data, the load is the task, and the breakdowns are wrong outputs, bias and outdated models.

Let's see this in practice. Nadia Rahman is chief executive of a fictional textile exporter in Bangladesh, with about four hundred staff. In one week, her managers bring her three AI proposals.

The finance manager wants AI to send payment reminders. Nadia asks what decides when a reminder goes out. The answer is a fixed schedule. So this is simple automation, and their accounting software may already do it. No AI is needed.

The quality manager wants a camera system that spots fabric faults. Nadia asks what it learns from. The vendor needs thousands of labelled photos of good and faulty fabric. This is machine learning, and the real work is collecting that training data from her own factory.

The sales director wants a generative AI assistant to draft replies to buyer emails. Nadia asks who checks the drafts and what goes into the tool. They agree that a person reviews every draft, and that prices and contract terms never go into a free tool.

With five terms, Nadia turns three vague AI projects into three clear decisions, each with its own cost and risk. That is the common mistake to avoid. AI is not one product that is good or bad. Ask your team which type they mean before you discuss budget or risk.

Let's recap. First, machine learning learns patterns from training data, and the model it produces is only as good as that data. Second, generative AI writes fluent content that can be wrong, so people must review it. Third, automation does not always need AI. Simple rules are often cheaper and more reliable for stable tasks.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will ask Claude or ChatGPT to explain these five terms with examples from your own industry. Then you will correct anything you would not say to your board. Please do not paste confidential company data into a free tool. In the next lesson, we look at where AI creates value. See you there.
```

## L02 Where AI Creates Value

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (682 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Many organisations start with one question. Where can we use AI? A better question is this. Which of our tasks cost the most time, create the most errors or slow down our customers? Value comes from the task, not from the technology.

In the last lesson, we learned the five terms leaders need. Now let's ask where AI actually creates value. In most organisations, it does so in three ways.

The first way is automating routine tasks. This is work that is repetitive, high in volume and low in judgement, such as sorting documents or routing requests. The value is saved time and fewer manual errors. People stay responsible for the exceptions.

The second way is supporting people's decisions. AI gives a person better information, but the person still decides. Think of flagging unusual transactions or forecasting demand. This is often the safest place to start, because a human checks every important outcome.

The third way is enabling new products or services, such as a service available at any hour. The value is growth or new reach. But it is also the most uncertain, because it depends on customers accepting something new.

A good task for AI has four features. It happens often. It follows a pattern. There is data about it. And a mistake can be caught and corrected. A rare task, or one where a single error is severe, usually needs strong human control, or no AI at all.

Value also has a price. Every use case needs data, people who can use and check it, changes to the workflow and ongoing cost. We cover these costs in the next lesson. For now, remember this. A use case only creates value if the benefit is larger than all of those costs together.

Here is a useful way to think about it. AI is like a new team member with a very specific skill set. You would not hire a person and then look for work to give them. You find the work first, then choose who fits. AI should be chosen the same way.

Let's look at three fictional organisations, one for each type of value. First, automate. Katarzyna Nowak runs operations at a freight company in Poland. Her team types details from hundreds of scanned delivery notes every day.

An AI document tool now reads the notes and fills in the system. Staff check only the notes the tool marks as uncertain. The value is time saved and fewer typing errors, and the control is a daily sample check.

Second, support. Wanjiru Kamau leads administration at a public hospital in Kenya. A forecasting tool uses past patient arrival records to predict busy days one week ahead. Her team still builds the staff rota, but with better information. Patient data stays inside the hospital's approved systems.

Third, enable. Rafael Souza is head of digital services at a regional bank in Brazil. The bank adds a chat assistant that answers simple questions in Portuguese at any hour. This is a new service, not only a faster old one. Rafael limits it to general questions, and hands anything about a customer's account to a human.

Notice that each leader chose the level of AI control based on the risk of a mistake, not on what the technology could do. And avoid a common mistake. New products sound strategic, but most organisations get faster, safer results by first automating or supporting tasks they already know well.

Let's recap. First, AI creates value in three main ways: automating routine tasks, supporting decisions and enabling new products or services. Second, good tasks for AI happen often, follow a pattern, have data and allow mistakes to be caught. Third, start from the problem and its cost, not from the technology.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will list ten tasks your team does every week. You will label each one automate, support or not suitable for AI, with a one-line reason. In the next lesson, we look at the real costs of AI: data, people and process. See you there.
```

## L03 The Real Costs: Data, People and Process

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (680 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Pronunciation: 'te reo Māori' is said 'teh REH-aw MAH-aw-ree'. Check the HeyGen voice pronounces it respectfully; use a phonetic override if needed.

```text
A vendor quotes a monthly subscription that fits easily inside your budget. Six months later, the project has used far more money and staff time than planned, and the tool is barely used. Where did the cost go?

In the last lesson, we saw where AI creates value. Now let's look at what it really costs. The licence or subscription is often only a small part of the total. The larger costs usually sit in four areas.

The first is data. AI needs data that is available, accurate and allowed to be used. Often the data sits in several systems, in different formats, with gaps. Cleaning and joining it takes time from people who know the business, and you may need a legal review first.

The second is people. Staff need training to use the tool and know its limits. Someone must own the project. Some staff will worry about their jobs, and managers need time to listen and explain. If people do not trust the tool, they will work around it.

The third is process. An AI tool changes how work flows. Who checks the output? What happens when the tool is unsure? Which steps are removed, and which are added? If the old process stays the same, AI often adds work instead of removing it.

The fourth is oversight. Outputs must be checked, especially at the start. Models can become less accurate when conditions change, so someone must monitor results and decide when to adjust or stop. This is an ongoing cost, not a one-time task.

Here is a simple rule for leaders. Before you approve an AI project, ask for an estimate under each of the four headings, in staff days as well as money. If the team can only describe the licence cost, the plan is not ready.

Think of a gym membership. Buying one does not make you fit. Results come from routine, coaching and time. An AI subscription is the membership. Clean data, trained staff, changed workflows and regular checks are the routine that produces results.

Let's see this in practice. Aroha Tane is director of customer services at a fictional regional council in New Zealand. She wants an AI tool to summarise and sort thousands of public comments on local plans. The subscription is modest. So she asks her team to list the hidden costs.

Data first. Comments arrive by email, web form and paper letter, so letters must be scanned. Comments include names and addresses, so personal details must be removed, or the tool must be approved for this data. That is several weeks of one person's time.

People next. Five officers need training on how to read the summaries, and when to go back to the original comments. Two are worried their role will disappear, so Aroha explains that their work will move to checking and deeper analysis.

For process, the report template changes, and an officer checks a sample of comments against each summary. For oversight, a senior officer reviews whether any group's views were missed, for example comments written in te reo Māori.

Aroha estimates that data and oversight together will cost more than the subscription in the first year. She still approves the project, because it will free officers for deeper work. But her budget is now honest. That avoids a common mistake: comparing the subscription with salary costs, calling it cheap, and then stopping a useful tool too soon.

Let's recap. First, the licence or subscription is often only a small part of the total cost of an AI project. Second, hidden costs sit in four areas: data, people, process and oversight. Third, before you approve a project, ask for estimates in staff time and money under all four headings.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will take one AI idea, perhaps from your task list in the last lesson. You will list its hidden costs under the four headings, estimate staff days, and mark the largest. In the next lesson, we cover finding and prioritising use cases. See you there.
```

## L04 Finding and Prioritising Use Cases

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (680 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
After a workshop, your managers hand you twenty AI ideas. All of them sound promising. You have budget and attention for two. How do you choose, without simply picking the idea from the loudest person in the room?

In the last lesson, we saw the real costs of AI. Now let's decide which ideas are worth them. A simple scoring method makes the choice clear and fair. You score each use case on two dimensions.

The first is business value. If this works, how much does it matter? Score it from one, low, to five, high. Think about time saved, quality improved, cost or risk reduced, and a better customer experience. Also ask how closely it supports your strategy.

The second is feasibility. How easy is it to deliver safely? Score four factors. Is the data available and allowed? How serious is a mistake? How much effort is needed? And do we have the skills? For every factor, a higher score is always better. So for risk and effort, five means low risk or low effort.

Here is the CertifAI scoring template. Each row is a use case. You score value, then data, risk, effort and skills, each from one to five. Average the four feasibility factors, and the last column tells you which box it falls into. You can copy it into any spreadsheet.

Now place each idea on a two by two matrix. High value and high feasibility are quick wins, so start there. High value but low feasibility are strategic projects. Plan them, and fix the data or skills gaps first. Low value but easy ideas are small improvements. And low on both, you avoid.

Think of planning a family trip on a fixed budget. Some places need visas and months of planning. Others are close and still enjoyable. You take the easy trip now and save for the big one. Remember, scores are judgements, not facts. They make your reasons visible.

Let's see it in action. Mei Ling is operations director of a fictional hotel group in Malaysia, with twelve properties. Her managers suggest six ideas, and she scores them with her team.

Drafting replies to guest reviews scores four for value and four and a half for feasibility, because staff check every draft and no personal data is needed. Summarising maintenance reports is also a quick win. Translating staff notices is easy but low value, so it is a small improvement.

Forecasting room demand has the highest value, five. But the booking data sits in three systems with different formats, so data scores only two. Face recognition at check-in scores low on risk, because of privacy and guest trust. She avoids it.

Personalised offers based on guest history also have high value, four. But feasibility is only two. So this idea is strategic too, and it waits until the basics are in place.

Notice how the scoring worked in the room. Each score came with a short reason, so managers could challenge it. The loudest voice no longer decides. The reasons do.

Mei Ling chooses the two quick wins, review replies and maintenance summaries, to build skills and trust. She picks demand forecasting as the longer-term project, and starts cleaning the data now. That avoids a common mistake: choosing the highest-value idea first, stalling on data, and deciding that AI does not work here.

Let's recap. First, score each use case on business value and on feasibility, which covers data, risk, effort and skills. Second, use the two by two matrix to find quick wins, strategic projects, small improvements and ideas to avoid. Third, start with one or two quick wins, and prepare one longer-term project.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will copy the CertifAI scoring template and score at least five use cases for your organisation. You can use your task list from lesson two. Then choose your top two, and ask a colleague to challenge one score. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at assessing your AI readiness. See you there.
```

## L05 Assessing Your AI Readiness

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (676 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Two organisations buy the same AI tool. Three months later, one is using it well. The other has stopped. The tool was the same, so the difference was the organisation. How ready is yours?

In the last lesson, you chose your top use cases. Now let's check whether your organisation is ready for them. AI readiness is how prepared your team is to adopt AI safely and usefully. It has five parts.

Data. Is the data you need accurate, accessible and allowed to be used? Skills. Can staff use AI tools, check their outputs and see their limits? Is there someone who can manage vendors and technical questions? Culture. Do people feel safe to try new tools and report problems?

Leadership support. Do senior leaders give time, budget and clear priorities? Do they speak about AI in a consistent way? And governance. Are there rules for which tools staff may use, what data goes into them, who owns each system, and how decisions are reviewed?

Here is the CertifAI AI Readiness Checklist. For each part, read the statement and score one if it is not true at all, three if it is partly true, and five if it is fully true. Be honest. A high score that is not true leads you to projects you cannot deliver.

Readiness does not stop you from starting. It changes where you start. Low on data? Begin with drafting tasks that use public or low-risk information. Low on skills? Choose simple tools with a human check, and train first. Low on governance? Write a simple acceptable-use policy before any pilot.

Low on culture? Start with a task your staff dislike, and involve them in the choice. Low on leadership support? Start small, measure carefully and build evidence.

Think of a health check before training for a long race. It does not tell you to stay at home. It tells you whether to begin with short runs or long ones, and which weakness to work on first.

Let's see it in practice. Lucía Quispe is executive director of a fictional non-profit in Peru that runs clean-water projects in rural areas. She scores her team of thirty people.

Data scores two, because project reports come in many formats, and some are only on paper. Skills score three. Several staff use free AI chat tools for drafting, but nobody has been trained. Culture scores four, because staff are open to new tools. Leadership support also scores four. The board has asked for better use of staff time.

But governance scores only one. There are no rules on AI tool use, and staff sometimes paste community members' names into free tools.

Lucía had planned to analyse all past project reports with AI. With these scores, she changes her plan. She starts with drafting donor reports from summary notes that contain no personal data. This task needs little data and suits her staff's skills.

Her one action for the lowest area is a one-page AI use policy, written within a month. It includes a clear rule: no personal or confidential data in AI tools. Digitising the old paper reports becomes a longer-term goal.

Notice the common mistake she avoided. Her average score is close to three, which feels reassuring. But an average hides the weakest area, and the weakest area is where projects fail. Always look at the lowest score first.

Let's recap. First, AI readiness has five parts: data, skills, culture, leadership support and governance. Second, rate each part honestly from one to five, and focus on the lowest score, not the average. Third, weak areas change which use cases you start with. They do not mean you cannot start.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will complete the CertifAI readiness checklist for your team, with evidence for each score. Ask a colleague to score it too, then write one action for your lowest area, with an owner and a date. That completes week one. Next week begins with AI risks every leader must manage. See you there.
```
