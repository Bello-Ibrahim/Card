## L06 AI Risks Every Leader Must Manage

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (684 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
An AI tool saves your team many hours a week. Then one wrong output reaches a customer, a regulator or a journalist. Suddenly the conversation is not about time saved, but about trust lost. Which risks should you manage before that day comes?

Welcome to week two. Last week, you found and prioritised your AI opportunities. Now we manage the risks. AI risk is manageable when you name it clearly, rate it and give it an owner. Six categories cover most situations.

The first is wrong or invented outputs. AI can give inaccurate predictions. Generative AI can invent facts, sources or numbers with confidence. The control is human review for anything that reaches customers or affects decisions, plus regular sample checks.

The second is bias and unfair treatment. A model trained on past decisions can repeat past unfairness towards certain groups. The control is to test results across groups before and after launch, and keep a person responsible for final decisions about people.

Third, privacy and data leaks. Staff may paste confidential data into tools that store or reuse it. Control it with clear data rules, approved business accounts and training. Fourth, security. Hidden instructions in a document or email can change a tool's behaviour. Limit what each tool can access, involve IT security and keep logs.

Fifth, legal and contractual exposure. AI use can conflict with data protection, employment or consumer rules, or with client contracts. These rules differ by country and change over time, so ask your legal team to review high-impact uses before launch.

Sixth, damage to reputation. Even a legal and accurate use can upset people if it feels hidden or unfair. The control is to be open about where you use AI, and to offer a way to reach a person.

Think of a building site. You do not stop building. You wear helmets, fence off dangerous areas and name a safety officer. Each hazard has a matching control, and a person who checks it. AI risk works the same way.

The tool for this is a risk register. It is a simple table. It lists each risk, rates likelihood and impact from one to five, and names a control and an owner. Multiply likelihood by impact to see which risks need attention first.

Here is an example. Priya Sandhu is HR director at a fictional engineering company in Canada. Her team wants an AI tool that screens job applications and ranks candidates. Before any pilot, she builds a risk register.

Her register has six risks. The tool might rank strong candidates low because of unusual CV formats. Applicant data might be reused by the vendor. Hidden text in a CV might change the ranking. Candidates might feel judged by a machine. Each risk gets a control and an owner, from the recruitment lead to legal counsel.

The highest score is bias, three times five, fifteen. The tool could favour groups that were hired more often in the past. So Priya sets a condition for the pilot. The tool may only sort applications for recruiters to review. It may not reject anyone automatically.

Notice who owns that risk. It is Priya, the HR director, not the IT team. That is the common mistake. Most AI risks are business risks, such as unfair treatment, broken trust and legal exposure. Technical staff help with controls, but a business leader must decide how much risk is acceptable.

Let's recap. First, the main AI risks are wrong outputs, bias, privacy, security, legal exposure and damage to reputation. Second, a risk register rates each risk on likelihood and impact, and names one control and one owner. Third, business leaders, not only technical teams, must own AI risks.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will take one of your top use cases from lesson four and build a risk register with at least five risks. Rate each one, and name a control and an owner by role. This register will feed into your capstone roadmap. In the next lesson, we look at governance, policy and regulation. See you there.
```

## L07 Governance, Policy and Regulation

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (671 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Some of your staff probably already use AI tools at work, with or without permission. So the question is not whether AI is in your organisation. The question is whether anyone has set the rules.

In the last lesson, we named the risks. Now let's set the rules that control them. AI governance is the set of rules, roles and reviews that keep AI use safe and in line with your goals. For most teams, it rests on three elements.

The first is clear rules for staff use of AI tools. An acceptable-use policy says which tools are approved, what data people may enter, and when they must check AI output. It also asks people to report anything harmful or clearly wrong. Keep it short, practical and in plain language.

The second is human oversight for important decisions. Decisions that affect people's jobs, money, health, rights or access to services need a named person who reviews the AI's suggestion and can change it. The more serious the impact, the stronger the review.

The third is a named owner for each AI system. The owner knows what the tool does, what data it uses, how it performs and when to review it. A simple list of your AI systems and their owners is one of the most useful governance tools you can create.

What about regulation? AI and data protection rules differ between countries, and they are changing. You do not need to memorise laws. You need to ask your legal team the right questions. Which rules apply where we operate? Does this use involve personal data? Does it affect decisions about people? What do our contracts say? And what must we document?

Think of the rules of the road. Traffic rules do not stop people from driving. They make it safe for many people to drive at the same time. A clear AI policy lets staff use AI with confidence, instead of guessing or hiding it.

Let's see governance in practice. Kwame Asante is managing partner of a fictional accountancy firm in Ghana, with forty-five staff. He learns that several staff use free AI chat tools to draft client letters. One has pasted a client's financial figures into a tool. This is exactly the privacy risk we saw in the last lesson.

Kwame does not ban AI. He asks an AI assistant to draft a one-page policy, using only a general description of the firm. The draft is useful, but too general. So he edits it.

He names one approved business account for client data, after his IT provider checks its data terms. Every client letter drafted with AI must be checked and signed by a qualified accountant. The operations manager owns the list of AI tools. And he asks the firm's legal adviser which rules apply to client data in AI tools.

He shares the policy in a staff meeting, explains the reasons behind each rule and invites questions. Staff say they feel more confident, because they now know what is allowed.

This avoids a common mistake: the total ban. Bans are hard to enforce, and staff often keep using personal accounts where you have no control. A clear policy with approved tools usually reduces risk more than a ban does. Kwame turned hidden use into safe, visible use.

Let's recap. First, good AI governance rests on clear rules for staff, human oversight for important decisions, and a named owner for each AI system. Second, an acceptable-use policy should be short, practical and specific about which data may go into which tools. Third, laws differ and change, so ask your legal team the right questions.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will use Claude or ChatGPT to draft a one-page acceptable-use policy for your team. Use general terms only, with no confidential information. Then edit every rule so it fits your organisation, and add three questions for your legal team. In the next lesson, we cover asking vendors the right questions. See you there.
```

## L08 Asking Vendors the Right Questions

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (685 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Every AI vendor demonstration looks impressive. The data is clean, the examples are well chosen, and the presenter knows exactly what to type. Your job is to find out what happens with your data, your staff and your contract after the demo ends.

Last time, we set the rules for AI inside your organisation. Now let's look outside, at the vendors who sell it. Vendor due diligence means asking structured questions before you buy, and getting the important answers in writing. Ask every vendor the same questions, so you can compare them fairly.

Here is the CertifAI Vendor Question List. It has fifteen questions in five groups. The first group is data. Where is our data stored? Is it used to train your models, can we switch that off, and is it in the contract? Who can access it, and how do we delete it?

The second group is accuracy and evidence. What accuracy can you show on data like ours, not only demo data? What are the known limits? Can we test it on our own sample first? The third group is security and compliance. Which standards do you meet, how do you prevent misuse, and how fast will you tell us about an incident?

The fourth group is cost and exit. How does pricing grow as our use grows? What costs are not in the quote? How can we leave, and how do we get our data back? The fifth group is support. Who fixes wrong outputs, and how will you tell us about model changes?

Listen for three warning signs. Answers that avoid the question. Claims without evidence. And a refusal to put important commitments in writing. When you hear one, ask a follow-up question.

Choosing an AI vendor is like hiring a senior employee. A confident interview is not enough. You check references, ask for examples of past work, agree terms in a contract and plan a trial period. The question list is your structured interview.

Let's see it in action. Ingrid Solberg is procurement director at a fictional public transport authority in Norway. Her team is comparing two vendors of an AI assistant that answers passenger questions. Both demos are strong, so the team asks all fifteen questions and records every answer.

Vendor A says conversations may be used to improve its models on the standard plan, but this can be switched off on the business plan. It offers a two-week test on the authority's own anonymised questions, shows current security evidence, and explains that pricing grows with the number of conversations.

Vendor B says the data is fully secure, but does not say where it is stored. It shows accuracy only on its own demo data. Its exit terms are unclear, and it will not confirm in writing whether data is used for training. That is all three warning signs.

Ingrid chooses Vendor A for a pilot. She lists four answers she needs in the contract: training opt-out, data location, incident notice time and data return on exit. And because pricing grows with use, finance models the cost at double the expected volume.

This avoids a common mistake: judging vendors mainly on the demo and the price. A demo shows the best case, not your case. The real differences are in data use, evidence on real data, cost at scale and exit terms, and these only appear when you ask directly.

Let's recap. First, ask every vendor the same structured questions about data, accuracy, security, cost, exit and support. Second, ask for evidence on data like yours, and test on your own sample before you commit. Third, get important answers in writing, especially about training on your data and how you can leave.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will ask Claude or ChatGPT to play a vendor pitching an AI product. Question it with at least ten questions from the list, and rate each answer clear, vague or missing. Remember not to paste any confidential data. In the next lesson, we look at running a pilot and measuring results. See you there.
```

## L09 Running a Pilot and Measuring Results

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M2_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (673 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Three months after launch, a manager says the new AI tool is going well. You ask how much time it saves, and how many errors it makes. Nobody knows, because nobody measured the work before. So, was it a success?

In the last lesson, you learned how to question vendors. Now let's test a use case properly. A pilot is a small, time-limited test of an AI use case in real work, designed to produce evidence for a decision. A good pilot has five parts.

First, a clear scope: one use case, one team and a limited set of tasks. A small scope keeps risk low, and makes results easier to understand. Second, a baseline. Measure how the work performs before the pilot starts, such as time per task, error rate and satisfaction. Without a baseline, you cannot prove any change.

Third, about three success measures, agreed before you start. Include one for value, such as time or cost. One for quality or risk, such as error rate. And one for people, such as staff or customer satisfaction. Set a target for each.

Fourth, a fixed duration, long enough for staff to learn the tool and for results to settle, and a decision date, in the calendar on day one. Fifth, a feedback plan, because staff who use the tool see problems first. On the decision date, you choose go, adjust or stop. And stopping is a valid result. Stopping a weak idea early saves money.

Think of a clinical trial for a new treatment. You record the patient's condition first, give the treatment for a fixed period, measure agreed outcomes, and then decide. You do not decide based on how the patient feels on day two.

Let's build one. Carlos Herrera is head of customer service at a fictional insurance company in Mexico. He wants to test an AI tool that drafts replies to customer emails about claims status. Agents edit and send every reply. The tool never sends anything automatically.

The scope is one team of twelve agents, claims-status emails only, in Spanish. For four weeks before the pilot, the team records the baseline: handling time per email, how often a customer must contact them again, and customer satisfaction.

His three measures are these. Handling time falls by an agreed target. The second-contact rate does not rise. And agents rate the tool at least three and a half out of five. Agents check every draft, and customer data is not used to train the vendor's models.

The pilot runs for eight weeks, with a decision meeting in week nine, and a short team check-in every Friday. At the meeting, handling time has improved and repeat contacts are stable. But agents say drafts are weak for complex claims. So Carlos decides to adjust. The tool stays for simple emails only.

Notice what Carlos did not do. He did not just ask agents whether they liked the tool. Opinions matter, but without a baseline and agreed measures, decisions depend on who argues best. Carlos also kept a shared log of weak drafts, so the feedback was evidence too.

Let's recap. First, a good pilot is small, time-limited and measured against a baseline taken before it starts. Second, agree about three success measures in advance, covering value, quality or risk, and people. Third, set a decision date for go, adjust or stop, and treat stopping as a valid result.

Now for your capstone. You will write a ninety-day AI adoption roadmap for your own team or organisation. It builds on your use-case scores, readiness check, risk register, policy and vendor questions. Today's pilot plan is step one. Step two, the full roadmap, comes in the last lesson.

So here is your exercise, below this video. Choose one or two of your priority use cases, and write a one-page pilot plan with a scope, a baseline, three success measures, a duration and a decision date. In the next and final lesson, we bring it all together in your ninety-day AI adoption roadmap. See you there.
```

## L10 Your 90-Day AI Adoption Roadmap

- **Filename:** `ai-04-ai-literacy-for-leaders-and-executives_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (689 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You now have use cases, a readiness score, a risk register, a policy draft, vendor questions and a pilot plan. On their own, they are six documents. Joined in the right order, they become a plan your team can follow from Monday.

In the last lesson, you wrote your pilot plan. In this final lesson, we turn all your work into a ninety-day roadmap, in three phases of thirty days each.

Days zero to thirty are for preparing. Name a sponsor and a project owner. Share the acceptable-use policy. Train the pilot team, check the data and take the baseline. And finish vendor due diligence and contract terms.

Days thirty-one to sixty are the pilot. Run it and measure it. Hold weekly check-ins, log problems and review risks against your register. Adjust small things quickly, but do not change the success measures.

Days sixty-one to ninety are for deciding and scaling. Review results against the baseline. Make the go, adjust or stop decision. Share what you learned, and plan the next step. Before you scale, confirm with your legal team that the wider use still fits the rules where you operate.

Here is the CertifAI roadmap template. It has one column for each phase. For every phase, you fill in six rows: goals, key actions, owners, risks and controls, success measures, and communication.

Bring your staff with you. Involve them in choosing and testing use cases. Be honest about how roles may change. Train people before you expect results. And give a clear way to raise concerns, then show that you act on them.

Then communicate at every phase. Say why you are doing this, what will happen, what it means for each group, and how people can give feedback. Tell the board what you will decide, and when. And explain risks openly, because hiding them damages trust later.

A roadmap is like a ship's voyage plan. You prepare the crew and supplies before you leave, check your position during the trip, and decide at the port whether to go further. The plan can change, but everyone knows the route and who is steering.

Here is a roadmap in action. Tran Minh Anh is managing director of a fictional food manufacturer in Vietnam, with two hundred staff. Her priority use case is AI support for forecasting weekly ingredient orders.

In the first thirty days, the supply chain manager owns the project. The IT manager cleans two years of order data, and the team completes the vendor questions. The main risk is poor data quality, so there is a data check before the pilot. The baseline of ordering errors is recorded.

In the pilot, buyers use the forecast as advice, and still place every order themselves. The risk is that buyers trust the forecast too much, so large differences are reviewed every week. The team measures ordering errors, waste and buyer satisfaction.

In the last phase, the leadership team reviews the results and decides to adjust and extend the pilot to a second factory. Minh Anh presents the results to all staff, and starts preparing the next use case.

Avoid one common mistake. Many roadmaps are just lists of technology to buy. A strong roadmap is about people, decisions and evidence. If yours has no owners or decision dates, it is a wish list, not a plan.

Let's recap. First, a ninety-day roadmap has three phases: prepare, pilot, and decide and scale. Second, every phase needs goals, owners, risks and controls, success measures and communication. Third, bringing staff with you is part of the plan, not an extra.

Your capstone roadmap is assessed on five criteria: use-case prioritisation, risks and controls, your pilot and vendor plan, the roadmap structure with owners and measures, and people and communication. The full rubric is on the course page.

Congratulations. You have completed AI Literacy for Leaders and Executives. Your final exercise is capstone step two. Use the template to write your ninety-day roadmap, check it against the rubric, and submit it. Describe people by role, with no confidential figures. You are now ready to lead AI in your organisation with confidence. Well done.
```
