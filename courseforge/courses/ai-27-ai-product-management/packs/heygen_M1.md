## L01 What Changes When the Product Is AI

- **Filename:** `ai-27-ai-product-management_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (709 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A search filter for hotels under one hundred dollars will never show a hotel at one hundred and forty. But an AI summary of reviews can say guests love the quiet rooms, when three guests complained about noise. Only one feature can be confidently wrong.

Hi, and welcome to AI Product Management. You already know how to discover, design and deliver a feature. So in this course, we focus only on what changes when the feature depends on an AI model.

Traditional software is deterministic. The same input gives the same output every time. If a filter shows the wrong hotels, there is a bug. An engineer fixes it, and it stays fixed.

AI features are probabilistic. A model gives the most likely answer, based on patterns it learned from data. Most answers are useful. Some are wrong, and a wrong answer often looks just as confident as a right one. The same input can even give a slightly different answer on a second run.

Three things follow for product work. First, quality is a rate, not a yes or no. You cannot say the summary works. You can say nine out of ten summaries on our test set were accurate. Second, quality depends on data. Missing languages or old information show up as product problems.

Third, model limits shape the design. You must design what the user sees when the model is wrong, unsure or unable to answer. That failure experience is part of the feature, not an edge case for later. And done now means the quality rate is high enough, with a plan to keep checking after launch.

Here is a simple way to picture it. A normal feature is like a calculator. It is always right, or it is broken.

An AI feature is like a talented new colleague. They are fast and often impressive. But they need clear instructions, they sometimes make mistakes with great confidence, and you check their work until you know where to trust them. You would not fire them for one mistake. And you would not let them sign contracts on their first day.

Let's see this in practice. Linh Nguyen is a product manager at a hypothetical hotel booking site in Vietnam. Her team is adding an AI summary of guest reviews to each hotel page, next to the existing price filter.

Linh builds a simple table for her team. How can each feature fail? The price filter fails when a bug shows the wrong hotels. The summary can invent a detail, miss a common complaint, or reflect only recent reviews. And the filter fails every time until it is fixed, while the summary fails for some share of hotels.

How do users notice? With the filter, the price on the page looks wrong. With the summary, users often notice only when they arrive at the hotel. So the product must respond differently. Link each claim to real reviews, label the summary as generated by AI, add a report button, and check a sample every week.

The table changes the conversation. The engineering lead had estimated two weeks, like any other feature. Now the team adds a set of test hotels to measure quality, the report button and a weekly review. Linh also sees reviews in Vietnamese, English and Korean, so she asks about quality for each language.

A common mistake is writing acceptance criteria like the summary is accurate. That cannot be tested as one yes or no. Write a rate on a defined test set instead, such as at least nine of ten summaries contain no invented claims. Then design what happens for the rest.

Let's recap. First, AI features are probabilistic. They give likely answers, and some are wrong while looking confident. Second, quality becomes a rate you measure on test data and keep watching after launch. Third, the experience for errors, such as sources, labels and fallbacks, is part of the feature from the start.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, compare a normal feature with an AI feature in a failure table, and find one AI failure that users would not notice. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at the AI capability menu. See you there.
```

## L02 The AI Capability Menu

- **Filename:** `ai-27-ai-product-management_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (700 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Let's add AI is not a product decision. Let's predict delivery time from past trips is. The first step from a vague idea to a real feature is naming the capability you need, and sometimes finding that you need none.

In the last lesson, we saw that AI features give likely answers. Today we look at the menu. Most AI features use one of six capability types. Knowing them helps you talk clearly with engineers, and choose the simplest option.

Prediction estimates a number or a future value from past data, such as a price, a time or a demand level. Ideally it gives a range. Classification puts an input into one of a fixed set of groups, such as urgent or normal. It gives a label, and often a confidence score.

Generation creates new content, such as text, images, code or audio. Large language models like Claude belong here. Because the content is new, it can contain invented details. Retrieval finds the most relevant existing items, such as help articles. It is often combined with generation, so the model answers from real sources instead of from memory.

Recommendation ranks items for a specific user, based on their behaviour and similar users. And agents are systems where a model plans steps and uses tools to finish a task, such as searching, filling a form and sending a message. They can do more, so they can also go wrong in more ways.

These types differ in risk. Prediction and classification have fixed output types, so they are easier to test. Generation and agents produce open outputs and actions, so testing and guardrails take more work. Keep this in mind when you estimate the work.

Think of a hardware shop. A saw, a drill and a power multi tool can all make a hole in wood. The multi tool can do the most, but it is heavier, more expensive and easier to use badly. A good builder picks the simplest tool that does the job. Agents and generation are the multi tools of AI.

And many problems need no AI at all. A fixed rule, a sort order or a good form may be clearer, cheaper and always correct. Use AI when the task needs judgement across many varied cases that people cannot write rules for.

Let's use the menu with three hypothetical teams. Priya Raman builds an agriculture app in India. Farmers ask whether they should sell their onions now or in two weeks. The team first imagined a chatbot.

But the real need is prediction: an expected price range for the next two weeks, from past market prices, season and local supply. A chatbot could explain it later. The value comes from the number and its range.

Emeka Obi runs a delivery service in Nigeria. Customers want to know when their parcel will arrive in Lagos traffic. That is prediction again. He also wants to sort complaints into late, damaged and wrong address. That is classification, a separate feature with its own data and tests.

Agnieszka Nowak runs an online home goods shop in Poland. Customers who bought this also liked is recommendation. A help assistant that answers from the shop's policy pages is retrieval plus generation. And an AI discount calculator? She drops it. Discounts follow fixed rules, so normal code is the right tool.

The common mistake is to start from the most exciting capability, often a chatbot or an agent, and then look for a problem. Start from the user task. Name the output the user needs, and let that output choose the capability.

Let's recap. First, the six types are prediction, classification, generation, retrieval, recommendation and agents, and each gives a different output. Second, generation and agents are flexible, but need more testing and guardrails. Third, choose the simplest capability that solves the problem, and remember that many ideas need a rule instead of AI.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, you will sort twelve product ideas by capability type on a FigJam board, and mark the ones that need no AI. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at finding AI opportunities in your product. See you there.
```

## L03 Finding AI Opportunities in Your Product

- **Filename:** `ai-27-ai-product-management_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (700 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask any team to find places for AI, and you will get a long list in an hour. Most of the list will be features nobody asked for. The skill is not generating ideas. It is finding the few that start from a real user problem.

Last time, we looked at the capability menu. Now, where do you use it? A strong AI opportunity usually has three ingredients at the same time.

First, a real user pain. Users already lose time, money or confidence at this step, and you can point to evidence, such as support tickets, funnel drop off or interview notes. Second, a repeated judgement task. Someone makes the same kind of decision again and again, across varied cases. Third, available data. Could you get examples of good and bad outcomes?

A useful method is to walk the user journey. At each step, ask where users struggle, where they wait, where they make a judgement, and where your staff do repetitive reviewing. Mark those points, then check each one against the three ingredients. If the task is rare, or if a simple rule can decide it, AI adds little.

Also look behind the product. Some of the best opportunities are internal, helping support staff or moderators with repeated judgement tasks. These often carry lower risk, because a trained employee sees the output before any customer does.

Think of a good doctor. A good doctor does not start with the newest medicine and look for a patient. They start with the symptoms, find the cause, and then choose a treatment. AI is one possible treatment. The user pain is the symptom, and the journey walk is the examination.

The common trap is solution first thinking: adding AI because it is popular, or because leadership asked for it. When you hear we need AI, translate it into a question. Which user problem, at which step, would this solve? These features are hard to measure, because nobody defined the problem they solve.

Let's see an example. Camila Rocha is a PM at a hypothetical language learning app in Brazil. Learners study English and Spanish on their phones, and leadership wants more AI in the product. So Camila starts with the learners, not with the technology.

Camila walks the journey with her team: sign up, placement test, daily lessons, speaking practice, reviewing mistakes and renewing. At each step, she collects evidence from support tickets, app reviews and interview notes.

Three ideas pass the check. Feedback on spoken answers, because learners do not know if their speaking is correct, although consent to use recordings must be checked. Explanations of each mistake, because many tickets ask why is this wrong. And a review plan based on past mistakes, because learners forget old words.

Two ideas are removed. AI written push notifications have no clear user problem. And an AI mascot that chats about the weather has no evidence of pain at all. The mascot was leadership's favourite idea. Neither idea connects to a measurable learner problem.

Camila does not argue about taste. She shows that no user evidence supports it, and that each kept idea connects to a measurable problem. The discussion moves from do we have AI, to which learner problem do we solve first.

A common mistake is judging an opportunity by how impressive the demo would be. Start every description with the user and the pain, before you name any AI capability. An impressive demo of a feature that solves no real problem still fails.

Let's recap. First, a strong opportunity combines a real user pain, a repeated judgement task and data you can get. Second, walk the user journey and internal workflows to find judgement points. Third, translate we need AI into which user problem at which step, and remove ideas with no evidence of pain.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, ask Claude for ten AI opportunities for a product you know, then remove any that do not start from a real user problem. It takes about twenty five minutes. In the next lesson, we score your opportunities on value, feasibility and risk. See you there. Save your sheet, because you will need it next time.
```

## L04 Scoring Opportunities: Value, Feasibility and Risk

- **Filename:** `ai-27-ai-product-management_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (690 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Two AI ideas can promise the same value. One costs staff a few minutes when it is wrong. The other pays money to the wrong person when it is wrong. A good scoring method sees that difference before anyone writes code.

Last time, you found opportunities that start from real user pain. Now you need to choose. You already know how to prioritise a backlog. For AI features, add one dimension that normal scoring often ignores: the cost of errors.

Score each idea from one to five on three dimensions. Value: how much it helps users and the business. Feasibility: do you have the data, the skills and a realistic technical path? And error cost: how bad is a wrong output? One means a user loses a few seconds. Five means financial, legal, safety or serious trust harm.

Because a high error cost is bad, flip it before you add. The total is value plus feasibility plus six minus error cost, with a maximum of fifteen. Keep the formula visible and the same for every idea, and write one sentence of reasoning next to each score. You can weight value more if your team prefers.

The numbers are not precise. Their purpose is to make the team state its assumptions, and argue about the right thing.

The most useful insight comes from ideas with high value and high error cost. Do not simply drop them. Ask whether a narrower scope lowers the error cost. Suggest instead of decide. Keep a human in the loop. Limit it to low stakes cases, or help staff instead of customers. Then score the narrow version as a new row.

Think of an investment committee. It does not only ask how much a project could earn. It also asks what could go wrong, and how much it would lose. A risky project might still be approved, but with a smaller first investment. Narrowing an AI feature is that smaller first investment.

Let's see it in action. Youssef Amrani is a PM at a hypothetical car insurance company in Morocco. His team has five AI ideas for the claims journey, and they score each one. In insurance, a wrong output can cost real money.

A plain language summary of claim status scores thirteen. A checklist of missing documents also scores thirteen. Both use data the company already has, and a wrong output is easy to notice and correct. Flagging possible fraud for human reviewers scores ten.

Automatically approving small claims has the highest value, but a very high error cost. A wrong approval pays money, and a wrong rejection harms a customer. It scores nine. So Youssef writes a narrower version: suggest approve or refer, and a claims officer confirms every decision.

The narrow version scores four, three and two, for a total of eleven. It is now a serious candidate, and it creates labelled data from officer decisions that could support a wider version later. That is a lower risk first step.

Repair cost from photos scores only eight, because the company has few labelled photos. Youssef notes it as a data problem to revisit, not a dead idea. His top three are the status summary, the checklist and the narrow approval suggestion, each with one sentence of reasoning.

A common mistake is scoring only value and feasibility, so the most dangerous ideas rise to the top. Always score error cost separately, and test a narrower scope first. Others make the opposite mistake, and give up on any high risk idea.

Let's recap. First, score AI opportunities on value, feasibility and error cost, with the same formula for every idea. Second, feasibility depends mostly on data. An idea without examples is not ready, however valuable. Third, for high value, high risk ideas, score a narrower version with human confirmation before you decide.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, score your ideas from the last lesson in a Google Sheets matrix and choose your top three, with one sentence of reasoning each. It takes about twenty five minutes. In the next lesson, we look at scoping an MVP AI feature. See you there.
```

## L05 Scoping an MVP AI Feature

- **Filename:** `ai-27-ai-product-management_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (713 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Every AI feature has two user journeys: the one where the AI is right, and the one where it is wrong. Most teams design the first one carefully, and discover the second one after launch. Today, we design both on day one.

You now have a top idea. The next step is to scope it. An AI minimum viable product should be narrow, and four limits help. In the last lesson, a narrower scope lowered the error cost. Now we make that concrete.

One user: a single group with a clear need, such as new sellers, not all users. One task: one judgement task done well. A human in the loop: in the first version, a person approves the output before it has an effect. And a clear fallback: the product must still work when the AI is unsure, fails or is switched off.

Then design the experience for errors as carefully as the happy path. Ask four questions. When the AI is wrong, how does the user notice and correct it? When it is unsure, what does the user see? When it should not answer, what message appears? And when it is unavailable, what is the normal path?

For the unsure case, your team can often use a confidence score or a set of rules to decide when to show nothing at all.

Also design a feedback loop: a simple way for users to say this was helpful, or this was wrong. This becomes your first real evaluation data. Keep it light, with one tap, so users actually use it.

Here is a way to picture it. A new pilot does not fly passengers alone on the first day. They fly one route, with a senior pilot next to them, and they practise emergency procedures before normal flights.

An AI MVP flies one route, with a human next to it, and its emergency procedures are designed before launch.

Let's see an example. Nour Hassan is a PM at a hypothetical online marketplace in Egypt, where people sell used furniture and electronics. Sellers get many buyer questions, such as can you deliver to Giza, and slow replies lose sales.

The first idea was an AI assistant that handles all buyer conversations. Nour narrows it. The user is sellers with fewer than twenty listings, who reply from their phones. The task is to suggest up to three short reply drafts, in Arabic or English to match the buyer. The seller taps, edits and sends. Nothing is sent automatically.

Now the failure paths. Wrong: a suggestion makes a delivery promise the seller never made. So suggestions never include prices, dates or promises unless they come from the listing. Unsure: the message is unclear. So show one neutral suggestion, asking the buyer to say more, or show none.

Should not answer: the message contains abuse or asks for personal data. So there are no suggestions, and a report this message link appears. Unavailable: the normal reply box appears, with no error message for the seller. In each case, the seller always knows what to do next.

Every suggestion has a small not useful button. Nour will use those taps, and whether sellers edit suggestions or send them unchanged, as early quality signals. These signals cost almost nothing to collect, and they show real use from the first day.

A common mistake is thinking a human in the loop makes errors unimportant. Busy users stop checking carefully when suggestions are usually right. So make errors easy to see. For example, keep promises and numbers out of the drafts, and do not rely only on the user's attention.

Let's recap. First, scope an AI MVP to one user, one task, a human in the loop and a clear fallback. Second, design four failure paths: wrong, unsure, should not answer and unavailable. Third, build a simple feedback signal into the first version, because it becomes your first real evaluation data.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, sketch the happy path and the four failure paths for your feature in Figma or FigJam. Keep the board, because you will reuse it in the capstone. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at data needs, and what your feature learns from. See you there.
```
