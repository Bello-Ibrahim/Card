# L01 What Changes When the Product Is AI | Presenter Script

Course: AI-27 · Video: 5 min · Words: 709

## Hook
A search filter for hotels under one hundred dollars will never show a hotel at one hundred and forty. But an AI summary of reviews can say guests love the quiet rooms, when three guests complained about noise. Only one feature can be confidently wrong.

## Explain
Hi, and welcome to AI Product Management. You already know how to discover, design and deliver a feature. So in this course, we focus only on what changes when the feature depends on an AI model.

Traditional software is deterministic. The same input gives the same output every time. If a filter shows the wrong hotels, there is a bug. An engineer fixes it, and it stays fixed.

AI features are probabilistic. A model gives the most likely answer, based on patterns it learned from data. Most answers are useful. Some are wrong, and a wrong answer often looks just as confident as a right one. The same input can even give a slightly different answer on a second run.

Three things follow for product work. First, quality is a rate, not a yes or no. You cannot say the summary works. You can say nine out of ten summaries on our test set were accurate. Second, quality depends on data. Missing languages or old information show up as product problems.

Third, model limits shape the design. You must design what the user sees when the model is wrong, unsure or unable to answer. That failure experience is part of the feature, not an edge case for later. And done now means the quality rate is high enough, with a plan to keep checking after launch.

Here is a simple way to picture it. A normal feature is like a calculator. It is always right, or it is broken.

An AI feature is like a talented new colleague. They are fast and often impressive. But they need clear instructions, they sometimes make mistakes with great confidence, and you check their work until you know where to trust them. You would not fire them for one mistake. And you would not let them sign contracts on their first day.

## Demonstrate
Let's see this in practice. Linh Nguyen is a product manager at a hypothetical hotel booking site in Vietnam. Her team is adding an AI summary of guest reviews to each hotel page, next to the existing price filter.

Linh builds a simple table for her team. How can each feature fail? The price filter fails when a bug shows the wrong hotels. The summary can invent a detail, miss a common complaint, or reflect only recent reviews. And the filter fails every time until it is fixed, while the summary fails for some share of hotels.

How do users notice? With the filter, the price on the page looks wrong. With the summary, users often notice only when they arrive at the hotel. So the product must respond differently. Link each claim to real reviews, label the summary as generated by AI, add a report button, and check a sample every week.

The table changes the conversation. The engineering lead had estimated two weeks, like any other feature. Now the team adds a set of test hotels to measure quality, the report button and a weekly review. Linh also sees reviews in Vietnamese, English and Korean, so she asks about quality for each language.

A common mistake is writing acceptance criteria like the summary is accurate. That cannot be tested as one yes or no. Write a rate on a defined test set instead, such as at least nine of ten summaries contain no invented claims. Then design what happens for the rest.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, AI features are probabilistic. They give likely answers, and some are wrong while looking confident. Second, quality becomes a rate you measure on test data and keep watching after launch. Third, the experience for errors, such as sources, labels and fallbacks, is part of the feature from the start.
