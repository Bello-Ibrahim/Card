# L01 What Changes When the Product Is AI

Course: AI-27 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
A search filter that shows "hotels under 100 dollars" will never show a hotel at 140 dollars. An AI summary of hotel reviews can say "guests love the quiet rooms" when three guests complained about noise. Both features look simple on screen. Only one of them can be confidently wrong, and that changes almost everything about how you manage it.

## Explanation
You already know how to discover, design and deliver a feature. This lesson is about what changes when the feature depends on an AI model.

**Traditional software is deterministic.** The same input gives the same output every time. If a filter shows the wrong hotels, there is a bug, an engineer finds it and fixes it, and it stays fixed.

**AI features are probabilistic.** A model gives the most likely answer based on patterns it learned from data. Most answers are useful. Some are wrong, and a wrong answer often looks just as confident as a right one. The same input can even give a slightly different output on a second run.

Three consequences follow for product work:

1. **Quality is a rate, not a yes-or-no.** You cannot say "the summary works". You can say "on our test set, 9 out of 10 summaries were accurate and none contained harmful content". You will learn to measure this in Module 2.
2. **Quality depends on data.** The model can only reflect what it learned from and what you give it at the moment of use. Missing languages, old information or biased examples show up as product problems.
3. **Model limits shape the design.** You must design what the user sees when the model is wrong, unsure or unable to answer. That failure experience is part of the feature, not an edge case to handle later.

This also changes delivery. With a normal feature, "done" means the code passes its tests. With an AI feature, "done" means the quality rate is high enough for this use, and you have a plan to keep checking it after launch, because inputs change over time.

**Analogy:** A normal feature is like a calculator. It is always right, or it is broken. An AI feature is like a talented new colleague. They are fast and often impressive, but they need clear instructions, they sometimes make mistakes with great confidence, and you check their work until you know where you can trust them. Nobody would fire the colleague for one mistake, and nobody would let them sign contracts on their first day.

For user trust, this means three design habits: show users where an answer came from when you can, make it easy to correct or ignore the AI, and never hide that content was generated.

## Worked Example
Linh Nguyen is a product manager at a hypothetical hotel booking site in Vietnam. Her team is adding an AI-generated summary of guest reviews to each hotel page. The existing price filter sits on the same page.

Linh builds a comparison table for her team:

| | Price filter | AI review summary |
|---|---|---|
| How it can fail | A bug shows hotels outside the price range | Summary invents a detail, misses a common complaint, or reflects only recent reviews |
| How often | Rarely, and every time until fixed | Some share of summaries, which changes with the reviews |
| How users notice | Price on the page does not match the filter | Often they do not notice until they arrive at the hotel |
| What the product should do | Fix the bug; add an automated test | Link each claim to real reviews, show "Summary generated by AI", let users report a wrong summary, and check a sample of summaries every week |

The table changes the conversation. The engineering lead had estimated the summary as "two weeks, like any other feature". After the table, the team adds work that a filter never needed: a set of test hotels to measure summary quality, a report button and a weekly review of samples. Linh also notices that some hotels have reviews in Vietnamese, English and Korean, so she adds "summary quality for each language" as a question for the team.

## Common Mistake
Many PMs treat the AI part as something that engineering will simply "make work", and they write acceptance criteria such as "the summary is accurate". That cannot be tested as a single yes-or-no. Instead, write criteria as rates on a defined test set, such as "at least 9 of 10 summaries in our test set contain no invented claims", and design what happens for the rest.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI features are probabilistic: they give likely answers, and some answers are wrong while looking confident.
2. Quality becomes a rate you measure on test data and keep watching after launch, and it depends on the data behind the model.
3. The experience for errors, such as sources, labels, corrections and fallbacks, is part of the feature and must be designed from the start.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Compare a deterministic feature with an AI feature in a failure table.
**Tools:** Google Sheets or any document; optional: Claude (free plan) to challenge your table [VERSION].
**Steps:**
1. Choose a product you know. Pick one normal feature (such as a search filter) and one AI feature (such as an AI-generated summary), real or imagined.
2. Make a table with four rows: how it can fail, how often, how users would notice, and what the product should do.
3. Fill in both columns. For the AI feature, list at least three different ways it can fail.
4. Optional: paste your table into Claude and ask, "What failure modes of the AI feature are missing?" Do not paste confidential product details or user data.
5. Circle one AI failure that users would not notice. Write one design idea that would make it visible.
**What good looks like:** The AI column shows several different failure types (invented details, missing information, uneven quality across languages or users), a realistic view of how users discover them, and concrete product responses such as sources, labels, report buttons and regular sampling.
