# L02 AI in Your Day

Course: AI-01 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O5 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Before lunch today, you may already have used five or more AI systems without noticing any of them. In this lesson we follow one ordinary day and stop each time AI is working in the background.

## Explanation
In L01 we saw that machine learning systems learn patterns from examples. To spot them in daily life, ask two questions about any feature:

1. **What does it predict?** Almost every machine learning feature makes a guess about something it cannot know for certain. For example: "Is this email spam?" or "Which song will this person enjoy next?"
2. **What data did it probably learn from?** To make that guess, the system needed many past examples. We usually cannot see this data, but we can make a sensible guess.

Here are six common features.

- **Spam filter.** It predicts whether a new email is spam or not. It probably learned from very large numbers of emails that users marked as "spam" or "not spam".
- **Video and music recommendations.** They predict which item you are likely to watch or play next. They probably learned from what many people chose, skipped, finished or replayed.
- **Map travel times.** A map app predicts how long a trip will take right now. It probably learned from past trips on the same roads at different times of day, together with current traffic signals from other users.
- **Face unlock.** Your phone predicts whether the face in front of the camera is yours. It probably learned general face patterns from many images, and then stored a description of your face from the pictures taken during setup.
- **Translation.** A translation tool predicts the most likely sentence in the target language. It probably learned from very large collections of texts that already exist in two or more languages.
- **Voice assistants.** They predict which words you said, and then what you want. They probably learned from many recordings of speech that were matched with written text.

Notice the word "probably". Companies do not always explain what data they use, so our answers are informed guesses. The goal is the habit of asking the two questions.

**Analogy:** Think of a weather forecaster. They cannot see tomorrow, but they have studied years of past weather, so each morning they can say "70% chance of rain". Every AI feature in your day is a small forecaster: it looks at the present situation and guesses based on past examples.

## Worked Example
Rahul is a delivery driver in Pune, India. Here is part of his day, seen through the two questions.

At 7:00 he opens his email. Two messages about "free prizes" are already in the spam folder. **Prediction:** spam or not spam. **Data:** emails that other users have marked.

At 8:00 he checks his map app. It says the route to the warehouse will take 25 minutes, not the usual 15. **Prediction:** travel time. **Data:** past and current trips by many drivers on those roads.

At 12:30 a customer writes to him in Tamil, which he does not read well, so he uses a translation tool. **Prediction:** the most likely English sentence. **Data:** texts that exist in both languages.

In the evening, a video app suggests a cricket highlights video. **Prediction:** what he will watch next. **Data:** his own viewing history and the choices of people with similar habits.

Sofía, a nurse in Valparaíso, Chile, has a different day: her keyboard suggests the next word in Spanish, and a music app builds a playlist for her night shift. The features change, but the pattern of "prediction plus past data" stays the same.

Some features may work differently or may not be available in every country or language. [REGION]

## Common Mistake
Many learners think that anything "smart" on a phone must be AI. An alarm that rings at 6:30 every day follows a fixed rule, and so does a calculator. A useful test: if the feature makes a guess that could be wrong and improves with more examples, it is probably machine learning. If it always gives the same output for the same input, it is probably not.

## Key Takeaways
1. You can spot machine learning in everyday products by asking two questions: what does it predict, and what data did it probably learn from?
2. Common examples include spam filters, recommendations, map travel times, face unlock, translation and voice assistants.
3. Not every automatic feature is AI. Fixed rules, like a timed alarm, do not learn from examples.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Keep an "AI diary" for one day. List 5 AI features you used and guess what each one predicts.
**Tools:** Pen and paper, or any notes app on your phone.
**Steps:**
1. Make a table with four columns: "Time", "Feature", "What it predicts" and "What data it probably learned from".
2. During one normal day, add a row each time you notice a feature that makes a guess. Look at email, maps, music, video, shopping, your keyboard and your camera.
3. Stop when you have at least 5 rows.
4. For each row, write one short sentence in the last two columns.
5. Cross out anything that follows a fixed rule, and replace it with a new feature.
**What good looks like:** Five different features, not five versions of the same one. Each prediction is written as a clear guess, such as "whether I will click this product". Each data guess names real past examples, such as "what other shoppers bought after viewing this item".
**Time:** about 20 minutes, spread across one day
