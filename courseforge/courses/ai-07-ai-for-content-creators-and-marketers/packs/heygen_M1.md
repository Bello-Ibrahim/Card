## L01 Where AI Fits in Content Work

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (736 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
It is Monday morning. Your list says three posts, one newsletter, two graphics and a short video, all by Friday. AI tools promise to help with all of it. But which tool helps with which job? And which jobs should stay with you?

Hi, and welcome to AI for Content Creators and Marketers. In this first lesson, we look at where AI fits in the work you already do, and where it does not.

Most content work follows the same six steps, for a large company or for your own small channel. Research, ideas, creation, editing, publishing, and finally measuring, where you look at results and decide what to change.

AI tools for content work fall into four main types. The first type is chat assistants, such as Claude or ChatGPT. They work with text. They are good at drafting, summarising, suggesting ideas and rewriting in a new tone. But they are weaker at facts. They can state wrong information in a very confident way.

The second type is design tools, such as Canva. They give you templates and layouts, and many now have AI features that suggest designs or remove backgrounds. They help you make consistent, branded graphics quickly.

The third type is image generators. They create new images from a written description, called a prompt. They are good for original illustrations and backgrounds, but they can struggle with text inside images, with hands and with exact details.

The fourth type is video editors, such as CapCut. They help you cut clips, add music and create automatic captions. They save time on the editing jobs you repeat every week.

No single tool covers the whole workflow. And none of them knows your brand, your customers or your goals unless you tell it. AI can speed up the middle of the work a lot. But the first decision, what to say, and the last decision, whether it is ready to publish, stay with you.

Here is a simple way to picture it. Think of AI as a kitchen assistant. The assistant can chop vegetables and wash dishes very quickly. But the chef still chooses the recipe and tastes the dish before it goes to the customer. If the chef stops tasting, a mistake reaches the table. In content work, you are the chef.

Let's look at an example. Yuki runs the social media channels for a small bakery in Osaka, Japan. She maps her weekly workflow, step by step.

For research, a chat assistant can group customer comments into themes. For ideas, it can suggest twenty post ideas from those themes. For creation, it can draft captions, and a design tool can help with graphics. For editing, it can suggest shorter versions. Scheduling features in her social apps can help with publishing. And for measuring, a chat assistant can summarise a small table of results.

But Yuki decides that one step stays fully human: the photos of the bread. Her customers follow the bakery because the photos are real. An AI loaf might look attractive, but it is not the bread they can buy. She also decides that she will read and approve every caption before it is posted.

Many beginners think AI is most useful for creation. They ask it to write a post, and they publish the result. In practice, AI often helps most with the slow, repeated parts around creation: grouping research, listing ideas, making variations and summarising results.

The opposite mistake is using AI for the step where your own knowledge matters most, such as your opinion, or your real product photos. So decide which steps need you before you choose a tool.

Let's recap. First, content work usually follows six steps: research, ideas, creation, editing, publishing and measuring. Second, the four main AI tool types are chat assistants, design tools, image generators and video editors, and each is good at different jobs. Third, AI can speed up many steps, but you decide what to say and whether it is ready to publish.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will map your own content workflow in six steps. You will write which type of AI tool could help at each step, and circle the one step you want to keep fully human. It takes about fifteen minutes. In the next lesson, Teaching AI Your Brand Voice, we will make AI sound like you. See you there.
```

## L02 Teaching AI Your Brand Voice

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (686 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask an AI assistant to write a post about your new coffee, and you get something like this. Discover the rich, bold flavour of our new blend. It is not wrong. It just sounds like everyone. And your brand is not everyone.

In the last lesson, we mapped where AI fits in your workflow. Now we make it sound like your brand. A chat assistant has read a huge amount of text. So without guidance, it writes in a safe, average style, the most common kind of marketing language.

To get text that sounds like you, give the assistant your brand voice as context. You do this with a brand voice guide. It is a short document, and one page is enough. It has four parts.

First, personality. Choose three to five adjectives, each with a short explanation, such as warm, not childish, or expert, not formal. Second, words to use, like the way you name your products or greet customers. Third, words to avoid, such as overused marketing words or claims you cannot prove.

Fourth, example posts. Add two or three real posts that sound exactly right. Examples teach style better than any description.

Paste the guide at the start of a conversation, or save it in the tool's project or custom instructions feature, if your plan has one. Then add your request after it. And only include information you are allowed to share. Never paste confidential plans or customer data into an AI tool.

Think of the voice guide as the notes you give a new team member on their first day. A talented writer still needs to know how your company speaks to customers. Without those notes, they write in their own general style. With them, their work starts to sound like the team.

Let's see it work. Valentina manages marketing for a sample coffee brand from a family farm in the hills near Medellín, in Colombia. The brand sells whole beans online.

First, she asks for an Instagram caption for a new washed coffee, with no guide. The answer says: elevate your mornings with our exquisite new blend, rich, smooth and bursting with flavour. It could be any coffee brand in the world.

Then she writes a short guide. Personality: warm like a family table, proud of the farm without boasting. Use: our farm, the harvest, the Suárez family. Avoid: elevate, exquisite, bursting with flavour, and health claims. She adds two past captions that followers liked.

She pastes the guide and repeats the request. The new caption says: this harvest, Don Álvaro tried something new, washing the beans before drying them. The result is a cleaner cup with notes of red apple and brown sugar. Small batch, from our farm to your kitchen.

She lists three differences. The second caption names a person, uses plain tasting notes and removes the empty words. But she still checks it. The AI invented red apple from general coffee knowledge, so she confirms the tasting notes with the farm. She also changes one phrase to match how Don Álvaro describes the process himself.

A common mistake is a guide that is only adjectives, like friendly, professional and fun. Almost every brand could use those words. Add clear use and avoid lists and, most important, real example posts. The second mistake is trusting the improved text completely. A better style does not mean correct facts.

Let's recap. First, without context, AI writes in an average style that could belong to any brand. Second, a one-page voice guide with personality, words to use, words to avoid and example posts helps AI match your brand. Third, text that sounds right can still contain invented details, so always review it for facts and voice.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, write a one-page voice guide for a real or sample brand. Then ask Claude or ChatGPT for the same post, once without the guide and once with it, and list three differences. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, Audience Research and Personas with AI, we learn who we are writing for. See you there.
```

## L03 Audience Research and Personas with AI

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (693 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask an AI assistant to describe your ideal customer. In ten seconds, you get a name, an age, a job, three hobbies and a favourite app. It looks like research. It is not. It is a well-written guess.

Last time, we taught AI your brand voice. Now we ask a different question. Who are you talking to? A persona is a short description of a typical member of your audience: who they are, what they need, what problems they have, and where they spend time online.

AI assistants can draft personas very quickly. You might ask for two audience personas for a small bicycle repair shop in a large city, with their main questions, problems and favourite channels. In seconds, you have a structure to work with.

But the AI does not know your customers. It combines general patterns from its training text. Some details will be reasonable. Some will be wrong for your market. And some will be stereotypes, simple and often unfair ideas about a group, such as assuming that all older customers avoid social media.

So treat every AI persona as a starting hypothesis. A hypothesis is a guess that you test. Test it against real sources. What do customers ask you in comments and messages? What do reviews praise or complain about? What do short surveys and your own analytics show? And what do staff who talk to customers every day already know?

As you check each detail, sort it into one of three groups: confirmed by real data, plausible but needs checking, or probably a stereotype. Then rewrite the persona from what you confirmed. And if you give AI real comments to summarise, remove names and personal details first.

Think of an AI persona like a sketch that a police artist draws from a short description. It helps you start looking. But you would never arrest someone based only on the sketch. You compare it with real evidence, and you correct it.

Let's see this in practice. Karim runs marketing for a sample bicycle repair shop in Casablanca, Morocco. He asks an AI assistant for two personas.

The first persona is Youssef, twenty-eight, a delivery rider who needs fast repairs, uses Instagram and wants low prices. Karim checks his sources. Fast repairs is confirmed, because many messages ask, can you fix it today? Delivery rider is plausible. The shop has some riders as customers, but Karim has no numbers.

Uses Instagram is partly wrong. His analytics and messages show that most repair requests come through a messaging app. And wants low prices is probably a stereotype. Reviews from riders talk about speed and reliability much more than price.

The second persona is a retired man who rides on weekends and does not use smartphones. This is mostly a stereotype. Several weekend riders in their sixties send photos of their bikes by phone before they book.

So Karim rewrites the first persona. Delivery riders and daily commuters who lose income when their bike is broken. Their main need is a same-day repair and a clear price before work starts. Their main channel is a messaging app. To check: what share of customers are delivery riders. He adds that question to a short customer survey.

The common mistake is copying the AI persona straight into a strategy document, because it looks detailed and professional. But detail is not evidence. A name and an exact age only make a guess feel real.

Let's recap. First, AI can draft personas quickly, but they are starting hypotheses, not research about your customers. Second, test each detail against real sources, such as comments, reviews, surveys and your own analytics. Third, label every detail as confirmed, needing checks or a probable stereotype, then rewrite the persona from the evidence.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, ask a free AI assistant for two personas for your brand. Mark every detail with one of the three labels, and rewrite one persona with a short list of things to check. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, Generating and Filtering Content Ideas, we turn these people into ideas worth making. See you there.
```

## L04 Generating and Filtering Content Ideas

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (688 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Getting forty content ideas from AI takes less than a minute. Choosing the eight that are worth making takes real thinking. This lesson is about both halves. And the second half is the one that matters.

You now have a voice guide and a persona. Let's use them to find ideas. AI assistants are very good at producing many ideas quickly. But the quality depends on the structure you give them. A vague prompt, like give me content ideas for my gym, returns ideas that any gym could post.

A structured prompt gives the AI a framework. Here are three useful ones. Content pillars are the three to five main themes your brand talks about. Formats are the types of content you can make, such as a short video, a carousel or a poll.

And customer questions are the real questions people ask you. Ask the AI to turn each one into a post idea. Then add your voice guide and persona, so the ideas fit your brand and your audience.

Then comes the filter. Score every idea from one to three on three criteria. Does it fit the brand? Is it useful to the audience? And is it possible to produce, with your time, budget, skills and permissions? Add the scores and keep the highest. When scores are equal, choose the idea that is most different from the rest, so your list has variety.

Think of AI ideas as a large catch from a fishing net. The net brings up many fish at once, but also weeds, stones and fish that are too small. Filtering is the sorting table, where you keep only what you can use.

Here is an example. Priya is the marketer for a sample fitness studio in Pune, India. The studio offers group classes for working adults. Her three pillars are short workouts, member stories, and rest and recovery.

She writes a structured prompt with her voice guide. She asks for forty ideas for busy working adults, grouped under her three pillars, each with a format. She also adds three real member questions. Can I join if I have never exercised? What should I eat before an evening class? And how do I avoid knee pain?

She gets forty ideas in a table and scores them. A ten-minute desk stretch video gets full marks, nine out of nine. A member story about a first class at forty-five gets eight. But celebrity workout secrets gets only three. It is off-brand, and it uses real people she has no permission to feature.

A meal plan for weight loss scores six. The audience wants it, but it is not possible to produce well. The studio has no nutrition expert, and diet advice without one could be unsafe. The knee pain question becomes a different idea: when to see a doctor about knee pain, with a trainer giving general tips.

Priya ends with a shortlist of eight ideas, across all three pillars and four formats. The list is short and varied, and every choice has a clear reason that she can explain to the studio owner.

The most common mistake is to skip the filter and post the first ideas in order. The first ideas are often the most general. And judging only by whether you like an idea is not enough. Clear scores make your choices easier to explain to a manager or client.

Let's recap. First, structured prompts using content pillars, formats and real customer questions give more useful ideas than vague prompts. Second, filter every idea on three criteria: fits the brand, useful to the audience, and possible to produce. Third, a short, varied, scored list is worth more than a long list of unfiltered ideas.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, generate thirty ideas for your brand with Claude or ChatGPT. Score each one on the three criteria, and keep your top eight, across at least two pillars and two formats. It takes about twenty-five minutes. In the next lesson, Rules of the Road: Copyright, Disclosure and Accuracy, we look at what to check before you publish. See you there.
```

## L05 Rules of the Road: Copyright, Disclosure and Accuracy

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (736 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You made a campaign image with AI in two minutes. Before you publish it, can you answer four questions? Who owns it? Are you allowed to use it for business? Should you say it was made with AI? And is everything in the post true?

In the last lesson, we filtered our ideas down to a strong shortlist. Before we create anything, let's set some rules of the road. This lesson teaches principles and questions, not legal rules. The law on AI and copyright is still developing, and it is different from country to country. When the stakes are high, such as a large paid campaign, ask your manager, your client or a legal adviser.

The first question is, who owns it? The answer can depend on where you are, and it is still being decided in many places. A practical principle is this. The more of your own creative work you add, such as writing, editing, selecting and arranging, the stronger your position is likely to be. And if you upload someone else's photo or text, you need the right to use it.

The second question is, does the tool's licence allow commercial use? Every AI tool has terms of use. They say what you may do with the outputs, and they can differ between free and paid plans. Read the current terms yourself, and write down the date you checked, because terms change.

The third question is, should you label it as made with AI? Check what your platforms and your market expect. Even where there is no rule, honesty protects trust. Many brands write a simple disclosure rule, for example: we label any image or video that is fully AI-generated and shows a realistic scene.

The fourth question is, is every fact and claim checked? AI can invent statistics, quotes, sources and product details. A false claim can mislead customers and damage their trust. So check every number, name, quote and claim against a source you trust.

And some rules never change. Never imitate a named artist or an existing brand. Never use real people, logos or copyrighted characters without permission. And never paste confidential or personal data into AI tools.

Publishing is like driving across a border. The basic habits are the same everywhere, but the exact rules change by country and over time. A careful driver checks the local rules before each trip.

Let's follow Tomás. He runs marketing for a sample language school in Buenos Aires, Argentina. He plans to use a chat assistant, a design tool and a free image generator.

He reads the current terms of each tool, and for each one he notes the plan, whether commercial use is allowed, any conditions, and the date. For one image generator, he cannot find a clear answer about commercial use on the free plan. So he marks it unclear, and does not use it for paid ads until he confirms.

Then he writes the school's disclosure rule. Any realistic image or video that is fully generated by AI is labelled, created with AI. Text drafted with AI and edited by the team does not need a label, unless a platform or local rule asks for one. He will review the rule every three months.

Finally, he checks a caption the AI wrote. It says students learn Spanish twice as fast with our method. The school has no evidence for this number. So he removes it.

A common mistake is to think, if the AI made it, it is free to use for anything. That is not safe. The opposite mistake is to avoid AI completely out of fear. The practical path is to check, record, add your own work, label where needed, and check every claim.

Let's recap. First, before publishing, ask four questions: who owns it, does the licence allow commercial use, should it be labelled, and is every claim checked. Second, these answers differ by country and platform and change over time, so check current sources and record the date. Third, a written disclosure rule and a fact-checking habit protect your audience's trust.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, check the current terms of three AI tools you plan to use, and write a short disclosure rule for your brand. It takes about twenty-five minutes. That completes week one. In the next lesson, Writing Social Posts and Captions, we start creating. See you there.
```
