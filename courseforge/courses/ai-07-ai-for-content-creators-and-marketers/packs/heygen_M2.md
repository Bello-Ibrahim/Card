## L06 Writing Social Posts and Captions

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (683 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A caption that works well on one platform can fail on another. One place rewards a long story. Another rewards a single sharp line. So why do so many people ask AI for a post, and then use the same text everywhere?

Welcome to week two, where we start creating. A good social post has four parts, and each one changes by platform. The first is length. Some platforms reward short text, and others allow longer stories. So check the current limits, and how much text shows before the reader taps more.

The second part is the hook, the first line, which decides whether people stop scrolling. It can be a question, a surprising detail or a clear benefit. The third is the call to action, what you want people to do next, such as comment with your favourite scent. The fourth is hashtags. Use a few relevant ones, never a long list of unrelated tags.

Put all of this in your prompt: the platform, the length, the type of hook, the call to action, the hashtag rule, and your voice guide. For example, an Instagram caption of about eighty words for a new lemongrass soap, with a sensory hook, a link in the profile, and three hashtags.

Then ask for several variations, such as a story, a list and a question. The first answer is rarely the best. Finally, make the post yours. Edit each one so that at least one sentence is fully your own, a real detail or an opinion that only your brand could share.

Asking for variations is like a photographer taking many shots of the same scene. Nobody expects the first photo to be the best. You take several, compare them and choose, and sometimes you combine the best parts.

Let's meet Wanjiru. She runs a sample handmade soap brand in Nairobi, Kenya, and she is launching a lemongrass and honey soap. Her voice guide says warm, local and honest. Use handmade in Nairobi, and ingredient names. Avoid miracle, cures and chemical-free.

She asks for three Instagram captions in three styles. The story style says: every Saturday, the lemongrass arrives from a small farm outside the city. By Monday, it is soap. The list style gives three things to know: fresh lemongrass, local honey, and small batches.

The question style begins: what does your morning smell like? Ours smells like lemongrass and honey. It ends by asking followers to share their favourite scent in the comments. Three styles give her real choices to compare.

Now Wanjiru checks the facts. The lemongrass does come from a farm outside the city, but it arrives on Fridays, not Saturdays. So she corrects it. One version also said the soap calms the skin. That is a health claim she cannot prove, so she removes it.

She chooses the story style for Instagram, and the list style for a short post on another platform. Then she adds her own sentence. My grandmother made lemongrass tea every morning, and this bar is for her. It is a real detail that keeps her human voice. No AI could have written that line.

The common mistake is accepting the first answer and posting the same text on every platform. Another is letting the AI add words like natural, healing or best in the city without evidence. Always ask for variations, adapt to each platform, and remove claims you cannot support.

Let's recap. First, good posts are shaped for each platform: length, hook, call to action and hashtags. Second, ask for several variations and choose or combine, instead of accepting the first answer. Third, add at least one sentence that is fully your own, and remove any claim you cannot prove.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, write five social posts for one product or service, using Claude or ChatGPT and your voice guide. In each post, write at least one sentence yourself and highlight it. Then check every fact before you post. It takes about twenty-five minutes. In the next lesson, Long-Form Content: Blogs and Newsletters, we go deeper. See you there.
```

## L07 Long-Form Content: Blogs and Newsletters

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (670 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
An AI assistant can write a six-hundred-word blog post in less than a minute. It will be correct and well organised, and very often forgettable. It may also contain a statistic that does not exist. Long-form content needs a different way of working.

In the last lesson, we wrote short social posts. Now we go longer. Blog posts and newsletters build trust over time. Readers come back because the writing is useful, and because they can hear a real person behind it. If you simply ask AI to write a blog post about a topic, you usually get general text that repeats other articles.

So build the piece step by step, with you in control. First, write your own outline: the title, the main point and three or four headings. Second, add your key points under each heading: examples, opinions, customer stories and facts you know are true.

Third, give the assistant your outline, notes and voice guide, and ask it to draft one section at a time. Fourth, edit it yourself. Rewrite the opening, cut repeated sentences, and add the experience, examples and opinions that only you have. That is what makes the piece original.

Then check every factual claim. AI assistants can invent statistics, quotes, studies and links that look real but are not. This is sometimes called hallucination. Highlight every number, name, date, quote and source. Find a source you can link to for each one. If you cannot, remove the claim, or rewrite it as your own opinion.

Writing with AI is like building a house with a fast construction team. You give them the plan, and they put up the walls quickly. But you decide where the windows go, you add the furniture that makes it a home, and you inspect every wall before anyone moves in.

Let's watch this in action. Anh writes a monthly newsletter for a sample small travel agency in Hanoi, Vietnam. This month, the topic is travelling in the rainy season.

First she writes her outline. The title is, why we love the rainy season, and how to plan for it. The four sections cover what the season is really like, three trips that are better in the rain, what to pack, and how the team changes plans when the weather turns.

Under each heading she adds notes. One is a story about a group who saw a waterfall at its fullest. Another is her team's rule: always keep one free afternoon in every rainy-season trip.

She asks the AI to draft each section. The draft is clear. But section one says that, according to tourism data, visitor numbers fall by forty percent in the rainy season. Anh cannot find any source for this number. So she deletes it, and writes: many of our clients avoid these months, so popular places are often quieter.

She also highlights a claim about average rainfall. She checks it against a national weather service, and links the source. Finally, she rewrites the opening with her own memory of her first rainy-season trip as a guide.

The most serious mistake is to trust numbers because they look precise. A made-up statistic with a decimal point looks more believable, not less. And if the outline is not yours, the piece will probably say nothing new.

Let's recap. First, build long-form content step by step: your outline, your key points, an AI draft, then human editing. Second, add your own experience, examples and opinions to make it original and useful. Third, highlight every factual claim and check it against a source you can link to, because AI can invent statistics, quotes and sources.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, draft a six-hundred-word blog post or newsletter with AI help, starting from your own outline. Highlight every factual claim, and link each one to a source or remove it. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, Designing Visuals with Canva and Free Image Generators, we move from words to pictures. See you there.
```

## L08 Designing Visuals with Canva and Free Image Generators

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (677 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
People often decide in one second whether to stop at your post, and the visual is usually what they see first. Today, you will watch three branded visuals being built on screen, one with an AI image, and a record of where every image came from.

Last time, we wrote long-form content. Now we design the pictures that go with it. We use two kinds of tool. Canva gives you templates and a brand kit, where you save your colours, fonts and logo. It also has AI features that can suggest designs or remove backgrounds. A free image generator, such as Google Gemini, creates original images from a written prompt.

A good image prompt has four parts. The subject, what is in the image. The style, such as a realistic photo in soft natural light. The colours, your brand colours in words. And the composition, such as a top view, with empty space on the left for text.

Some safety rules. Do not generate real people, real logos or copyrighted characters. Do not ask for the style of a named living artist. Do not upload photos of people without their consent. And record the tool and prompt for every AI image, so you can check licences and labels later.

Why does a brand kit matter? It is like a school uniform. Each student is different, but the uniform shows at once which school they belong to. Consistent colours and fonts do the same for your visuals.

Let's build it. Fatou designs visuals for a sample textile shop in Dakar, Senegal, that sells hand-dyed fabric. She opens Canva and goes to the brand kit. She adds two brand colours by their codes, one font for headings and one for body text.

She searches templates for Instagram post, and picks a simple layout with space for an image and a short headline. Then she applies her brand kit, and the template changes to her colours and fonts.

Now the image. In Google Gemini, she types her prompt: folded hand-dyed cotton fabric in deep indigo and teal patterns, on a light wooden shelf, soft morning light, realistic photo, with empty space at the top. Notice that the prompt covers all four parts.

She looks closely at the results. She rejects one where the patterns melt into each other, and one with an extra object. She chooses the best image, downloads it, and places it in her Canva template.

She adds the headline, new indigo collection, in the brand heading font. Then she zooms out, to check that the text is easy to read on a phone screen. She downloads the design.

Last, she opens her asset log, a simple spreadsheet. She writes the visual's name, the tool, the exact prompt, the date, the plan, and whether she has checked the licence.

She makes two more visuals in the same style: a quote card with a customer review, and a tip card on washing dyed fabric. The AI image for the tip card shows a label with letters that are not real words. So she removes the label, and adds real text in Canva.

The common mistake is to accept the first image without looking closely. AI images often contain errors, such as letters that are not real words, odd hands, or objects joined together. If an image shows your product, it must look like your real product, or customers may feel misled. Zoom in and check every detail.

Let's recap. First, Canva's brand kit and templates keep your colours, fonts and layout consistent. Second, a good image prompt describes subject, style, colours and composition. Third, avoid real people, logos, copyrighted characters and named artists' styles, and log the tool, prompt and licence check for every AI image.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, create three branded visuals in Canva, with at least one AI-generated image. Record the tool and prompt for each image, and check the licence terms. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, Short Video with CapCut, we add movement. See you there.
```

## L09 Short Video with CapCut

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M2_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (678 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Many people watch short videos on a phone, often with the sound off. They decide in the first two seconds whether to keep watching. So two things matter more than expensive equipment: a strong opening, and clear captions.

In the last lesson, we designed branded visuals. Now we make them move. A short video of about thirty seconds needs a simple plan. First, a hook in the first two seconds. Show the result first, ask a question or show movement. Then three scenes, each with one idea, such as problem, product in use, and result. And finally, a call to action, shown on screen and said aloud.

Ask Claude or ChatGPT to draft this plan as a script and shot list. That is a table with the time, what the camera shows, what is said, and the on-screen text. Give it your voice guide, the product and the platform. Then change any shot you cannot really film.

CapCut is a video editor for phone and computer. It has templates, simple effects and auto-captions, which turn speech into on-screen text. Auto-captions save time, but they make mistakes, especially with names, product words, accents and noisy places. Captions also make video accessible to people who are deaf or hard of hearing.

A short video is like a shop window seen from a moving bus. You have a few seconds. So the most interesting thing must be at the front, clearly lit, and easy to understand at a glance.

Let's make one. Mateus runs social media for a sample plant shop in São Paulo, Brazil. He asks an AI assistant for a thirty-second script and shot list for a vertical video on how to repot a small plant, with a hook, three scenes and a call to action.

One shot needs an outdoor garden, and the shop has none. So Mateus changes it to the shop counter. He films five short clips on his phone, vertically, near a window for good light. Then he opens CapCut, starts a new project and imports the clips in order.

He trims the first clip so the video opens on the most striking moment: roots coming out of a pot that is too small. It lasts less than two seconds. Then he trims each scene to match the script, and deletes the pauses.

Now the captions. He opens the captions feature and chooses auto-captions for the spoken language. Then he plays the video, and reads every caption. He finds two errors. The plant name is spelled wrong, and soil is written as soul. He corrects both by hand.

He chooses a high-contrast caption style, white text with a dark outline, and moves the captions up from the bottom edge, where app buttons may cover them. On the last scene he adds the call to action: visit us for free repotting advice.

He adds quiet background music from the editor's library, and checks its licence for commercial use. Finally, he exports the video in vertical format, and watches it once on a phone with the sound off.

The most common mistake is to trust auto-captions and publish without reading them. One wrong word can change the meaning. Another mistake is a slow start, such as a logo or a long hello. Put the best moment first, and the logo at the end.

Let's recap. First, plan a thirty-second video as a hook, three scenes and a call to action, and use AI to draft the script and shot list. Second, captions and a strong first two seconds matter on mobile, because many people watch without sound and scroll quickly. Third, auto-captions save time but make mistakes, so check them word by word.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, ask Claude or ChatGPT for a thirty-second script and shot list. Film it on your phone, edit it in CapCut with captions, and check every caption word by word. It takes about forty minutes. In the next lesson, Keeping a Human Voice: Editing and Reviewing AI Content, we bring it all together. See you there.
```

## L10 Keeping a Human Voice: Editing and Reviewing AI Content

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (678 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
In today's fast-paced world, it is more important than ever to. If you have read that sentence many times, you have already learned to spot unedited AI text. And so have your customers.

Over the last four lessons, you created posts, articles, visuals and video with AI. Now we make sure they still sound like you, and are safe to publish. Unedited AI text often has signs that readers notice, even if they cannot name them.

The first sign is generic openings, such as, unlock the power of. The second is repeated patterns, like the same sentence shape again and again, or every paragraph ending with a question. The third is claims without evidence, like proven, or loved by thousands. And the fourth is no specific details, nothing that could only come from your brand, your people or your place.

When readers notice these signs, trust can fall, because the content feels like it was made without care. So use a five-point review checklist on every piece. Is it accurate, with every fact checked? Is it on-brand, matching your voice guide? Is it original, with something only you could know, and no copying or imitating of another creator or brand?

Is it inclusive, with respectful language, no stereotypes, and captions or alt text so that different people can use it? And is it legally safe, with licences checked, permissions in place, disclosure that follows your rule, and no misleading claims? If a piece fails one point, fix it, or do not publish it.

Think of it this way. AI gives you clay. Clay has potential, but a lump of clay is not a finished pot. Your editing shapes it into something people recognise as yours. And your review is the firing that makes it strong enough to use.

Let's review a real draft. Hira manages content for a sample independent bookshop in Lahore, Pakistan. The shop is holding a poetry evening.

The AI draft says: in today's busy world, it is more important than ever to slow down. Join us for an unforgettable evening of poetry. Loved by thousands of readers, our events are the best in the city. Do not miss out.

Hira applies the checklist. Accurate fails, because loved by thousands has no evidence. On-brand fails, because the shop's voice is quiet, literary and welcoming, and the exclamation marks do not fit. Original fails, because nothing is specific to this evening.

Inclusive needs improving, because the post does not say whether the venue is step-free, and people often ask. And legally safe fails, because best in the city is a claim she cannot support.

Her rewrite says: on Thursday evening, three local poets will read new work in Urdu and English, between the shelves where many of them first found poetry. Free tea is served. The shop is step-free. Reserve a free seat through the link in our profile.

Hira explains her changes. She removed the generic opening and the claims she could not prove. She added the real details of the evening, and the access information. And she matched the calm voice of the shop.

A common mistake is to edit only grammar and spelling. AI text usually has few errors of that kind. The real problems are generic language, unsupported claims and missing human detail. And asking a tool to make it sound more human is not enough. The real fix is your own specific knowledge.

Let's recap. First, unedited AI text often shows generic openings, repeated patterns, claims without evidence and no specific details. Second, review every piece with the five-point checklist: accurate, on-brand, original, inclusive and legally safe. Third, the strongest edit adds specific human knowledge that only you or your brand have.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, review three pieces of AI-assisted content you made this week with the five-point checklist. Then rewrite the weakest one, and explain what you changed. It takes about thirty minutes. Next week, you build your capstone campaign. We start with the next lesson, Repurposing One Idea Across Channels. See you there.
```
