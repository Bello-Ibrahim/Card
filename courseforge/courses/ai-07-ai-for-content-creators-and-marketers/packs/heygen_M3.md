## L11 Repurposing One Idea Across Channels

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (681 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You spent a whole day making one excellent tutorial video. It gets posted once, and a week later, nobody sees it. What if that one day of work could fill a week of content, on four different channels?

Welcome to week three, where you build your campaign. We start with repurposing. Repurposing means turning one core piece of content into several new pieces for different channels. The core piece is usually the one that took the most work, such as a blog post, a video or a webinar.

But repurposing is not copying. Each channel has a different audience, format and length. Short-video platforms are vertical, fast and visual, with one idea per video. Image-led feeds want a strong visual or a carousel. Professional networks want a clear insight for colleagues and decision-makers.

Newsletter readers chose to hear from you, so they expect more depth and a personal tone. And blogs are long and searchable, with headings people can return to. When you paste the same text everywhere, it usually performs poorly. A caption written for a short video makes no sense in an email.

AI is very useful here. Give it the core content, such as a transcript, and ask for one specific new format. For example, turn this tutorial into a five-slide carousel, with one tip per slide and no more than twenty words each. Then edit each piece, and check that nothing new and untrue was added.

For every new piece, ask one question. What changes for this audience? Write the answer in one sentence. If the answer is nothing, you are copying, not repurposing.

Repurposing is like cooking one large pot of rice. From the same pot, you can make fried rice, a rice salad and rice pudding. The base is the same, but each dish is prepared differently, for a different meal and different people.

Let's see an example. Lucas is a content creator in Lisbon, Portugal. He teaches beginners how to edit photos on a phone. He recorded one twelve-minute tutorial called five edits that fix most phone photos. With AI's help, he turns it into seven pieces of content.

First, he asks the AI for a summary of his transcript with timestamps. Then he asks for each format separately. Three short vertical videos, one edit in each, with a hook in the first two seconds for people scrolling quickly. And a carousel of five slides with before and after images, for people who want a quick reference.

He writes a professional post about why small businesses should fix their product photos before paying for ads. That is a business angle, for owners. A newsletter section tells a personal story about his worst photo, for subscribers who already trust him. And a blog post with headings is searchable, for people looking for answers.

But the AI draft of the professional post claims that better photos double sales. Lucas has no evidence for this. So he replaces it with his own experience, a client whose customers asked fewer questions after the photos improved.

The most common mistake is to change only the length. A shorter version of the same text is still written for the wrong audience. And when AI adapts content, it may add numbers or promises that were not in the original. Review every repurposed piece as if it were new.

Let's recap. First, repurposing turns one core piece of content into several new pieces for different channels. Second, channels differ in audience, format and length, so copying the same text everywhere usually performs poorly. Third, for each new piece, write what changes for that audience, and check that the AI did not add untrue details.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, choose one piece of your content, and use AI to repurpose it into at least four formats. Under each one, write one sentence about what you changed for that audience. It takes about thirty minutes. These pieces can feed your capstone campaign. In the next lesson, Planning a Thirty-Day Campaign Calendar, we start the capstone itself. See you there.
```

## L12 Planning a 30-Day Campaign Calendar

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (680 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Posting when you have time produces random content, and random results. A campaign is different. One goal, one audience, and a clear plan for thirty days. And this lesson starts your capstone.

Last time, we turned one idea into content for many channels. Now we plan when all of it goes out. A campaign plan answers six questions, in order. You decide the first four. AI can help with the rest.

First, the goal. That is one main result, stated so you can measure it, such as one hundred and fifty sign-ups for a free workshop by the end of June. Second, the audience, the persona you confirmed in lesson three. Third, the channels, two to four places where that audience really spends time. For this capstone, choose at least three.

Remember, fewer channels done well are better than many done badly. Fourth, key dates, such as launches, events and deadlines. Then, with AI's help, the fifth and sixth: three content pillars that support the goal, and a posting rhythm based on what you can really produce.

Give these decisions to an AI assistant and ask for a thirty-day calendar as a table. Useful columns include the day and date, a theme for each week, the channel, the format, the pillar, the topic, the call to action, the asset status, and who owns it. Add a notes column too, for example to say where a piece was repurposed from, or whether it needs a label.

Then review the draft. Are the key dates in the right places? Is the rhythm realistic? Does every week move the audience toward the goal? And are the pillars balanced? Fix anything that is wrong before you continue.

A campaign calendar is like a train timetable. The destination is fixed, and that is your goal. The stations are your key dates, and each train leaves at a known time. Without a timetable, trains might still run, but nobody could plan a journey.

Let's plan one. Amara runs a campaign for a sample eco-friendly cleaning products brand in Accra, Ghana. Her goal is that two hundred people sign up for a refill station launch list in thirty days.

Her audience is young families in the city who want to reduce plastic waste, but worry about cost. Her channels are an image-led social feed, a short-video platform and email. The refill station opens on day twenty-two, and there is a community clean-up event on day fifteen.

Her pillars are how to refill, real savings, and our community. Her rhythm is four feed posts, two short videos and one email each week. She gives all this to the AI, and asks that week four builds toward the opening, and follows up after it.

The AI returns a full table, and Amara reviews it. She finds two problems. The clean-up event is on day sixteen, not fifteen. And week two has no real savings content, which is the persona's main worry. She fixes both. She also cuts week three's videos to one, because she knows she has less time that week.

The common mistake is to let the AI choose the goal, channels and dates. The calendar looks complete, but it is not connected to your real business. And a realistic calendar that you finish is much better than an ambitious one you abandon in week two.

Let's recap. First, you decide the goal, audience, channels and key dates, and AI helps fill in the structure. Second, a clear calendar template includes the day, date, week theme, channel, format, pillar, topic, call to action, status, owner and notes. Third, review the AI draft for correct dates, balanced pillars and a rhythm you can really produce.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone. Write your campaign goal and audience, then build a thirty-day calendar with AI help, for a real or sample brand, across at least three channels. It takes about forty minutes. Keep your calendar safe. In the next lesson, Producing Campaign Assets at Speed, you start making it real. See you there.
```

## L13 Producing Campaign Assets at Speed

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M3_L13_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (687 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Making one post at a time means opening the same tools, finding the same files and writing the same prompts, every single day. Making a whole week of content in one focused session can be much faster, if you prepare well.

You now have a thirty-day calendar. In this lesson, you produce the first week of it. The method is batch production. That means creating many similar pieces in one working session, instead of one piece each day. It saves time, because you prepare once and stay focused on one type of task.

Prepare three things before you start. Your voice guide, ready to paste. Your templates, in Canva for posts, quotes and tips, and in CapCut for short videos. And saved prompts, prompts that worked well before, stored in a document with blanks to fill in, such as the platform, the length and the topic.

Then work in blocks, by type of task, not by day. First a text block, for all captions and emails. Then a visual block, for all the graphics. Then a video block, to film and edit the week's short video. And last, a review block, where you check everything with the five-point checklist.

As you work, keep an asset log, a spreadsheet with one row per asset. It records which tools produced which parts, which prompt you used, whether you checked the licence, and whether the asset needs a disclosure label under your rule.

Batch production is like preparing lunches for the whole week on Sunday. You wash all the vegetables at once, cook the rice once, and pack the boxes in a row. It is faster than cooking every day. But you still check that each box is fresh and correct.

Let's follow Noor. She runs content for a sample handmade jewellery brand in Cairo, Egypt. Days one to seven of her calendar have five feed posts, one short video and one email, across three channels.

On Saturday morning, she works in blocks. In the text block, she uses her saved prompt and voice guide to draft all five captions and the email, with three variations each. She chooses, combines, and adds a personal sentence to each one.

In the visual block, she makes five graphics with her three Canva templates. Three use her own product photos, and two use AI-generated background textures. She logs the image tool and prompt for each one. In the video block, she films her hands shaping a silver ring, and edits it in CapCut with auto-captions, checking each word.

In the review block, she places all seven assets side by side. She notices that three captions start with the same question, so she rewrites two. And the email mentions free delivery, but that offer ends before day seven. So she corrects it.

Her asset log now has seven rows. For the two AI textures, disclosure needed says no, because under her rule, abstract backgrounds that do not show a realistic scene need no label. She writes the rule in the log, so a reviewer can see her reasoning.

The common mistake is to rush the review, because the batch felt productive. Speed must not remove the review. In fact, batching makes review easier, because you can compare every piece side by side. Mistakes in batch work repeat. One wrong price can appear in every piece. And fill in the log as you create each asset, not later from memory.

Let's recap. First, batch production creates a week of content in one session, by working in blocks: text, visuals, video, then review. Second, prepare your voice guide, templates and saved prompts before you start. Third, keep an asset log as you work, recording tools, prompts, licence checks and disclosure decisions for every asset.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step two of your capstone. Produce the content for the first seven days of your campaign with Claude or ChatGPT, Canva and CapCut, and keep an asset log as you go. It takes about an hour. In the next lesson, Measuring Results and Improving with AI, we see what is working. See you there.
```

## L14 Measuring Results and Improving with AI

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M3_L14_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (673 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
After a week of posting, you have numbers: views, likes and clicks. An AI assistant can read them and tell you exactly what to do next, in a very confident voice. But should you follow its advice?

In the last lesson, you produced your first week of content. Now let's see how it performs. First, choose a few measures that match your goal. Reach is how many different people saw it. Engagement is how many reacted, commented, shared or saved it. Clicks is how many followed a link. And sign-ups or sales is how many completed the action in your goal.

If your goal is sign-ups, reach and likes are helpful signs, but sign-ups matter most. A post with fewer likes that brings more sign-ups is doing the more important job. Platforms count these measures in different ways, so check each tool's definitions.

AI can summarise a small results table quickly and suggest changes. But a small amount of data can mislead. In one week, one result can be unusual because of the day, the weather, a holiday or luck. The AI may still describe a clear trend. So check its reasoning. How many posts is this based on? Could something else explain it? Is it connected to the goal?

And never paste personal data, such as the names or emails of people who signed up. Use totals only. Reading one week of results is like judging a restaurant after one meal. One bad evening might be the chef's day off. You look for patterns over several visits before you change the whole menu.

Let's try it. Ewa manages a sample campaign for a vegan bakery in Kraków, Poland. Her goal is one hundred and twenty sign-ups for a baking class in thirty days. After week one, she has twenty-two sign-ups.

Here is her sample data. The two short videos reached the most people, three thousand four hundred and two thousand one hundred, but brought only one sign-up each. The email reached six hundred people, and brought nine sign-ups. The recipe carousel brought six. You can use this table in the exercise.

She gives the AI the table and the goal. She asks for a summary and three improvements, and asks it to explain its reasoning and say how confident it is.

The first idea is to post more short videos, because they have the highest reach. Ewa changes this. The videos brought only two sign-ups. So she keeps them for awareness, but adds a clear sign-up call to action at the end.

The second idea is to send the email twice a week, because it brings the most sign-ups. Ewa accepts a smaller version. She adds one short reminder email in week three, because too many emails may make people unsubscribe.

The third idea is to stop quote cards completely, because they perform worst. Ewa rejects this for now. It is one post on one day. She will try one more quote card, with a stronger call to action, before she decides. Then she updates her calendar, with a reason for each change.

The common mistake is following AI suggestions because they sound clear and confident. Another is judging content only by reach or likes. Always ask two questions. Which content moves people toward the goal? And is this result based on enough posts to trust?

Let's recap. First, choose a few measures that match your goal, and give the most weight to the one closest to it. Second, AI can summarise results and suggest changes, but small amounts of data can mislead, so check its reasoning. Third, accept, change or reject each suggestion with a reason, then update your calendar.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below, give an AI assistant the sample table, or your own totals, and ask for three improvement ideas. Accept, change or reject each one with a reason, and update your capstone calendar. It takes about twenty-five minutes. In the final lesson, Final Review and Campaign Presentation, we finish your campaign. See you there.
```

## L15 Final Review and Campaign Presentation

- **Filename:** `ai-07-ai-for-content-creators-and-marketers_M3_L15_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (666 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your campaign is almost ready. Before you show it to a client or a manager, one question matters most. If they check any single post, will it be accurate, on-brand and safe to publish?

Last time, you used your results to improve the plan. Now we finish it. The final stage has two parts: a quality review of the whole campaign, and a clear presentation. Let's start with the review, at three levels.

First, every asset. Apply the five-point checklist: accurate, on-brand, original, inclusive and legally safe. Second, the voice. Read several pieces in a row. Do they sound like the same brand? Look for words from your avoid list, and patterns that repeat across the month.

Third, your disclosure rule. Open your asset log and check every AI-generated item. Does each one follow your rule? Is every licence checked? Then look at the whole campaign. Does every week move the audience toward the goal, and are the key dates correct?

Then the presentation. Clients and managers are busy. So present a short document in seven sections: goal and audience, strategy, the thirty-day calendar, sample assets from each channel, the asset log, your review notes, and a measurement plan.

In the strategy section, add one sentence to explain each choice: your channels, pillars, weekly themes and posting rhythm. In the measurement plan, say which measures you will track, and when you will review them. And be honest about how AI was used. Clients and managers increasingly want to know, and a clear asset log shows care and control.

The final review is like the checks a pilot makes before take-off. The plane may have flown well yesterday, but the pilot still follows the list every time. Small problems are easy to fix on the ground, and costly in the air.

Let's watch a final review. Rafael prepares a campaign for a sample co-working space in Manila, in the Philippines. The goal is forty bookings for a free trial day in thirty days, across a social feed, a professional network and email.

At the asset level, he finds a carousel that says the space is open twenty-four hours. It is actually open from seven in the morning to ten at night, so he corrects it. At the voice level, he notices that the word community appears in almost every caption, so he uses it less.

At the disclosure level, his log shows one realistic AI image of an office, with no label. His rule says realistic AI scenes must be labelled, and this image could make people think it is the real space. So he replaces it with a real photo.

His document has seven short sections. Under review notes, he lists two open questions for his manager. Can we use the photo of the member event? And do we have written consent from the people in it? This makes the decisions clear, instead of hiding them.

Take your time with the review. A common mistake is to check only the first week in detail. Problems often appear later in the month, when work was faster, and dates or offers had changed. Another is leaving out the asset log and review notes. Those show a client that the campaign is safe to publish.

Let's recap. First, review the whole campaign at three levels: every asset, the brand voice across the month, and the disclosure rule, using your asset log. Second, present it as a short, seven-section document. Third, be open about how AI was used, and list open questions, so decision-makers can see that the campaign is under human control.

Congratulations. You have finished AI for Content Creators and Marketers. Your last step is capstone step three. Review and finish your thirty-day campaign, and present it as a short document. It takes about an hour. Before you submit, read the capstone rubric and tick every item on the submission checklist. Then submit your campaign. You planned it, you shaped it, and it sounds like you. Well done.
```
