# L04 When AI Gets It Wrong: Checking Output

Course: AI-06 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O2, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
An AI assistant writes you a perfect-looking email in five seconds. The grammar is excellent and the tone is friendly. But one date is wrong, and it has added a promise you never made. If you send it, your name is on that mistake, not the AI's.

## Explanation
AI assistants predict likely text. They do not look up the truth before they answer. This means they can:

- **State wrong facts** with full confidence, such as a wrong date, number or name.
- **Invent sources**, such as a report, a law or a web link that does not exist.
- **Change your meaning**, for example turning "we may offer a discount" into "we will offer a discount".
- **Add or lose details**, such as dropping a deadline from your notes or adding a detail you did not give.

When an AI tool invents information, people often call it a **hallucination**. It is not a sign that the tool is broken. It is a normal risk of how these tools work, so checking is part of every task.

Use this **4-step review checklist** before you use or send any AI output:

1. **Facts and numbers:** Are all facts, figures, prices and quantities correct? Check them against your own notes or an original source.
2. **Names and dates:** Are the names, job titles, days and dates right? Does "Friday" match the date you wrote?
3. **Tone for the reader:** Does it sound right for this person? Is it too formal, too casual, too long, or does it promise something you cannot deliver?
4. **Confidential content:** Was anything confidential added, and was anything important lost? Remember the rules from L03 and your employer's AI policy. [REGION]

The more important the text, the more carefully you check. A thank-you note to your team needs a quick read. A message to a customer about money or deadlines needs a careful, line-by-line check.

**Analogy:** Checking AI output is like proofreading a colleague's draft before your name goes on it. The colleague may be skilled and quick, but you are the one who signs it. You would read every number and date, and ask, "Is this what I actually want to say?"

## Worked Example
Kofi is a hypothetical events coordinator at a university in Kumasi, Ghana. He asks a free AI assistant for an email inviting staff to a training day.

```text
Write a short email inviting staff to a first-aid training day on Tuesday 13 October, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m., in Room B12. Places are limited to 20. Ask people to reply by Friday.
```

The draft looks good. Kofi uses the checklist:

1. **Facts and numbers:** The draft says "places are limited to 25". Wrong. He changes it to 20.
2. **Names and dates:** It says "Tuesday 13 October". Kofi checks a calendar to make sure that date really is a Tuesday in the correct year, because the AI does not always know. He also sees it wrote "9 a.m. to 2 p.m." and corrects the end time.
3. **Tone:** The draft ends "Lunch will be provided!" Kofi never said that. He deletes it, because it is a promise he cannot keep.
4. **Confidential content:** Nothing confidential was added, and the reply deadline is still there.

Three errors in a short email. Each one would have caused confusion if Kofi had sent the first draft.

## Common Mistake
Many people check only the spelling and grammar, because those are the errors they are used to finding. AI output is usually well written, so it "feels" correct. The real risks are in the facts, numbers, dates and promises. The correction is to use the 4-step checklist every time, and to spend most of your checking time on facts and meaning, not on style.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI assistants can state wrong facts, invent sources and change your meaning, all with full confidence.
2. Use the 4-step review checklist: facts and numbers, names and dates, tone for the reader, and confidential content added or lost.
3. You are responsible for anything you send, so always check AI output before you use it or share it.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Ask a free AI assistant to write a short email that includes 3 facts you can check, then use the checklist to find and correct any errors.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); an official public holiday calendar for your country; a notes app.
**Steps:**
1. Ask a free AI assistant: "Write a short email to my team listing the next 3 public holidays in [your country] and reminding them to plan their leave." Do not add any names or private details.
2. Find the official public holiday list for your country, for example on a government website. [REGION]
3. Check each date and holiday name in the email against the official list.
4. Go through all 4 steps of the checklist and write one line for each step: "correct" or the error you found.
5. Correct the email yourself and save the final version.
**What good looks like:** A four-line checklist record and a corrected email. Each date has been checked against an official source, and any error (wrong date, wrong name, missing holiday, added promise) is noted and fixed.
**Time:** about 20 minutes

## Review Flags
- [REGION] Public holiday dates and official sources differ by country; learners check their own country's official list. No specific dates are stated in the course. Employer AI policies also differ by organisation and country.
- The date in Kofi's example is hypothetical, and the lesson itself tells learners to check the weekday against a calendar.
- Kofi and his university are hypothetical.
