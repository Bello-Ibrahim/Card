# L10 Map Your Tasks: Where AI Helps and Where It Does Not | Presenter Script

Course: AI-06 · Video: 5 min · Words: 688

## Hook
Use AI for everything is not a plan. Some of your tasks will get faster with AI. Others will get slower, riskier or simply worse. The skill is knowing which is which, and that starts with looking closely at your own work.

## Explain
In the last lesson, we organised a week of tasks. Now we start the capstone, by choosing the right tasks for AI. Good tasks usually have three features. They are repeated, so a good method pays back many times. They are text-based, about writing, reading or organising words. And they are low-risk, so a mistake is easy to notice and fix.

Tasks that involve personal data, legal or financial decisions, sensitive conversations or judgement about people are poor candidates, or they need strict human control. Your employer's AI policy may also say which tasks and data are allowed.

Most tasks are not all AI or no AI. They are made of steps. So break each task into steps, and give each step one of three labels.

AI can draft, where it writes a first version that you check. AI can check, where you do the work and it helps you review it. And human only, where the step needs your judgement, your relationships, private data or a final decision. Each label tells you who does the work.

Then time the task as you do it now. This is your before measurement for the capstone. Do not rely on general claims about how much time AI saves. Your own tasks, your own speed and your own checking time are what matter.

Think of reading a recipe before you cook with a helper. You decide which steps the helper can do, such as chopping. Which steps they can check, such as tasting the salt. And which steps you must do yourself, such as deciding when the dish is ready.

## Demonstrate
Let's map a real task. Fatima is a school administrator in Casablanca, in Morocco. Every week she writes a newsletter for parents. It takes time every week, and it is mostly text, so it is a good task to map. She breaks it into six steps.

Step one, collect news from teachers. That is human only, because it needs her relationships with staff. Step two, choose which items to include. Also human only, because it needs judgement about what parents need. Step three, write short paragraphs from the teachers' notes. AI can draft that, once she removes student names.

Step four, make the language simple and friendly. AI can draft that, because rewording is a strength. Step five, check dates, times and event details. AI can list all the dates for her, but she confirms each one herself. Step six, approve and send. Human only, because the final responsibility is hers.

So three of her six steps, three, four and five, are good candidates for AI support. And step five still needs her to confirm every date.

Fatima also writes a safety rule for step three. Teachers' notes sometimes include student names, so she removes them before pasting anything into a free tool, following the school's policy. Then she writes the newsletter the usual way this week, and records her before time.

A common mistake is to start with your hardest or most important task. Those tasks need the most judgement and carry the most risk. Start with repeated, text-based, low-risk tasks, and map every step.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, good tasks for AI are repeated, text-based and low-risk. Second, break each task into steps, and label each step AI can draft, AI can check, or human only. Third, time each task as you do it now, so you can measure your own results later instead of trusting general claims.
