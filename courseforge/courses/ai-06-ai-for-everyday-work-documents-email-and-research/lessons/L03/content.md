# L03 Safe Use at Work: Privacy and Confidential Data

Course: AI-06 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
It takes two seconds to copy a customer complaint, paste it into a chatbot and ask for a polite reply. It can take much longer to explain to your manager why a customer's name, address and account number are now stored in an outside tool. This lesson helps you avoid that situation.

## Explanation
When you type or paste text into an AI assistant, that text is sent to the provider's computers. Depending on the tool, the plan and your settings, it may be stored for some time, and in some cases it may be used to improve the provider's models. [VERSION] These settings differ between free and paid plans and they change often, so you cannot assume that a free chat is private. [VERSION]

**Never paste these into a free, public AI tool:**

- **Personal data** about customers, patients, students or colleagues: names, addresses, phone numbers, ID numbers, health or salary details.
- **Passwords, access codes and security information.**
- **Contracts and legal documents** that are not public.
- **Unreleased financial figures**, prices, plans or product information.
- Anything your organisation marks as **confidential or internal**.

Some text is **safe after you remove details**. You can replace names with roles ("the customer", "Colleague A"), remove numbers or round them, and describe the situation in general terms. The AI still helps with the wording, but no private data leaves your computer.

**Free consumer tools and company-approved tools are different.** Many organisations pay for business versions of AI tools, such as Microsoft Copilot or Gemini in Workspace, that come with different data agreements. [VERSION] Your IT or legal team decides which tools are approved and for what data. That is why the first rule is always: **follow your employer's AI policy.** If there is no policy, ask your manager before you use AI with work documents.

Data protection laws also differ by country and region. [REGION] This course does not give legal advice. Your employer's policy is designed to follow the rules that apply to your organisation, so it is your best guide.

**Analogy:** Using a free AI tool is like discussing work in a busy public café. You can talk about general ideas, how to word a polite reply, or how to plan a meeting. You would not read a customer's file aloud or share the company's unreleased results at the next table.

## Worked Example
Priya is a hypothetical claims assistant at an insurance company in Pune, India. A customer has written an angry email about a delayed payment, and Priya wants help with a calm reply.

First, she checks her company's AI policy. It says staff may use the approved business AI tool for internal text, but must not paste customer data into free tools. Priya is working in a free tool today, so she does not paste the email.

Instead she writes:

```text
A customer is upset because an insurance payment is two weeks late. We have now approved it and it will arrive within 5 working days. Write a calm, polite reply of about 100 words that apologises and explains the next step.
```

She has removed the name, the policy number and the exact amount. The AI writes a good reply. Priya then adds the customer's name and details herself, inside the company email system, and checks the whole message before sending.

## Common Mistake
Many people think, "It is only one email, and nobody will read it." But the risk is not about who reads one chat. It is about personal and confidential data leaving the systems your organisation controls, which can break company rules and, in some cases, the law. [REGION] Another mistake is to think that deleting a chat afterwards solves the problem. Depending on the provider, the data may already be stored. [VERSION] The correction is to remove or replace sensitive details before you paste, every time.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never paste personal data, passwords, contracts, unreleased figures or anything confidential into a free AI tool.
2. You can often still get help by removing names and numbers and describing the situation in general terms.
3. Free consumer tools and company-approved tools have different data terms, so always follow your employer's AI policy first.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Sort 10 example snippets of work text into "safe to paste", "safe after removing names and numbers" or "never paste", then find out about your organisation's AI policy.
**Tools:** Pen and paper or a notes app; your organisation's intranet or HR/IT team.
**Steps:**
1. Sort these 10 snippets: (1) "Ideas for a team lunch"; (2) a customer's email with name and phone number; (3) the Wi-Fi password for the office; (4) a public job advert from your website; (5) next quarter's sales figures before release; (6) "A colleague is often late; how do I raise it politely?" with no name; (7) a supplier contract; (8) meeting notes that mention colleagues' health; (9) a paragraph from a public government web page; (10) a complaint email with all names and numbers removed.
2. Write one reason for each choice.
3. Compare with the answer key on the course page.
4. Search your intranet or ask your manager, HR or IT team whether your organisation has an AI policy. [REGION]
5. Write down one rule from the policy, or write "no policy found" and who you asked.
**What good looks like:** All 10 snippets sorted with a short reason each. Snippets 3, 5, 7 and 8 are "never paste"; snippet 2 is "safe after removing names and numbers" at best. One real policy rule is noted, or a clear note that no policy exists.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
