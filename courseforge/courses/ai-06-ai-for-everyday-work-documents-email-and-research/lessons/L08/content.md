# L08 Researching a Topic with AI

Course: AI-06 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O4, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
You ask an AI assistant about a work topic, and it gives you a clear answer with three references to reports and a link. You click the link. It does not work. You search for the report title. It does not exist. This lesson shows you how to research with AI without being caught by this problem.

## Explanation
AI assistants are very useful at the **start** of research, when a topic is new to you. They can:

- **Explain a topic** in plain language, so you understand the basic ideas quickly.
- **Create a list of questions** you should answer before you make a decision.
- **Suggest search terms** and the types of sources to look for, such as government guidance, professional bodies or academic studies.
- **Compare options** at a general level, such as the advantages and disadvantages of different approaches.

AI assistants are **not reliable as the final source** of facts. They can invent facts, and they can invent **citations**: report titles, authors, statistics, laws and web links that look real but do not exist. Some tools can search the web and show links, which helps, but the answer can still misreport what the source says. [VERSION]

So research with AI in two stages:

1. **Explore with AI:** understand the topic, collect questions and search terms.
2. **Verify in original sources:** for every key claim you will use, find the original source yourself. Open every link. Check that the source exists, that it is trustworthy, that it is recent enough and that it really says what the AI claimed.

If you cannot find an original source for a claim, do not use the claim.

A useful prompt for the first stage:

```text
I am new to [topic]. Explain the main ideas in plain English. Then list 8 questions I should research, and suggest search terms and the types of official sources I should check.
```

**Analogy:** An AI assistant in research is like a helpful person at a conference who tells you what they remember from many talks. They help you understand the subject and know where to look. But you would not quote them in a report until you had found and read the original talk yourself.

## Worked Example
Mei is a hypothetical HR officer at a technology company in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia. Her director asks her to research options for a hybrid-work policy.

She starts with a general prompt, with no company details:

```text
Explain the common types of hybrid-work policy for office staff, with advantages and disadvantages of each. List questions an HR team should answer before choosing one.
```

The answer gives her four policy types and a useful list of questions about fairness, equipment and team meetings. It also says that "a national law requires employers to consider flexible work requests" and cites a named report with a statistic about productivity.

Mei checks every claim before she shares her notes:

- **The four policy types:** she finds them described in guidance from professional HR bodies. Correct.
- **The legal claim:** she searches the official government website. The rules are different from what the AI said, so she marks the claim "partly correct" and uses the official wording. [REGION]
- **The report and statistic:** she searches for the report title and the authors. She cannot find the report anywhere. She deletes the claim completely.

Her final notes contain only claims she has checked, and each one lists its original source.

## Common Mistake
The most serious mistake is copying AI references into a report or presentation without opening them. An invented citation in your work damages your credibility and can lead to wrong decisions. A second mistake is to believe a claim because the AI gave a link. The link may be real but say something different. The correction: open every source, read the relevant part, and use only what you have confirmed yourself.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use AI to explore a new topic: explain it, list questions and suggest search terms and types of sources.
2. AI can invent facts and citations, so open and check every reference and link before you use it.
3. Use only claims that you have confirmed in an original, trustworthy source, and record that source in your notes.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Research a work-related question with a free AI assistant, choose 3 key claims from its answer and check each one against an original source.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); a web search engine; official or professional websites related to your topic; a notes app or spreadsheet.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a work question that you can research with public information, for example "What are good practices for onboarding remote staff?" Do not include company details.
2. Ask a free AI assistant to explain the topic and to include sources.
3. Choose 3 key claims from the answer, including at least one fact or number and one reference if it gave any.
4. For each claim, search for an original source. Open every link and search for every report title.
5. Record each claim in a table with 3 columns: the claim, the original source you found, and your result: "correct", "partly correct" or "wrong / not found".
**What good looks like:** A table with 3 checked claims. Each has a real source (web address or document title) or a clear note that no source could be found. Any claim you could not verify is marked and not used.
**Time:** about 30 minutes
