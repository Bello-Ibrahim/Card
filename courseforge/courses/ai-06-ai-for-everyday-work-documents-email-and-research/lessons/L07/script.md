# L07 Summarising Long Documents and Meetings | Presenter Script

Course: AI-06 · Video: 5 min · Words: 714

## Hook
A forty-page report arrives on Monday, and your manager wants the main points by Tuesday. An AI assistant can give you a summary in seconds. But does that summary contain what your manager needs? And is it correct?

## Explain
In the last lesson, we built documents step by step. Today we go the other way, and make long texts short. A summary is only useful if it has a purpose. Just saying summarise this gives you a general summary. A prompt with a purpose gives you what you really need.

For example, ask for the key decisions in a document. Or every action, who is responsible and the deadline, in a table. Or five bullet points for a busy manager who has not read it. Or, when you have two versions, a list of what has changed.

There are two main ways to summarise at work. The first is to paste text, or upload a file, into a chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT. This works well for public documents, such as a government guidance page. Free plans may limit how long the text can be. And never upload confidential documents or meeting notes to a free tool.

The second way is the AI built into your work tools, such as Microsoft Copilot or Gemini in Workspace. They can summarise documents and email threads, and in some plans meetings, without copying the text out. If your employer provides them, they are often the approved route for internal documents.

Whichever tool you use, follow your employer's AI policy. And for anything important, compare the summary with the original. A summary can miss a condition, merge two points, change a number, or say something more strongly than the original.

Think of a colleague who reads the report and tells you the main points in the corridor. It saves you a lot of time. But before you make a decision, you open the report and check the parts that matter.

## Demonstrate
Let's try it. Rangi is a policy officer at a local council in Wellington, in New Zealand. He needs to brief his team on a long public consultation report. I'll show the same steps with a public government guidance page.

Here is the public document in my browser. As I scroll, you can see it is long. Next, I open Claude in a new tab and start a new chat. This is a public document, so it is safe to paste.

Now the prompt with a purpose. Summarise this in five bullet points for a team manager who has not read it. Then list any actions or deadlines in a table with three columns: action, who and deadline.

I type the prompt under the pasted text and press Enter. Here is the answer: five short bullets, and a table of actions and deadlines.

Now the important part. I go back to the original and check two points. This bullet matches the original, so it is correct. But this one has changed a detail. The original is more careful than the summary.

That is exactly what happens to Rangi. One bullet says the council will reduce parking fees. The original says the council will consider reducing them. Rangi corrects it before he sends the brief, because that small change would give his team the wrong message.

Finally, the built-in route. In a work account, I open a document and use the built-in summary button. Notice that the text never leaves the work system. Use this route for internal documents, if your employer has approved it.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, ask for summaries with a clear purpose, such as key decisions, actions with owners, or a short version for one reader. Second, paste only public documents into free chatbots, and use your employer's approved tools for internal documents and meetings. Third, AI summaries can miss or change details, so compare them with the original for anything important.
