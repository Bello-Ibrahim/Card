# L07 Summarising Long Documents and Meetings

Course: AI-06 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O4 · Video: 5 min (screen demo)

## Hook
A 40-page report arrives on Monday, and your manager wants "the main points" by Tuesday. An AI assistant can give you a summary in seconds. The question is whether that summary contains what your manager actually needs, and whether it is correct.

## Explanation
A summary is only useful if it has a **purpose**. "Summarise this" gives a general summary. A purpose-driven prompt gives you exactly what you need:

- **Key decisions:** "List the decisions made in this document."
- **Action items with owners:** "List every action, who is responsible and the deadline, in a table."
- **A short version for a specific reader:** "Summarise this in 5 bullet points for a busy manager who has not read it."
- **What changed:** "Compare these two versions and list what is different."

There are two main ways to summarise at work.

**1. Pasting text or uploading a file to a chatbot.** You copy the text, or upload the file, into a tool such as Claude or ChatGPT. This works well for **public** documents, such as a government guidance page or a published report. Free plans may limit how long the text or file can be. [VERSION] Never upload confidential documents or meeting notes to a free tool.

**2. Built-in AI in your work tools.** Microsoft Copilot in Microsoft 365 and Gemini in Google Workspace can summarise documents, email threads and, in some plans, meetings, without copying the text out. [VERSION] These features depend on your organisation's plan and settings, and they change often. [VERSION] If your employer provides them, they are often the approved route for internal documents. Always follow your employer's AI policy. [REGION]

Whichever tool you use, **compare the summary with the original for anything important.** AI summaries can miss a key condition, merge two points into one, change a number or state something more strongly than the original.

**Analogy:** An AI summary is like asking a colleague to read a report and tell you the main points in the corridor. It saves you a lot of time. But before you make a decision or tell your manager, you open the report and check the parts that matter.

## Worked Example
Rangi is a hypothetical policy officer at a local council in Wellington, New Zealand. He needs to brief his team on a long public consultation report.

**Screen demo steps for the presenter:**

1. Open a public document in the browser, such as a long guidance page from any government website. Show its length by scrolling.
2. Open a free AI assistant in a new tab and start a new chat. [VERSION]
3. Copy a section of the public document, or upload the file if the plan allows it. [VERSION] Say aloud: "This is a public document, so it is safe to paste."
4. Type a purpose-driven prompt:

```text
Summarise this document in 5 bullet points for a team manager who has not read it. Then list any actions or deadlines it mentions in a table with columns: Action, Who, Deadline.
```

5. Show the answer. Then scroll back to the original and check two points on screen: one that is correct, and one detail that the summary missed or changed.
6. Open a document in Microsoft Copilot or Gemini in Workspace (if available on the presenter's account) and show the built-in summary button. [VERSION] Point out that the text did not need to be copied out of the work system.

In Rangi's case, the summary is mostly good. But one bullet says "the council will reduce parking fees". The original says the council "will consider" reducing them. Rangi corrects it before he sends the brief, because that small change would give his team the wrong message.

## Common Mistake
Many people forward an AI summary without reading the original at all. A summary can sound complete while leaving out a condition, an exception or a deadline. Another common mistake is pasting internal meeting notes or confidential reports into a free chatbot to save time. The correction: use free chatbots for public documents, use only approved tools for internal ones, and check every important point against the original.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask for summaries with a clear purpose: key decisions, action items with owners, or a short version for a specific reader.
2. Paste only public documents into free chatbots; for internal documents and meetings, use the tools your employer has approved.
3. AI summaries can miss or change details, so compare the summary with the original for anything important.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Ask a free AI assistant for a 5-point summary and a list of action items from a long public document, then mark one point it got right and one detail it missed or changed.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier) in a web browser; a long public document, such as a government guidance page or an organisation's annual report.
**Steps:**
1. Find a long public document related to your work. Do not use internal or confidential documents.
2. Open a free AI assistant and start a new chat. [VERSION]
3. Paste the text, or upload the file if your plan allows it. [VERSION]
4. Ask: "Summarise this in 5 bullet points for a busy manager, then list any action items or deadlines in a table."
5. Read the original document and compare it with the summary.
6. Mark one summary point that is fully correct, and one detail that the summary missed, changed or stated too strongly. Write down where it appears in the original.
**What good looks like:** A saved summary and action list, plus two notes: one correct point with its location in the original, and one missed or changed detail with the correct wording from the original.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
