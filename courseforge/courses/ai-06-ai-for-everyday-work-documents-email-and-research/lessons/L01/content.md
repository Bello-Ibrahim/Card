# L01 What an AI Assistant Can Do for Your Work

Course: AI-06 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Think about your last working week. How much of it did you spend writing emails, rewording documents, reading long texts or making lists? An AI assistant can help with many of these tasks. It can also make mistakes that you must catch. This lesson shows you where it helps and where it does not.

## Explanation
An **AI assistant** is a chat tool that reads what you type and writes an answer in natural language. Well-known examples are Claude, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Gemini. [VERSION] Most of them have a free version that you use in a web browser or a phone app, and paid or company versions with more features. [VERSION]

These tools learned from very large amounts of text. When you ask a question, they predict which words are likely to come next, one piece at a time. This is why they write fluently. It is also why they can sound sure about something that is not true.

AI assistants are usually **good at**:

- **Drafting:** a first version of an email, notice, report section or job advert.
- **Rewording:** making text shorter, clearer, more formal or friendlier.
- **Summarising:** turning a long text that you give them into key points.
- **Brainstorming:** ideas for a meeting agenda, questions for an interview or titles for a presentation.

They are usually **weak at**:

- **Exact facts:** numbers, dates, names, laws and prices can be wrong.
- **Recent events:** some tools do not know about recent news unless they can search the web, and even then they can make errors. [VERSION]
- **Your private context:** the tool does not know your company, your manager, your customers or your deadlines unless you tell it.

**Analogy:** An AI assistant is like a fast, well-read new colleague on their first day. They can write a clear draft in seconds and they know a little about many subjects. But they do not yet know your company, your customers or how your team works, and sometimes they guess instead of saying "I don't know". You would welcome their help, but you would check their work before you send it with your name on it.

Two safety rules start now and run through the whole course. First, **do not paste confidential or personal data into free AI tools**, such as customer names, colleague details, passwords or unreleased figures. Second, **follow your employer's AI policy**, because rules differ between organisations and countries. [REGION] You will learn more about both in L03.

## Worked Example
Tomasz is a hypothetical logistics coordinator in Gdańsk, Poland. On Monday he tries a free AI assistant on three small tasks.

**Task 1: an out-of-office message.** He types:

```text
Write a short, polite out-of-office message. I am away Monday to Wednesday. Urgent questions go to the shift supervisor.
```

The answer is useful as it is. He changes one word and uses it.

**Task 2: ideas for a team meeting.** He asks for five ideas to make the weekly shift handover meeting shorter. Three ideas are useful. Two do not fit his warehouse, because the tool does not know how his team works.

**Task 3: a fact question.** He asks which rules apply to driver rest times in his country. The answer sounds confident, but Tomasz cannot tell whether it is current or correct. He decides to check the official source instead and not to rely on the AI answer.

Tomasz writes a note: "Good for drafts and ideas. Not reliable for rules and facts. Always check."

## Common Mistake
Many beginners treat an AI assistant like a search engine or an expert that always knows the answer. It does not work that way. It predicts likely text, so a wrong answer can look exactly as polished as a right one. The correction is simple: use AI for drafting, rewording, summarising and ideas, and treat every fact, number, name and date as something to check before you use it.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Gemini are good at drafting, rewording, summarising and brainstorming.
2. They are weak at exact facts, recent events and your private work context, and they can sound confident when they are wrong.
3. Never paste confidential or personal data into free tools, follow your employer's AI policy, and check the output before you use it.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Try a free AI assistant on 3 small tasks from your real working week, and note which answers were useful as they were and which needed changes.
**Tools:** The free tier of Claude or ChatGPT in a web browser; a notes app or paper.
**Steps:**
1. Open the free version of Claude or ChatGPT and create an account if needed. [VERSION]
2. Choose 3 small tasks from your week, for example an out-of-office message, ideas for a meeting agenda and a short thank-you note. Do not include any names, customer details or confidential information.
3. Ask the assistant for help with each task in one or two plain sentences.
4. For each answer, write "useful as it was", "useful after changes" or "not useful", and one sentence explaining why.
5. Circle any facts, numbers or dates in the answers that you would need to check.
**What good looks like:** Three short notes, each with a rating and a reason, for example "useful after changes: the tone was too formal for my team". At least one note identifies a fact or detail that needs checking.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
