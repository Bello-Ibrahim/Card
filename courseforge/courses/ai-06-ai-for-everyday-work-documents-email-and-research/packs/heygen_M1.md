## L01 What an AI Assistant Can Do for Your Work

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (726 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Think about your last working week. How much of it went on emails, rewording documents, reading long texts or making lists? An AI assistant can help with many of these tasks. It can also make mistakes that you must catch.

Hi, and welcome to AI for Everyday Work: Documents, Email and Research. In this first lesson, we look at what an AI assistant can do for your work, and where it is weak.

An AI assistant is a chat tool that reads what you type and writes an answer in natural language. Well-known examples are Claude, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Gemini. Most of them have a free version in a web browser or phone app, and paid or company versions with more features.

These tools learned from very large amounts of text. When you ask something, they predict which words are likely to come next. That is why they write so fluently. It is also why they can sound sure about something that is not true.

They are usually good at four things. Drafting, such as a first version of an email or a job advert. Rewording, to make text shorter, clearer or friendlier. Summarising a long text that you give them. And brainstorming ideas for an agenda or a presentation.

They are usually weak at three things. Exact facts, such as numbers, dates, names, laws and prices. Recent events, which some tools may not know about. And your private context. The tool does not know your company, your customers or your deadlines unless you tell it.

Here is a simple way to picture it. An AI assistant is like a fast, well-read new colleague on their first day. They can write a clear draft in seconds.

But they do not yet know your company or how your team works, and sometimes they guess instead of saying I don't know. You would welcome their help. But you would check their work before it goes out with your name on it.

Two safety rules start today and run through the whole course. First, do not paste confidential or personal data into free AI tools. That means customer names, colleague details, passwords or unreleased figures. Second, follow your employer's AI policy, because rules differ between organisations and countries.

Let's see this at work. Tomasz is a logistics coordinator in Gdańsk, in Poland. On Monday, he tries a free AI assistant on three small tasks from his week.

First, an out-of-office message. He asks for a short, polite message that says he is away from Monday to Wednesday, and that urgent questions go to the shift supervisor. The answer is useful as it is. He changes one word and uses it.

Second, ideas for a team meeting. He asks for five ideas to make the weekly shift handover shorter. Three ideas are useful. Two do not fit his warehouse, because the tool does not know how his team works.

Third, a fact question. He asks which rules apply to driver rest times in his country. The answer sounds confident. But Tomasz cannot tell whether it is current or correct. So he checks the official source instead, and does not rely on the AI answer.

Tomasz writes a short note to himself. Good for drafts and ideas. Not reliable for rules and facts. Always check.

A common mistake is to treat an AI assistant like a search engine, or an expert that always knows the answer. It predicts likely text, so a wrong answer can look just as polished as a right one. Use it for drafts, rewording, summaries and ideas, and check every fact, number, name and date.

Let's recap. First, AI assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini are good at drafting, rewording, summarising and brainstorming. Second, they are weak at exact facts, recent events and your private work context, and they can sound confident when they are wrong. Third, keep confidential data out, follow your employer's policy, and check the output before you use it.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, try a free AI assistant on three small tasks from your real week, with no names or confidential details. Rate each answer, and circle any facts you would need to check. It takes about twenty minutes. The next lesson is Writing Clear Prompts: Task, Context, Format, Examples. See you there.
```

## L02 Writing Clear Prompts: Task, Context, Format, Examples

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (738 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** The AI answers in the demo will differ from the lesson text. Record the vague answer first and point out any invented or missing details it really contains; if it happens to invent none, say so in the edit and keep the comparison on length and tone.

```text
Two people ask the same AI assistant for a notice about the office move. One gets a vague, generic text that needs rewriting. The other gets a draft she can send after a few small edits. The tool is the same. The difference is the prompt.

In the last lesson, we saw what an AI assistant is good at. Today we learn how to ask it clearly. A prompt is the instruction you type. The assistant only knows what is in your prompt and in the conversation so far. If you leave out important details, it fills the gaps with guesses.

A clear prompt has four parts. First, the task, what you want the assistant to do. Use a clear verb, such as write, summarise, list or compare. Second, the context, the background it needs. Who is the reader? What is the situation? What are the key dates and places?

Third, the format, what the answer should look like. For example, an email of about one hundred and twenty words, five bullet points, or a friendly and simple tone. Fourth, an example, if you have one. An old notice that worked well helps a lot.

Think of it like briefing a temporary colleague who has just arrived. If you only say do the notice, they will guess. If you tell them what it is about, who will read it and how long it should be, they can do good work the first time.

You do not always need all four parts. A quick question may only need a task. But for anything you will send to other people, context and format usually give a much better first draft. And you can continue the conversation. If the answer is too long, just ask for a shorter one.

One safety rule from lesson one still applies. Your context must not include confidential or personal data. Use general descriptions, such as our team of forty staff, instead of names and private details.

Let's try it. Amara is an office manager in Lagos, in Nigeria. Her company is moving to a new floor, and she must tell the staff. I'm using the free version of Claude in a web browser.

Here is her first prompt. It is one short sentence, with no details at all.

I open a new chat, type the vague prompt and press Enter. Look at the answer. It is long and very general. It may also invent details that are not true, such as a move to a new city.

Now the improved prompt, with the four parts on separate lines. The task is a notice to staff. The context gives the facts: from the third floor to the seventh floor, on Friday, desks packed by Thursday at four, boxes at reception. The format is a friendly email of about one hundred and twenty words. And the example is an opening line.

I open a second new chat, so the first answer does not influence this one. I type the improved prompt, one part per line, and press Enter.

Now let's put the two answers side by side. The second one uses the correct floors and dates. It is the right length, and the tone is friendly. The first one is longer and guesses.

I don't need to start again to improve it. In the second chat I type a follow-up, make it twenty words shorter, and the assistant rewrites the draft.

Amara still checks the dates, changes one sentence so it sounds like her, and then sends it. A common mistake is to write a very short prompt and then decide that AI is not useful. Before you press Enter, ask yourself: would a new colleague understand this request?

Let's recap. First, a clear prompt has four parts: task, context, format and, if you have one, an example. Second, the more relevant context you give, the fewer guesses the assistant makes, but never include confidential or personal data. Third, you can improve an answer by continuing the conversation, for example make it shorter, or use a more formal tone.

Your turn. In the exercise below this video, take the vague request, write a notice about the new parking rules, and rewrite it with the four parts. Invent the details and use no real names. Compare the two answers and note three differences. The next lesson is Safe Use at Work: Privacy and Confidential Data. See you there.
```

## L03 Safe Use at Work: Privacy and Confidential Data

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (686 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
It takes two seconds to copy a customer complaint, paste it into a chatbot and ask for a polite reply. It can take much longer to explain to your manager why that customer's name and account number are now in an outside tool.

In this lesson, we look at safe use. When you type or paste text into an AI assistant, it is sent to the provider's computers. Depending on the tool, the plan and your settings, it may be stored for some time, and it may even be used to improve the tool.

These settings are different on free and paid plans, and they change often. So you cannot assume that a free chat is private.

So, what should you never paste into a free, public AI tool? Personal data about customers, patients, students or colleagues, such as names, addresses, ID numbers, health or salary details. Passwords and access codes. Contracts that are not public. Unreleased financial figures, prices or plans. And anything your organisation marks as confidential or internal.

But some text is safe after you remove the details. Replace names with roles, such as the customer or colleague A. Remove numbers or round them, and describe the situation in general terms. The AI still helps with the wording, but no private data leaves your computer.

Free consumer tools and company-approved tools are also different. Many organisations pay for business versions, such as Microsoft Copilot or Gemini in Workspace, which can come with different data agreements. Your IT or legal team decides which tools are approved, and for what data.

That is why the first rule is always the same. Follow your employer's AI policy. If there is no policy, ask your manager before you use AI with work documents. Data protection laws also differ by country and region, and this course does not give legal advice. Your employer's policy is your best guide.

Here is a simple picture. Using a free AI tool is like talking about work in a busy public café. You can discuss general ideas, or how to word a polite reply. But you would not read a customer's file aloud at the next table.

Let's see this in practice. Priya is a claims assistant at an insurance company in Pune, in India. A customer has written an angry email about a late payment, and Priya wants help with a calm reply.

First, she checks her company's AI policy. It says staff may use the approved business tool for internal text, but must not paste customer data into free tools. Today Priya is using a free tool, so she does not paste the email.

Instead, she writes a general prompt. A customer is upset because a payment is two weeks late. It is now approved and will arrive within five working days. She asks for a calm, polite reply of about one hundred words that apologises and explains the next step.

She has removed the name, the policy number and the exact amount. The AI writes a good reply. Then Priya adds the customer's details herself, inside the company email system, and checks the whole message before she sends it.

A common mistake is to think, it is only one email, nobody will read it. But the risk is data leaving the systems your organisation controls. And deleting the chat afterwards may not help, because depending on the provider, the data may already be stored.

Let's recap. First, never paste personal data, passwords, contracts, unreleased figures or anything confidential into a free AI tool. Second, you can often still get help by removing names and numbers and describing the situation in general terms. Third, free tools and company-approved tools have different data terms, so always follow your employer's AI policy first.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, sort ten example snippets of work text into safe to paste, safe after removing details, or never paste. Then find out whether your organisation has an AI policy, and note one rule from it. The next lesson is When AI Gets It Wrong: Checking Output. See you there.
```

## L04 When AI Gets It Wrong: Checking Output

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (683 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** The date in Kofi's example (Tuesday 13 October, shown on the scene 10 slide) is hypothetical, and the lesson tells learners to check the weekday against a calendar. The voiceover does not say the date aloud; before recording, check the slide date matches a Tuesday in the year shown, or remove the year.

```text
An AI assistant writes you a perfect-looking email in five seconds. The grammar is excellent. But one date is wrong, and it has added a promise you never made. If you send it, your name is on that mistake, not the AI's.

In the last lesson, we kept confidential data out of AI tools. Today, we check what comes back. Remember, AI assistants predict likely text. They do not look up the truth before they answer.

This means they can state wrong facts with full confidence, such as a wrong date, number or name. They can invent sources, such as a report or a web link that does not exist. They can change your meaning, for example turning we may offer a discount into we will offer a discount. And they can add or lose details.

When an AI tool invents information, people often call it a hallucination. It does not mean the tool is broken. It is a normal risk of how these tools work. So checking is part of every task.

Here is a four-step review checklist. Step one, facts and numbers. Check every figure, price and quantity against your notes or an original source. Step two, names and dates. Are the names, days and dates right? Does Friday match the date you wrote?

Step three, tone for the reader. Is it too formal, too casual or too long? Does it promise something you cannot deliver? Step four, confidential content. Was anything confidential added, and was anything important lost? Remember the rules from the last lesson, and your employer's AI policy.

The more important the text, the more carefully you check. A thank-you note to your team needs a quick read. A message to a customer about money or deadlines needs a careful, line-by-line check.

Think of it like proofreading a colleague's draft before your name goes on it. The colleague may be skilled and quick, but you are the one who signs it. So you read every number and date.

Let's use the checklist. Kofi is an events coordinator at a university in Kumasi, in Ghana. He asks a free AI assistant for an email that invites staff to a first-aid training day.

His prompt gives the day and date, the time from nine until one, the room, a limit of twenty places, and a request to reply by Friday. The draft looks good. Now he checks it.

Step one, facts and numbers. The draft says places are limited to twenty-five. That is wrong, so he changes it to twenty. Step two, names and dates. He checks a calendar to make sure the date really is a Tuesday, because the AI does not always know. He also sees the end time is two, not one, and corrects it.

Step three, tone. The draft ends with, lunch will be provided. Kofi never said that. He deletes it, because it is a promise he cannot keep. Step four, confidential content. Nothing confidential was added, and the reply deadline is still there.

Three errors in one short email. Each one would have caused confusion if Kofi had sent the first draft.

A common mistake is to check only spelling and grammar. AI output is usually well written, so it feels correct. The real risks are in the facts, numbers, dates and promises. So spend most of your checking time there.

Let's recap. First, AI assistants can state wrong facts, invent sources and change your meaning, all with full confidence. Second, use the four-step checklist: facts and numbers, names and dates, tone for the reader, and confidential content added or lost. Third, you are responsible for anything you send, so always check AI output before you use or share it.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, ask a free AI assistant for a short team email that lists the next three public holidays in your country. Check each date against the official list, go through all four steps, and correct the email yourself. This week is done. Next week starts with Drafting and Replying to Emails. See you there.
```
