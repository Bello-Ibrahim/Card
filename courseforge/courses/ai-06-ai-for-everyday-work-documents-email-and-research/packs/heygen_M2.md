## L05 Drafting and Replying to Emails

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M2_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (694 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Some emails take two minutes to write. Others take much longer: the reminder to a late supplier, or the reply where every word matters. AI can help you start those difficult emails, as long as the final version still sounds like you.

This is week two. We now put the prompt and checking skills from week one to work, starting with email. AI assistants can help with three common email jobs.

The first job is drafting a new email from notes. Write your main points as short bullets, and let the assistant turn them into full sentences. Your bullets hold the facts, so the AI does not need to guess them.

The second job is replying to a long thread. You can ask the assistant to list the open questions and draft a reply that answers each one. But before you paste a thread, remove names, email addresses, signatures and anything confidential, and check your employer's AI policy. If your organisation has an approved AI tool inside your email system, that may be the better choice.

The third job is changing tone. The same message can be more formal, friendlier, shorter or more direct. You simply ask in a follow-up message, like the examples on screen.

A useful habit is to ask for two versions in different tones, and then combine the best parts yourself. That gives you choices, instead of one answer you must accept or reject.

After the AI drafts, you edit. Check it with the four-step checklist from lesson four. Then make it sound like you. AI drafts often use phrases you would never say, such as very formal openings or too many polite words. Your reader knows how you write, so change those phrases.

Think of a skilled assistant who types up your rough notes. The letter looks professional, but you still read it, correct it and sign it. If it does not sound like you, you change it.

Let's see an example. Carlos is a purchasing assistant in São Paulo, in Brazil. A supplier has not delivered an order of office chairs that was due last week. He needs a reminder that is polite but firm.

He writes three points, with the supplier's name removed. Forty chairs were due last Monday. The new team starts on the first of next month. And please confirm a delivery date by Thursday. He asks for two versions of about one hundred words, one very polite, and one polite but firm.

The very polite version is kind but weak. The Thursday deadline is in the last line, and it is easy to miss. The firm version puts the deadline first, but it sounds cold. It ends with, failure to respond will be escalated.

Carlos combines them. He keeps the friendly opening from the first version and the clear deadline from the second. He removes the cold last line, because he has worked with this supplier for years, and he would never write that.

He adds the supplier's name and the order number himself, inside his company email system. Then he checks the facts. Forty chairs, due last Monday, confirmation by Thursday. All correct. The final email is clear, firm, and sounds like Carlos.

A common mistake is to send the AI draft with only small changes. The email is correct, but it does not sound like the sender, and it can feel cold. Always edit the final version in your own words.

Let's recap. First, give the AI your key points as bullets, so the facts come from you and not from its guesses. Second, ask for two versions in different tones, then combine the best parts and edit them so they sound like you. Third, remove names and personal details before pasting any email, follow your employer's AI policy, and check the final email before you send it.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, write three bullet points for a real email you need to send, with names replaced by roles. Ask for two versions in different tones, combine the best parts, and check the result. The next lesson is Writing and Editing Documents. See you there.
```

## L06 Writing and Editing Documents

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (691 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask an AI assistant to write our new safety procedure, and it will give you three pages in seconds. They will look professional. They will also be full of steps that your workplace does not use. There is a better way.

In the last lesson, we worked on emails. Today we move to longer documents. AI can help with two kinds of document work. Drafting means first versions of reports, procedures, briefings, agendas and job descriptions. Editing means making your own text clearer, shorter, better organised or easier for non-experts.

When you edit, the facts come from your own text, so the risk of invented content is lower. But you must still check that the meaning has not changed.

For drafting, the key idea is to build the document step by step. First, ask for an outline: a list of headings and short notes. Second, improve the outline yourself. Remove sections that do not apply, add what is missing, and put the steps in the order your team really follows. This is where your knowledge matters most.

Third, draft one section at a time. Give the assistant the facts for that section and ask it to write it. Fourth, edit each section. Check the facts, remove anything invented, and adjust the words for your readers. Each step is small enough to check properly.

For editing, you can use prompts like the ones on screen. Rewrite a paragraph in plain English for new staff, and keep all the facts. Make a section shorter without removing any steps. Or list any sentences that could be read in two ways.

Here is a picture to remember. Building a document with AI is like working with a builder from a plan. You would not say build me a house in one sentence and accept whatever appears.

You agree the plan first, then build one room at a time, and inspect each room before you move on. And as always, keep confidential documents out of free tools, and follow your employer's AI policy.

Let's see it in practice. Aigerim is an office coordinator at an engineering company in Almaty, in Kazakhstan. Her manager asks her for a one-page procedure for new staff on how to request office equipment. She starts by asking for an outline only.

The AI suggests seven sections, including budget approval by the finance director, and vendor selection. Aigerim knows these do not apply. New staff choose from a fixed list, and their team leader approves. So she removes both, adds where to collect your equipment, and reorders the rest. Her outline now has five sections.

Next, she asks the AI to draft just one section, how to submit a request. She gives it the facts. Use the online form, choose from the standard list, and your team leader approves within two working days. Now the AI has the real facts, so it does not need to guess them.

The draft adds a new promise. You will receive an email confirmation within one hour. Aigerim is not sure this is true, so she asks the IT team. It is not, so she deletes it. She keeps only facts that she can confirm.

Finally, she asks the AI to rewrite the section in shorter sentences for new staff. Then she checks that no step was lost. The most common mistake is to ask for the whole document in one prompt. The long result hides invented steps and wrong details, and it is tiring to check.

Let's recap. First, use AI both for first drafts and for editing your own text, to make it clearer, shorter or simpler. Second, build documents step by step: outline, improve the outline yourself, draft one section, then edit. Third, check every section for invented details and changed meaning, and keep confidential documents out of free tools.

Your turn. In the exercise below this video, choose a simple procedure you know well, such as booking a meeting room. Ask for an outline, improve it yourself, then have the AI draft one section and check it line by line. The next lesson is Summarising Long Documents and Meetings. See you there.
```

## L07 Summarising Long Documents and Meetings

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (710 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A forty-page report arrives on Monday, and your manager wants the main points by Tuesday. An AI assistant can give you a summary in seconds. But does that summary contain what your manager needs? And is it correct?

In the last lesson, we built documents step by step. Today we go the other way, and make long texts short. A summary is only useful if it has a purpose. Just saying summarise this gives you a general summary. A prompt with a purpose gives you what you really need.

For example, ask for the key decisions in a document. Or every action, who is responsible and the deadline, in a table. Or five bullet points for a busy manager who has not read it. Or, when you have two versions, a list of what has changed.

There are two main ways to summarise at work. The first is to paste text, or upload a file, into a chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT. This works well for public documents, such as a government guidance page. Free plans may limit how long the text can be. And never upload confidential documents or meeting notes to a free tool.

The second way is the AI built into your work tools, such as Microsoft Copilot or Gemini in Workspace. They can summarise documents and email threads, and in some plans meetings, without copying the text out. If your employer provides them, they are often the approved route for internal documents.

Whichever tool you use, follow your employer's AI policy. And for anything important, compare the summary with the original. A summary can miss a condition, merge two points, change a number, or say something more strongly than the original.

Think of a colleague who reads the report and tells you the main points in the corridor. It saves you a lot of time. But before you make a decision, you open the report and check the parts that matter.

Let's try it. Rangi is a policy officer at a local council in Wellington, in New Zealand. He needs to brief his team on a long public consultation report. I'll show the same steps with a public government guidance page.

Here is the public document in my browser. As I scroll, you can see it is long. Next, I open Claude in a new tab and start a new chat. This is a public document, so it is safe to paste.

Now the prompt with a purpose. Summarise this in five bullet points for a team manager who has not read it. Then list any actions or deadlines in a table with three columns: action, who and deadline.

I type the prompt under the pasted text and press Enter. Here is the answer: five short bullets, and a table of actions and deadlines.

Now the important part. I go back to the original and check two points. This bullet matches the original, so it is correct. But this one has changed a detail. The original is more careful than the summary.

That is exactly what happens to Rangi. One bullet says the council will reduce parking fees. The original says the council will consider reducing them. Rangi corrects it before he sends the brief, because that small change would give his team the wrong message.

Finally, the built-in route. In a work account, I open a document and use the built-in summary button. Notice that the text never leaves the work system. Use this route for internal documents, if your employer has approved it.

Let's recap. First, ask for summaries with a clear purpose, such as key decisions, actions with owners, or a short version for one reader. Second, paste only public documents into free chatbots, and use your employer's approved tools for internal documents and meetings. Third, AI summaries can miss or change details, so compare them with the original for anything important.

Your turn. In the exercise below this video, choose a long public document, such as a government guidance page. Ask a free AI assistant for a five-point summary and a list of action items. Then mark one point it got right, and one detail it missed or changed. The next lesson is Researching a Topic with AI. See you there.
```

## L08 Researching a Topic with AI

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (688 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** [REGION] Laws on flexible and hybrid work differ by country. The voiceover does not say what any law says; it only shows Mei finding that the AI's legal claim differs from the official source.

```text
You ask an AI assistant about a work topic. It gives you a clear answer with three references and a link. You click the link. It does not work. You search for the report. It does not exist.

In the last lesson, we summarised documents that we already had. Today, we research a topic that is new to us. AI assistants are very useful at the start of research. They can explain a topic in plain language, list the questions you should answer, suggest search terms and types of sources, and compare options at a general level.

But AI is not reliable as the final source of facts. It can invent facts. It can also invent citations: report titles, authors, statistics, laws and web links that look real but do not exist. Some tools can search the web and show links, which helps. But the answer can still misreport what the source says.

So research with AI in two stages. Stage one, explore with AI. Understand the topic and collect questions and search terms. Stage two, verify in original sources. For every key claim, find the original yourself. Open every link. Check that the source exists, that it is trustworthy and recent, and that it really says what the AI claimed.

And one simple rule. If you cannot find an original source for a claim, do not use the claim.

Here is a useful first-stage prompt. It asks for the main ideas in plain English, then a list of questions to research, and suggestions for search terms and official sources to check.

Think of a helpful person at a conference who tells you what they remember from many talks. They help you understand the subject and know where to look. But you would not quote them until you had read the original talk yourself.

Let's see this in practice. Mei is an HR officer at a technology company in Kuala Lumpur, in Malaysia. Her director asks her to research options for a hybrid-work policy.

She starts with a general prompt, with no company details. She asks for the common types of hybrid-work policy, the advantages and disadvantages of each, and the questions an HR team should answer before choosing one.

The answer gives her four policy types, and useful questions about fairness, equipment and team meetings. It also makes a claim about what a national law requires, and it cites a named report with a statistic about productivity. Some of this is useful, and some of it needs checking.

Mei checks every claim before she shares her notes. The four policy types appear in guidance from professional HR bodies, so they are correct. For the legal claim, she checks the official government website. The rules are different from what the AI said, so she marks it partly correct and uses the official wording.

Then the report and its statistic. She searches for the title and the authors. She cannot find the report anywhere, so she deletes the claim completely. An invented report in her notes could have misled her director. Her final notes contain only claims she has checked, and each one lists its original source.

The most serious mistake is copying AI references into a report without opening them. And a real link is not enough, because the page may say something different. Open every source, read the relevant part, and use only what you have confirmed.

Let's recap. First, use AI to explore a new topic: explain it, list questions, and suggest search terms and types of sources. Second, AI can invent facts and citations, so open and check every reference and link. Third, use only claims you have confirmed in an original, trustworthy source, and record that source in your notes.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, research a work question with public information and no company details. Choose three key claims from the answer and check each one against an original source. Mark each as correct, partly correct, or wrong. Next week, we redesign your work week, starting with Organising Your Work: Plans, Lists and Templates. See you there.
```
