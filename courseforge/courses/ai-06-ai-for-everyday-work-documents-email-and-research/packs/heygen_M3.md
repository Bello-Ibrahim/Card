## L09 Organising Your Work: Plans, Lists and Templates

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M3_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (685 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
It is Monday morning. You have fifteen things to do, some urgent, some large, and some you have avoided for weeks. An AI assistant can turn that list into a neat plan in seconds. But only you know which task really cannot be late.

This is week three, where we redesign your work week. In this lesson, AI helps you organise your work in three ways. First, it can turn a messy list into a plan. It can group similar tasks, suggest an order, and spread the work across the days.

Second, it can suggest checklists for tasks with many small steps, such as preparing a meeting or welcoming a new team member. You add the steps it missed and remove the ones that do not apply.

Third, it can build reusable templates for tasks you repeat, such as a weekly status update or a meeting agenda. The template has headings and spaces to fill in, so you do not start from a blank page each week.

Here is a useful planning prompt. It asks the assistant to group the tasks, suggest a priority order, and spread them across the week, with your available hours per day. It also asks for a table with a column called why this order.

That why column matters. It shows the assistant's reasoning, so you can see where it guessed. Remember, it does not know your deadlines, your manager's priorities, or which task depends on another team. It may put an easy task first because it looks quick.

So here is the rule for this lesson. AI can suggest an order, but you decide the priorities.

Think of a planning partner who arranges the pieces on the table. They put the pieces in a neat order quickly. But you know which pieces matter most, because you know the deadlines and the people.

And before you paste any task list, replace the names of clients, colleagues and projects with general labels, such as client A report or project X. As always, follow your employer's AI policy.

Let's see an example. Oksana is a programme assistant at a non-profit organisation in Lviv, in Ukraine. Her list for next week has twelve tasks, including a report for a donor, booking a venue for volunteer training, and the Friday team meeting agenda.

She replaces the donor's real name with donor A, and pastes the list with the planning prompt. The AI puts update contact list on Monday morning, because it is quick and easy. It puts the donor report on Thursday.

Oksana changes two things and writes down why. First, she moves the donor report to Monday and Tuesday. The AI did not know that her manager must review it on Wednesday, before it goes to the donor on Thursday.

Second, she moves the venue booking to Monday. Venues in her city book up quickly at this time of year, and she knows the training date cannot change.

Then she asks for a reusable agenda template for the Friday team meeting, with headings for updates, decisions and actions. She edits it once and saves it to use every week.

A common mistake is to accept the AI's plan because it looks organised. But a neat plan is not the same as a correct plan. Read the why behind each choice, change what conflicts with what you know, and write down your reason.

Let's recap. First, AI can turn a messy list into a weekly plan, suggest checklists and draft reusable templates. Second, the assistant does not know your deadlines, dependencies or people, so you decide the final priorities. Third, remove client, colleague and project names before pasting a task list, and ask the AI to explain its order so you can see where it guessed.

Your turn. In the exercise below this video, paste your real task list for next week, with sensitive details removed, and ask for a prioritised plan. Change at least two of its choices and write down why. Your capstone starts in the next lesson, Map Your Tasks: Where AI Helps and Where It Does Not. See you there.
```

## L10 Map Your Tasks: Where AI Helps and Where It Does Not

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M3_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (682 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Use AI for everything is not a plan. Some of your tasks will get faster with AI. Others will get slower, riskier or simply worse. The skill is knowing which is which, and that starts with looking closely at your own work.

In the last lesson, we organised a week of tasks. Now we start the capstone, by choosing the right tasks for AI. Good tasks usually have three features. They are repeated, so a good method pays back many times. They are text-based, about writing, reading or organising words. And they are low-risk, so a mistake is easy to notice and fix.

Tasks that involve personal data, legal or financial decisions, sensitive conversations or judgement about people are poor candidates, or they need strict human control. Your employer's AI policy may also say which tasks and data are allowed.

Most tasks are not all AI or no AI. They are made of steps. So break each task into steps, and give each step one of three labels.

AI can draft, where it writes a first version that you check. AI can check, where you do the work and it helps you review it. And human only, where the step needs your judgement, your relationships, private data or a final decision. Each label tells you who does the work.

Then time the task as you do it now. This is your before measurement for the capstone. Do not rely on general claims about how much time AI saves. Your own tasks, your own speed and your own checking time are what matter.

Think of reading a recipe before you cook with a helper. You decide which steps the helper can do, such as chopping. Which steps they can check, such as tasting the salt. And which steps you must do yourself, such as deciding when the dish is ready.

Let's map a real task. Fatima is a school administrator in Casablanca, in Morocco. Every week she writes a newsletter for parents. It takes time every week, and it is mostly text, so it is a good task to map. She breaks it into six steps.

Step one, collect news from teachers. That is human only, because it needs her relationships with staff. Step two, choose which items to include. Also human only, because it needs judgement about what parents need. Step three, write short paragraphs from the teachers' notes. AI can draft that, once she removes student names.

Step four, make the language simple and friendly. AI can draft that, because rewording is a strength. Step five, check dates, times and event details. AI can list all the dates for her, but she confirms each one herself. Step six, approve and send. Human only, because the final responsibility is hers.

So three of her six steps, three, four and five, are good candidates for AI support. And step five still needs her to confirm every date.

Fatima also writes a safety rule for step three. Teachers' notes sometimes include student names, so she removes them before pasting anything into a free tool, following the school's policy. Then she writes the newsletter the usual way this week, and records her before time.

A common mistake is to start with your hardest or most important task. Those tasks need the most judgement and carry the most risk. Start with repeated, text-based, low-risk tasks, and map every step.

Let's recap. First, good tasks for AI are repeated, text-based and low-risk. Second, break each task into steps, and label each step AI can draft, AI can check, or human only. Third, time each task as you do it now, so you can measure your own results later instead of trusting general claims.

This is capstone step one. In the exercise below this video, choose three weekly tasks from your own job. Map each task into steps, label each step, add a safety note, and time each task the usual way this week. Save your table, because you will need it next. The next lesson is Build Reusable Prompts and a Personal Prompt Library. See you there.
```

## L11 Build Reusable Prompts and a Personal Prompt Library

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (684 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Last month you wrote a prompt that gave you a perfect weekly report. This week, you cannot remember how you wrote it, so you start again from zero. A small personal prompt library solves this problem.

In the last lesson, you mapped three of your tasks. Now we write the prompts for them. A reusable prompt is a prompt you save and use again, with spaces to fill in for the details that change each time. It follows the four parts from lesson two.

Here is a simple template. Task, what you want. Reader, who will read it and what they need. Context, the key facts for this week, with no personal data. Format, the length, structure and tone. And example, a past version you liked, with names removed.

The spaces in brackets change each week. Everything else stays the same. You fill in the gaps, paste it into your AI assistant, and get a consistent result.

The first version of a prompt rarely gives the best result. So test it, look at what went wrong, and change the prompt, not just the output. If the answer was too long, add a word limit. If the tone was too formal, ask for a friendly tone.

If it invented details, tell it to use only the facts you give, and to write missing instead of guessing. If it forgot a section, add that heading to the format line. After two or three rounds, the prompt is usually reliable for that task.

Where should you keep your library? A simple document or notes file is enough. Give each prompt a clear name. Store only the template with empty spaces, never a filled-in version with names or figures. Keep it where your employer allows work files, and follow your employer's AI policy. Some AI tools also let you save instructions inside the tool, so check what your plan offers.

Think of a recipe book. You do not invent a new recipe every time you cook the same dish. You follow the version that worked, change the ingredients for today, and note any improvement in the margin.

Let's build one. Sione is a front office supervisor at a hotel in Suva, in Fiji. Every Monday, he writes a short update for the hotel manager about the week before.

His first prompt just asks for a weekly update about the front office. The result is generic, and it invents figures.

So he builds a reusable prompt. The task is a weekly front office update. The reader is the hotel manager, who wants a quick overview. The context is a space for this week's facts. The format has three headings: highlights, problems and next week, in one hundred and fifty words or fewer. And one clear rule: use only the facts I give you.

He tests it with last week's notes, with guest names removed. The result is good, but the problems section sounds too negative. So he adds neutral tone, focus on solutions, to the format line. The second result is right.

He saves the template in his work notes file, under the name weekly manager update, with the context line left empty. Next Monday, he only needs to add the new facts.

A common mistake is to save prompts that already contain one week's details, such as client names or prices. Then your library becomes a store of confidential data. Save templates, not filled-in versions.

Let's recap. First, a reusable prompt keeps the task, reader and format fixed, and leaves spaces for the facts that change. Second, improve a prompt by fixing the prompt itself after each test, for example with a length limit or a do not guess rule. Third, keep your library in an approved place and free of confidential data.

This is capstone step two. In the exercise below this video, write a reusable prompt for each of your three tasks. Test each one with this week's facts, with names removed, check the result, and improve the prompt at least once. The last lesson is Measure Time Saved and Present Your Redesign. See you there.
```

## L12 Measure Time Saved and Present Your Redesign

- **Filename:** `ai-06-ai-for-everyday-work-documents-email-and-research_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (685 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You may have heard claims that AI will save you many hours every week. Maybe it will, and maybe it will not. The only honest way to know is to measure your own work, including the time you spend checking what the AI produced.

In the last lesson, you built reusable prompts. In this final lesson, you measure the results. Real time savings differ from person to person and from task to task. A fast writer may save little on emails, but a lot on summaries. A task with many facts may need so much checking that AI saves no time at all. That is why this course makes no general claims.

Here is how to measure honestly. Your before time is how long the task took the usual way. You recorded it in lesson ten. Your after time is how long it takes with AI. Include all of it: writing the prompt, waiting, reading, checking, editing and correcting.

Time saved is the before time minus the after time. It can be zero, or even negative. That is a useful result, not a failure. It tells you that this task, or this step, is better done without AI. And measure more than once if you can, because the first try with a new prompt is usually slower.

Time is not the only result. Also judge quality. Was the result as good as your usual work? And judge safety. Did you follow the data rules and your employer's AI policy? A task that is faster but less accurate is not an improvement.

And review every output with the checklist from lesson four. Facts and numbers, names and dates, tone for the reader, and confidential content. Record what you found and fixed.

Think of testing a new route to work. You do not trust someone else's claim that it is faster. You time the old route and the new one yourself, on normal days, and include the time to find parking. Then you decide.

Let's look at an example. Lucía is an office administrator at an accounting firm in Santiago, in Chile. She redesigned three weekly tasks: a team update, a summary of a public tax guidance page, and replies to routine client questions.

Here is her results table. For the team update and the summary, her after times were shorter, even with checking included. The checklist still found a wrong date in one, and a changed condition in the other. But her client replies were slightly slower with AI. So she looks closer at why.

Each reply needed a lot of editing to sound personal. So she decides to write those replies herself, and to use AI only to check them for clarity. She also notes her safety rules. Client names and figures were never pasted, and she used her firm's approved tool for internal notes.

Now, your capstone. It brings together lessons ten, eleven and twelve. You submit your task maps with before times, your three reusable prompts with first and improved versions, a results table like Lucía's, and a one-page summary. For each task, decide: keep, change, or stop using AI. The capstone rubric on the course page shows how each part is assessed.

A common mistake is to time only the moment the AI writes the answer. That makes AI look faster than it really is. Time the whole task, and report every result honestly, including zero or negative savings.

Let's recap. First, measure time saved yourself: before time minus after time, including prompts, checking and editing. Second, judge quality and safety as well as time, and review every output with the four-step checklist. Third, a zero or negative result is useful, because it shows you where AI does not help.

Congratulations. You have finished AI for Everyday Work. You have learned to prompt clearly, work safely, and check everything. Now complete capstone step three. Do your three tasks with AI, time them, check every output, and write your one-page summary. Keep confidential data out, and follow your employer's policy. Then submit your capstone on the course page. Well done, and good luck.
```
