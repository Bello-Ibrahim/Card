## L01 The AI Startup Landscape

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (731 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
New AI companies appear all the time. Some build giant models that cost a fortune to train. Others build a simple app for one type of shop in one city. Both are called AI startups. So where could you realistically begin?

Hi, and welcome to Building an AI Startup. You do not need any business training. We will explain every term as we go.

A startup is a young company that is still searching for a product that many customers want, and for a way to earn money from it that can grow. A founder is the person who starts the company.

Most startups move through five stages. First, an idea: a problem you want to solve. Second, validation: evidence that real people have this problem. Third, an MVP, or minimum viable product, the smallest product that tests your main idea with real users. Fourth, product market fit, when customers keep paying and tell others. Fifth, growth. This course covers the first three.

AI startups can be grouped into four layers. Think of floors in a building. On the ground floor is infrastructure: companies that build large AI models, the chips that run them, or the data centres that host them. This floor needs huge amounts of money and computing power.

Above that are tools for builders, which help other companies build with AI, for example by testing AI output or tracking costs. Then vertical AI, built for one industry, such as law, farming or dentistry. Vertical means going deep into one sector. At the top are applications, for general tasks like writing emails or editing photos.

Most applications and vertical products do not train their own model. They use a model from a model supplier, a company that offers a large model over the internet. The startup adds the rest: the screens, the instructions, the data, the connections to other tools, and customer support.

Here is a picture to remember. In a city's food business, a few large companies run flour mills. That is infrastructure. Others sell ovens. Those are tools. A bakery that makes bread only for hospitals is vertical. A café that sells to anyone is an application.

You do not need to own a flour mill to open a successful bakery. For a small team, vertical AI is often the best place to start, because it wins by knowing one industry better than anyone else.

Let's meet three made up founders. Amara lives in Ghana. Farmers near her ask about pests and planting in local languages, but most advice online is in English.

So she builds a phone service. Farmers send a voice note and get advice in their own language. This is vertical AI for farming. Her strength is not the model. It is her knowledge of local crops and languages, and her trust with farmer groups.

Diego lives in Colombia. Small clinics lose hours each week writing appointment reminders and summarising patient forms. He builds a tool for clinic receptionists. That is also vertical AI. Because it touches health information, he must take data protection seriously, which we cover in lesson eleven.

Mei lives in Malaysia. Logistics companies ask her to read delivery documents that arrive in many formats. Later, other software developers want the same function inside their own products. So she considers selling it as a tool for builders.

None of the three trains a large model. All three add knowledge, data and relationships to existing models. And that brings us to a common mistake.

Many new founders believe a real AI startup must build its own model. So they give up, or spend their first months and money on technology no customer asked for. Training a large model belongs to the infrastructure layer. For most founders, the valuable work is understanding a customer's problem deeply.

Let's recap. First, the startup journey moves from idea to validation, MVP, product market fit and growth. Second, AI startups sit in four layers: infrastructure, tools for builders, vertical AI and applications. Third, a small team with little money usually has the best chance in vertical AI.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, list five AI startup ideas, classify each by layer, and mark the ones a two person team could start with little money. It takes about twenty minutes. Next, we look at finding a problem worth solving. See you there.
```

## L02 Finding a Problem Worth Solving

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (699 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
I want to build something with AI. Many founders start with this sentence. But customers do not pay for AI. They pay to make a problem go away. Today you will learn how to find a problem that is really worth your time.

In the last lesson, we looked at the layers of AI startups. Now we go one step back, to the very start of the journey: the problem.

Starting with the technology is sometimes called a solution looking for a problem. The result often works, but nobody needs it. Starting with the problem is the opposite. You find a group of people, learn what makes their work difficult, and only then ask whether AI can help. Sometimes the best answer is not AI at all.

A problem worth solving usually passes three tests. The first is pain. Does it cost people money, time, customers or sleep? A small annoyance is not enough. The second is frequency. A problem that happens every day is easier to build a business around than one that happens once a year.

The third is willingness to pay. A good sign is that people already spend money or time on a solution, even a poor one. For example, a paid assistant, a homemade spreadsheet, or a tool they complain about.

Write each problem as a short problem statement. A specific group of people struggle to do a task, because of a reason, which costs them time, money or risk. Be specific. Small businesses is too wide. Owners of family run pharmacies in smaller cities is a group you can find and talk to.

Choose a group you can reach: people from your job, your family business or your community. You will need to interview them in the next lesson.

Think of a good doctor. She listens to the patient's symptoms before she chooses a treatment. Where does it hurt? How often? Since when? A doctor who gives every patient the same medicine, because she likes it, helps very few people. A founder who starts with I want to use AI is like that doctor. Listen to the symptoms first.

Let's see this in action. Lan is a made up founder in Da Nang, Vietnam. She used to work as a bookkeeper, so she knows small companies and their accountants well. She writes three problem statements and scores each from one to five.

First, small accounting firms struggle to sort clients' photos of receipts, because the images are messy, which costs staff several hours a week. Second, young professionals struggle to plan a budget. Third, tour guides struggle to write social media posts.

The receipts problem scores thirteen out of fifteen. The other two score seven each. Why? Accounting firms already pay junior staff to do this work. It happens every week. And mistakes can cause problems with tax filings.

The budgeting problem is real, but many young professionals say they would not pay for help, and free apps already exist. The tour guide problem is simply not painful enough.

Notice that Lan has not mentioned a model or an app yet. She has a problem, a group she can reach, and early reasons to believe it matters. But these scores are still guesses. In the next lesson, she will test them.

A common mistake is to choose the problem that excites you most, or where AI looks impressive. Another is to score willingness to pay from hope. Friends say I would definitely pay for that, just to be kind. Better evidence is what people spend today. If nobody spends anything, ask yourself why.

Let's recap. First, start with a specific group of people and their problem, not with the technology. Second, a problem worth solving is painful, frequent, and something people will pay to solve. Third, the best evidence of willingness to pay is what people already spend today.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, write three problem statements for people you can reach, and score each on pain, frequency and willingness to pay. Mark any score without evidence as a guess. It takes about twenty five minutes. Next, we look at customer discovery interviews. See you there.
```

## L03 Customer Discovery Interviews

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (685 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Ask a friend, would you use an app that does this, and they will almost always say yes. It feels good, but it tells you almost nothing. Today you will learn to ask questions that people cannot answer just to be polite.

In the last lesson, you chose a problem. Now you test it by talking to people. This is called customer discovery: talking to potential customers to learn about their problem before you build. The goal is not to sell your idea. The goal is to learn the truth.

The most important rule is this. Ask about past behaviour, not opinions about your idea. Opinions about the future, like would you pay for this, are cheap and often wrong. Facts about the past, like what did you do last time, are much more reliable.

Good questions sound like this. When did this problem last happen? What did you do about it? What did it cost you, in time, money or lost customers? What have you tried already? And who else is involved?

Use a short script of about twenty minutes. Thank the person and say you are not selling anything. Ask for consent to take notes, and promise not to use their name. Do not record audio unless they agree. Ask about their normal week. Then spend most of the time on the last time the problem happened, what it cost, and what they use today.

Close by asking who else you should talk to. And throughout, listen more than you talk. When you hear something important, just say, can you tell me more?

Here is a picture to remember. A detective does not ask a suspect, would you ever commit a crime? She asks, where were you on Tuesday at eight in the evening? Specific questions about real events are much harder to answer with a comfortable story.

Let's watch this work. Elif is a made up founder in Izmir, Türkiye. She believes small shop owners lose money by running out of popular products and ordering too much of slow ones. She interviews five owners, and she does not mention AI.

She asks a grocer, when did you last run out of something? Last Friday, bread flour, before a holiday weekend. What did you do? I sent my nephew to a wholesaler, and we lost half a day. How do you decide what to order? I look at the shelves and write a list in a notebook.

After five interviews, Elif removes all names and labels her notes Shop A to Shop E. Then she asks Claude to list common patterns, and how many shops support each one. Claude suggests three.

One, ordering is done from memory or a notebook, in four of five shops. Two, stock problems cluster before holidays, in three of five. Three, owners want an app to predict demand. Claude says three of five.

Elif checks her notes. Patterns one and two are correct. Pattern three is not supported. No owner asked for an app. One owner only said it would be nice to know earlier. Claude turned a wish into a request. So Elif deletes it and plans more interviews about holiday ordering.

The most common mistake is pitching during the interview. The person politely agrees, and you leave feeling validated, but you learned nothing. Keep your idea out of the conversation, at least until the very end. A second mistake is accepting an AI summary without checking it. Every pattern should point back to real words in your notes.

Let's recap. First, ask about specific past events, actions and costs, not opinions about your idea. Second, use a short script, ask for consent, and listen more than you talk. Third, remove names before using AI to find patterns, and check every pattern against your own notes.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, interview three to five potential customers. Remove names from your notes, use Claude to find patterns, and check each one yourself. Plan about ninety minutes, including the interviews. Next, we look at testing demand before you build. See you there.
```

## L04 Testing Demand Before You Build

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (686 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Interviews tell you that a problem is real. They do not tell you whether people will act: sign up, give their time, or pay. You can find out in a few days, before you build anything.

In the last lesson, you learned to interview customers. Now we test whether they will do something. A demand test is a small, cheap experiment that asks people to take a real action. Actions are stronger evidence than words.

The first test is a landing page. That is a single web page that describes your product and asks visitors to do one thing, such as leave their email to join a waitlist. You can make one with Canva or a free no-code builder. If people will not even leave an email, they are unlikely to pay later.

The second is a concierge test. You deliver the service yourself, by hand, and the customer knows a person is doing it. The third is a Wizard of Oz test. The customer uses what looks like a product, but a person does the AI's job behind the scenes. Be honest if customers ask, and never promise automation you cannot deliver.

Before any test, write a success threshold, the result that counts as a yes. For example, if at least twenty of one hundred visitors join the waitlist, we continue. That is a conversion rate of twenty percent: the share of people who take the action. Set the number before the test, so you cannot change your mind later.

Stronger actions give stronger evidence. From weakest to strongest: a like on social media, an email address, time on a call, a small deposit or pre order, and finally a paid order.

Think of a chef before a restaurant opens. She runs a few pop up evenings in a borrowed kitchen. She does not buy the building first. If people come, pay and return, she has evidence. If the tables are empty, she has lost a few evenings, not her savings.

Let's look at an example. Hamza is a made up founder in Lahore, Pakistan. He believes parents of secondary school students want affordable evening help with maths homework. His long term idea is an AI tutor on the phone that explains problems in Urdu or English.

But he does not build anything. He runs a concierge test. He makes a flyer and a simple landing page. Send a photo of a maths problem before nine in the evening, and get a step by step explanation in thirty minutes.

Before he shares it, he writes his threshold. In two weeks, at least fifteen families send a problem, and at least five agree to pay a small weekly fee for a third week. Then he shares the page in parent groups at two schools.

When photos arrive, Hamza and a friend who teaches maths write the explanations by hand. There is no AI yet, only people doing the job.

After two weeks, twenty two families have sent problems, and seven have agreed to pay. The threshold is met. He also learns three things he could not have guessed. Most requests arrive between eight and nine in the evening. Parents prefer voice notes. And geometry problems with diagrams are the hardest.

A common mistake is deciding what success means after you see the numbers. A weak result suddenly becomes a promising start. Write the threshold down and share it first. And share your page with your real target group, not only friends and family.

Let's recap. First, demand tests ask people for a real action, which is stronger evidence than an opinion. Second, landing pages, concierge tests and Wizard of Oz tests let you learn before you build the AI part. Third, set your threshold before the test, and share it with your real target group.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, build a one page landing page with Canva or a no-code builder, write your success threshold, and share it with at least ten people in your target group. Next, we ask a hard question: is it defensible? See you there.
```

## L05 Is It Defensible? Moats for AI Startups

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (676 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Imagine you spend three months building an AI tool that writes perfect job adverts. Customers like it. Then a competitor copies it in a weekend, and your model supplier adds the same feature for free. What do you have left?

In the last lesson, you tested demand. Now we ask whether your business can last, and what makes an AI business hard to copy. A moat is anything that makes your business hard to copy or replace. The word comes from the water around a castle. A business with a strong moat is called defensible.

Many AI products are wrappers: a simple screen and some instructions around a public model. A wrapper is a fine way to start, test demand and learn what customers need. But let's be honest. On its own, it is easy to copy, because anyone can use the same model and write similar instructions.

There is a second risk. The model supplier may add the same feature to its own product. So the model is not your moat, because your competitors can buy it too.

So what are stronger moats? Proprietary data, meaning data only you have, that improves your product, and that you have the right to use. Workflow integration, where your product sits inside the customer's daily work and replacing it would be painful. And distribution, a way to reach customers that others do not have.

Next, domain expertise, deep knowledge of an industry's rules and language. Then trust, a reputation for accuracy and good support, which matters most in health, finance and law. And sometimes network effects, where the product becomes more valuable as more people use it.

Here is a picture. Anyone can buy the same oven. But a bakery with loyal customers, a secret recipe improved over years, and a contract to supply the local hospital is very hard to copy. The AI model is the oven. Your data, workflow, distribution and trust are the recipe, the customers and the contract.

Let's score a real case. Rafael is a made up founder in Goiás, Brazil. His product reads invoices and bank statements for small farms and prepares monthly accounts for their accountants. It uses a public model.

He completes a moat scorecard. He scores each moat from zero to three, for today and for one year from now. Today his total is only five out of eighteen. A competitor could copy his screens and instructions quickly. The model supplier could add invoice reading too.

But look at his plan. He will keep corrected examples of rural invoices, with permission. He will export directly into the accounting software local accountants use. A farmers' cooperative could recommend him. His family runs a farm, so he knows local rural tax rules. And accountants review every output.

Notice that his scores today are low. That is normal for a new idea. What matters is the plan, because those things are much harder to copy than a screen and a prompt. So Rafael picks one action for the next month: sign a pilot agreement with the cooperative.

A common mistake is saying our prompts are our secret, or we use the best model. Instructions can be rewritten, and competitors can buy the same model, sometimes the same day. Another mistake is claiming we have data when it belongs to customers. A data moat is only real if you have the legal right to use it.

Let's recap. First, a product that only wraps a public model is easy to copy, and the model supplier may add the same feature. Second, real moats come from data, workflow, distribution, expertise, trust and sometimes network effects. Third, most startups begin without a moat. What matters is a clear plan to build one within a year.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, complete a moat scorecard for your idea, and write one action that would make it harder to copy within a year. It takes about twenty five minutes. Next week, we start building, with scoping the MVP. See you there.
```
