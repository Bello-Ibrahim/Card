## L11 Legal, Ethical and Data Risks

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (680 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your MVP works, and your first customers are happy. Then one asks, where does my data go? Who owns the text your AI writes? What happens if it gives wrong advice? If you cannot answer, that customer may leave.

Welcome to the final week. Before you launch, we look at risk. This lesson helps you spot risks early. It is not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change over time, so before you launch, get advice from a qualified lawyer where you operate.

The first area is data protection. Personal data is information that can identify a person, like a name, phone number or health record. Many countries have laws about it. Common principles are: collect only what you need, tell people how you use it, keep it secure, and delete it when you no longer need it.

The second is consent, meaning a person clearly agrees. Ask before you record interviews, use customer data to improve your product, or share data with another company, including your model supplier. Do not hide it in long terms nobody reads. Clear, simple words build trust, and trust is part of your moat.

The third is ownership of AI outputs. Agree in your customer terms who may use the outputs and how, and ask a lawyer what applies in your country. The fourth is model supplier terms. They decide how your data may be stored or used. Read the terms for your plan, because free and paid plans may differ.

The fifth is sector rules. Health, finance, law, education and employment often have extra rules, and some regions also have rules specific to AI. Then there are ethical risks, which may not be illegal but still cause harm: biased results, overconfident answers, and replacing human judgement where it is needed.

Think of a food stall. Cooking well is not enough. You also follow hygiene rules and label allergens. A great recipe does not protect you if customers become ill. Legal and data risks are the hygiene rules of an AI startup.

Let's fill in a checklist. Zofia is a made up founder in Kraków, Poland. Her MVP reads job applications for small companies and suggests which candidates to interview. She rates each risk as low, medium or high, thinking about how likely it is and how much harm it could cause.

Applications contain personal data. That is high. Her next step is legal advice, collecting only what employers need, and setting a deletion period. Screening job applicants may count as a higher risk use of AI in her region. Also high. She will ask a lawyer which duties apply, and make sure a person makes every final decision.

The model may favour some groups unfairly, for example by age or gender. High. So she removes names, photos and ages before the AI sees them, and tests results across groups. She will also publish how she checks for fairness. Model supplier terms are medium. Ownership of summaries is low.

Then Zofia changes her product. Instead of ranking candidates, it summarises each application against the employer's criteria, and a person makes every decision. That reduces risk, and the product is still useful. Notice that one design choice solved several risks at once.

A common mistake is thinking legal questions matter later, when you are bigger. But what data you collect and what you promise are hard to change later. Another mistake is copying another company's privacy pages. Their business and country may be very different from yours.

Let's recap. First, check five areas early: data protection, consent, ownership of outputs, model supplier terms and sector rules. Second, laws differ by country, and this lesson is not legal advice. Third, rate each risk as low, medium or high, and give every high risk a concrete next step.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, complete a risk checklist for your startup, with a next step for each high risk, and write three questions for a local lawyer. Next, we look at funding options and what investors look for. See you there.
```

## L12 Funding Options and What Investors Look For

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (679 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
How much money should I raise? That is often a new founder's first question. A better first question is: do I need outside money at all, and what will I give in return? Every type of funding has a price, even when it looks free.

In the last lesson, you checked your risks. Now we look at money. Every route gives you something, and every route asks for something back. Here are the main funding routes, from the least outside control to the most.

Bootstrapping means you fund the company yourself, with savings or revenue from early customers. You keep full control, but growth may be slower. Grants come from governments, foundations or development organisations, and you usually do not repay them. In return, they often expect reports and a specific use of the money.

Accelerators give small teams training, mentors and contacts for a fixed period, sometimes with a small investment. Angel investors are individuals who invest their own money, usually for equity. Equity means part ownership of the company. Venture capital firms invest other people's money in startups that could grow very large, and they expect very fast growth.

When you sell equity, your share gets smaller. This is called dilution. For example, if you own one hundred percent and sell ten percent to an angel, you now own ninety percent.

What do investors usually look for at this stage? A real problem that customers feel. Evidence of demand, such as sign ups, pilots or paying customers, not only opinions. A strong team with the right skills and industry knowledge, which shows why you are the right people. And a path to a defensible business, with a moat and healthy unit economics.

Choosing funding is like choosing how to travel. Walking is slow, but you choose every step. That is bootstrapping. A bus follows its own route and timetable, like grants and accelerators. A fast train goes far quickly, but you cannot get off when you want, and it only goes to large destinations. That is venture capital.

Let's compare routes for one founder. Ravi is a made up founder in Pune, India. His MVP helps small textile workshops check fabric quality from phone photos. He has twelve pilot workshops and three paying customers.

First, bootstrapping with customer revenue. It gives full control, but growth is slow, and Ravi keeps a part time job. It is a good fit for the next six months.

Second, a regional grant for manufacturing. It is made up for this example. The money is not repaid, but it needs a detailed application, reports, and spending on agreed activities. It fits well, because his product helps small manufacturers.

Third, an angel investor from the textile industry. That brings money, contacts and advice, but also a share of the company, regular updates and clear growth plans. It is possible later, once he has more paying customers.

Ravi does not consider venture capital yet. He does not have the evidence of very fast growth that venture firms expect. So he applies for the grant, and aims to grow from three to fifteen paying customers in six months.

A common mistake is thinking that raising money is a sign of success. It is not. It is a tool with a cost. Some businesses grow well on customer revenue alone. Another mistake is pitching before you have evidence. Without evidence, a pitch is mostly opinion. Your validation work from the first week is what makes investors listen.

Let's recap. First, the main routes are bootstrapping, grants, accelerators, angel investors and venture capital, and each asks for something in return. Second, selling equity dilutes your ownership. Third, investors usually look for a real problem, evidence of demand, a strong team and a path to a defensible business.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, list three funding routes that could fit your startup in your region. Check each one on its official pages, and note what it requires and expects. Next, we start your capstone with finishing your MVP demo. See you there.
```

## L13 Finishing Your MVP Demo

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M3_L13_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (685 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You have two minutes. Someone important is watching your screen. You could show every button and every setting. Or you could show one person solving one real problem. Which one will they remember?

In the last lesson, you looked at funding. Now your capstone begins: a validated AI startup concept, an MVP demo and a ten slide pitch deck. Step one, today, is a two minute demo and a one page validation summary. Step two is the pitch deck in Canva. Step three is pitch practice and your final submission.

The rubric scores five areas: validation evidence, the MVP and its quality checks, unit economics and business model, risk and readiness, and the final deck and demo. Read it before you start.

A good demo shows one user completing the core job from start to finish. It is not a tour of features. Start with the problem, in about fifteen seconds. Introduce a made up user. Then spend a minute or more on the core task, and talk through what is happening in plain language. Show the result, then briefly show how you check quality, and close with what comes next.

Use fake data only. Record your screen with a free screen recorder. Many computers already have one built in. Close other tabs and hide notifications, so no personal or confidential information appears on screen.

Your one page validation summary includes your problem statement, your interviews and top three patterns, your demand test and whether it met the threshold, your test set pass rate, and one thing you learned that changed your product.

Think of a film trailer. It does not show the whole film. It shows the main character, the main problem and one exciting moment, so people want to see more. Your demo is a trailer for your product.

Let's plan a demo. Yasmin is a made up founder in Cairo, Egypt. Her MVP helps small online clothing shops write product descriptions in Arabic and English, from a photo and a few notes.

She opens with the problem. Small online shops spend hours writing descriptions in two languages. In her interviews, seven of nine owners said this delays new stock. Then a persona card: Nadia, a made up shop owner who sells handmade scarves.

The core task takes about a minute. Nadia uploads a photo, adds three notes about material and size, and presses create. The product drafts both descriptions, using only her notes. Then she edits one word and copies it to her shop.

Next, quality. I tested fifteen cases, and thirteen passed. When the notes are missing the size, the tool asks for it instead of guessing. And the close: next, a pilot with ten shops.

In her first recording, Yasmin spent forty seconds on the settings page. She removed it. Settings are not part of the core job.

Yasmin also writes her one page summary. Nine interviews, with the top three patterns and a quote for each. Her demand test, and whether it met the threshold. Her pass rate of thirteen out of fifteen. And one thing she learned that changed her product. The summary gives the demo its proof, so every number must come from her real notes.

The most common mistake is trying to show everything. The second is a live demo that fails because of a slow connection or an unexpected AI answer. So record in advance, practise several times, and keep the recording. Never hide failures in your written summary, but choose a clean, realistic example for the demo.

Let's recap. First, a demo shows one user completing the core job, from problem to result, in about two minutes. Second, use a clear structure: problem, user, core task, result, quality check and close. Third, record in advance with fake data, and add a one page summary of your evidence.

Now it is your turn. This is capstone step one. In the exercise below this video, record a two minute demo of your MVP, and summarise your validation evidence on one page. Read the rubric first. Next, we build the ten slide pitch deck. See you there.
```

## L14 Building the 10-Slide Pitch Deck

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M3_L14_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (688 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A person reading your deck may give each slide only a few seconds. If a slide tries to say five things, they will remember none. If each slide says one thing clearly, they can follow your whole story in minutes.

In the last lesson, you recorded your demo. Now, capstone step two. A pitch deck is a short set of slides that explains your business. The rule for every slide is one message. Write that message as the slide title, as a full sentence, and use the rest of the slide to support it.

Here is the outline. Problem, solution, and a demo with screenshots. Then customer evidence: interviews, your demand test and any pilots. Then market: the first customers you can realistically reach, calculated from the bottom up, so you can explain every number.

Then business model, with your margin and your plan for heavy users. Moat, where you are honest that the model itself is not the moat. Competition, including spreadsheets, manual work and general AI chat apps. Team. And finally the ask: exactly what you want from this audience.

Keep the text short, use large fonts and simple visuals. And keep your risk checklist and unit economics sheet ready as backup, because good questions will come after the last slide.

A pitch deck is like road signs on a motorway. Each sign gives one piece of information, large and clear, because drivers see it only for a moment. A sign with a paragraph of text would be useless.

Let's build one. Olumide is a made up founder in Ibadan, Nigeria. His MVP helps pharmacies check stock and expiry dates by reading photos of shelves and invoices. We build his deck in Canva. The menus may look a little different when you try it, but the steps are the same.

We open Canva with a free account, search for pitch deck, and choose a simple template with plenty of white space. Then we set it to exactly ten slides, deleting or duplicating slides as needed.

Next, we apply the brand colours and font once, so every slide matches. Then we write all the slide titles first. For slide one, small pharmacies lose stock to expiry because they track dates on paper.

On slide four, we add the evidence. Eleven interviews. A waitlist of thirty four out of one hundred and twenty visitors, against a threshold of twenty five. And four pharmacies in a paid pilot.

On slide three, we add screenshots from the MVP with the uploads option. First, we check that they contain no real customer data. On slide six, we add a simple chart of revenue and cost to serve for a typical customer.

Now a check. The first draft of slide seven said, we use the latest AI. That is not a moat. So we change it: a growing set of corrected shelf photos from partner pharmacies, used with permission, and a distribution deal with a pharmacists' association.

Finally, we use present mode to read through the whole deck, checking that each slide has one message. Then we download it as a PDF, or share a view only link.

A common mistake is filling slides with long sentences and reading them aloud. Another is using a large market number from the internet that you cannot explain. Use a small, realistic number you can defend. And never skip the ask. Every pitch should end with a clear request, such as a pilot, an introduction or a specific investment.

Let's recap. First, use ten slides: problem, solution, demo, evidence, market, business model, moat, competition, team and the ask. Second, give every slide one message, written as a full sentence title. Third, use your own evidence and unit economics, and keep backup detail ready for questions.

Now it is your turn. This is capstone step two. In the exercise below this video, build your ten slide pitch deck in Canva, using your validation evidence and unit economics. Check that slide seven describes a real moat and slide ten has a clear ask. Next, and last, we look at pitching to customers and investors. See you there.
```

## L15 Pitching to Customers and Investors

- **Filename:** `ai-28-building-an-ai-startup_M3_L15_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.8 minutes (669 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
You tell the same story to a shop owner and to an investor. The shop owner asks, will this save me time on Monday morning? The investor asks, why can't a large company copy this? Are you ready for both?

In the last lesson, you built your deck. Now, in this final lesson, you learn to pitch it. A pitch is a short, clear explanation of why your product matters, told to a specific audience. The facts stay the same, but the emphasis changes.

Customers care about their own problem. Does it solve it, and how quickly? What does it cost? Is my data safe? How much work is it to start? Investors care about the business. How large can it become? Why this team? What evidence exists? Is it defensible, and are the unit economics healthy?

You will get questions you cannot fully answer. Admit what you do not know, and say how you will find out. Use evidence, not hope. Four of five pilot customers renewed is stronger than everyone loves it. And name the risk with your plan.

So for customers, lead with the problem, the demo and the price. For investors, lead with the problem and your evidence, then the business model, the moat and the ask.

Never invent numbers, customers or partnerships, even small ones. People often check, and one discovered exaggeration can end the relationship.

Think of a tour guide showing the same old city to schoolchildren and to architects. The streets do not change. For children, she talks about stories and people. For architects, she talks about materials and structure. A good pitch is the same city, guided for a different group.

Let's hear two pitches. Achieng is a made up founder in Kisumu, Kenya. Her MVP helps small fish traders record sales and predict weekly demand, using voice notes in Swahili, Dholuo or English.

Her customer pitch: you lose money when fish is left unsold. Send a voice note after each sale, and every Sunday you get a message saying how much to buy each day. It costs less than one kilogram of fish a week, which is a placeholder price, and you can stop any time.

Her investor opening: we interviewed eighteen traders. Twelve joined our pilot, and seven now pay weekly by mobile money. Our moat is a growing record of local sales patterns, collected with consent, and a partnership with a traders' group for distribution.

Then she practises with Claude. She asks it to act as a sceptical investor, someone who doubts her claims. It asks one hard question at a time, about demand, defensibility, unit economics and risks, and after each answer it tells her what was weak. She pastes only a short summary of her startup, with no confidential data.

The hardest question: your pilot is small, and the traders know you personally. How do you know strangers will pay? Achieng has no strong answer. So she adds a next step to her deck: a test with twenty traders in a market where nobody knows her.

A common mistake is giving customers an investor pitch, full of market size. Customers do not care about your market size. They care about Monday morning. Another is defending every weak point. An honest, we have not tested that yet, and here is how we will, builds more trust.

Let's recap. First, keep the facts the same, but lead with value for customers, and with evidence, moat and economics for investors. Second, answer hard questions honestly. Third, practise with Claude as a sceptical investor, and use its hardest questions to improve your deck.

This is capstone step three. In the exercise below, practise your pitch with Claude, improve at least two slides, and then submit your validation summary, your demo and your final deck. Check it against the rubric first. Congratulations on finishing Building an AI Startup. You started with a problem, and now you have evidence, a working MVP and a pitch. I look forward to seeing your capstone.
```
