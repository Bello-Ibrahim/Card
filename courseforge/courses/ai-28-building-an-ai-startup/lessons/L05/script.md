# L05 Is It Defensible? Moats for AI Startups | Presenter Script

Course: AI-28 · Video: 5 min · Words: 667

## Hook
Imagine you spend three months building an AI tool that writes perfect job adverts. Customers like it. Then a competitor copies it in a weekend, and your model supplier adds the same feature for free. What do you have left?

## Explain
In the last lesson, you tested demand. Now we ask whether your business can last. A moat is anything that makes your business hard to copy or replace. The word comes from the water around a castle. A business with a strong moat is called defensible.

Many AI products are wrappers: a simple screen and some instructions around a public model. A wrapper is a fine way to start, test demand and learn what customers need. But let's be honest. On its own, it is easy to copy, because anyone can use the same model and write similar instructions.

There is a second risk. The model supplier may add the same feature to its own product. So the model is not your moat, because your competitors can buy it too.

So what are stronger moats? Proprietary data, meaning data only you have, that improves your product, and that you have the right to use. Workflow integration, where your product sits inside the customer's daily work and replacing it would be painful. And distribution, a way to reach customers that others do not have.

Next, domain expertise, deep knowledge of an industry's rules and language. Then trust, a reputation for accuracy and good support, which matters most in health, finance and law. And sometimes network effects, where the product becomes more valuable as more people use it.

Here is a picture. Anyone can buy the same oven. But a bakery with loyal customers, a secret recipe improved over years, and a contract to supply the local hospital is very hard to copy. The AI model is the oven. Your data, workflow, distribution and trust are the recipe, the customers and the contract.

## Demonstrate
Let's score a real case. Rafael is a made up founder in Goiás, Brazil. His product reads invoices and bank statements for small farms and prepares monthly accounts for their accountants. It uses a public model.

He completes a moat scorecard. He scores each moat from zero to three, for today and for one year from now. Today his total is only five out of eighteen. A competitor could copy his screens and instructions quickly. The model supplier could add invoice reading too.

But look at his plan. He will keep corrected examples of rural invoices, with permission. He will export directly into the accounting software local accountants use. A farmers' cooperative could recommend him. His family runs a farm, so he knows local rural tax rules. And accountants review every output.

Notice that his scores today are low. That is normal for a new idea. What matters is the plan, because those things are much harder to copy than a screen and a prompt. So Rafael picks one action for the next month: sign a pilot agreement with the cooperative.

A common mistake is saying our prompts are our secret, or we use the best model. Instructions can be rewritten, and competitors can buy the same model, sometimes the same day. Another mistake is claiming we have data when it belongs to customers. A data moat is only real if you have the legal right to use it.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a product that only wraps a public model is easy to copy, and the model supplier may add the same feature. Second, real moats come from data, workflow, distribution, expertise, trust and sometimes network effects. Third, most startups begin without a moat. What matters is a clear plan to build one within a year.
