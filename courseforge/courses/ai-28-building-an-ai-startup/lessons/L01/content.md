# L01 The AI Startup Landscape

Course: AI-28 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
New AI companies appear all the time. Some build giant models that are very expensive to train. Others build a simple app for one type of shop in one city. Both are called "AI startups". So where could you, perhaps with one partner and very little money, realistically begin?

## Explanation
A **startup** is a young company that is still searching for a product that many customers want and a way to earn money from it that can grow. A **founder** is a person who starts the company. In this course you will follow the main stages of the **startup journey**:

1. **Idea**: a problem you want to solve.
2. **Validation**: evidence that real people have this problem and want a solution.
3. **MVP (minimum viable product)**: the smallest product that lets you test your main idea with real users.
4. **Product–market fit**: the point where customers keep using and paying for the product, and tell others about it.
5. **Growth**: selling to many more customers, often with outside money.

AI startups can be grouped into four **layers**. Think of them as floors in a building, with each floor resting on the one below.

- **Infrastructure** (the ground floor): companies that build large AI models, the chips that run them, or the data centres that host them. This layer needs very large amounts of money, specialist staff and computing power.
- **Tools for builders**: products that help other companies build with AI, such as services that test AI output, store documents for AI search, or monitor costs. The customers are usually developers.
- **Vertical AI**: products built for one industry, such as law, farming or dentistry. "Vertical" means the product goes deep into one sector instead of serving everyone.
- **Applications**: products for a general task that many people share, such as writing emails, summarising meetings or editing photos.

Most applications and vertical AI products do not train their own model. They use a model from a **model supplier** (a company that offers a large model through the internet) and add the rest: the screens, the instructions to the model, the data, the connection to the customer's other tools, and the customer support.

**Analogy:** Think of a city's food business. A few large companies grow wheat and run flour mills. That is infrastructure. Others sell ovens and baking equipment. Those are tools. A bakery that makes bread only for hospitals, with the right ingredients for patients, is vertical. A café that sells bread to anyone who walks in is an application. You do not need to own a flour mill to open a successful bakery.

For a small team with little money, the realistic places to start are usually vertical AI and applications. Vertical AI is often the better choice, because general applications compete with large technology companies and with the model suppliers themselves. A vertical product wins by knowing one industry better than anyone else: its language, its rules, its daily problems and the people in it.

## Worked Example
Three hypothetical founders show the difference.

**Amara** lives in Ghana. Many small farmers near her ask questions about pests and planting in local languages, and most farming advice online is in English. She builds a phone service where farmers send a voice note and receive advice in their own language. This is **vertical AI** for agriculture. Her strength is her knowledge of local crops and languages, and her trusted relationships with farmer groups.

**Diego** lives in Colombia. He sees that small clinics lose hours each week writing appointment reminders and summarising patient forms. He builds a tool for clinic receptionists. This is also **vertical AI**, for small health providers. Because it touches health information, he will need to take data protection seriously (see L11).

**Mei** lives in Malaysia. Logistics companies ask her for help to read delivery documents that arrive in many formats. She first builds a document-reading service for them. Later she notices that other software developers want the same function inside their own products, so she considers selling it as a **tool for builders**.

None of the three trains a large model. All three add knowledge, data and relationships to existing models.

## Common Mistake
Many new founders think that a "real" AI startup must build its own model. So they either give up, or spend their first months and money on technology that customers never asked for. Training a large model belongs to the infrastructure layer, where a small team cannot usually compete. For most founders, the valuable work is understanding a customer's problem deeply and using existing models to solve it well.

## Key Takeaways
1. The startup journey moves from idea to validation, MVP, product–market fit and growth. This course covers the first three stages.
2. AI startups sit in four layers: infrastructure, tools for builders, vertical AI and applications.
3. A small team with little money usually has the best chance in vertical AI, where deep knowledge of one industry is an advantage.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** List 5 AI startup ideas you find interesting and classify each by layer. Mark the ones a two-person team could start with little funding.
**Tools:** A notes app, a free spreadsheet, or pen and paper. Optional: Claude (free plan) to challenge your classification.
**Steps:**
1. Write down 5 AI startup ideas. They can be your own ideas or products you have seen or heard about.
2. For each idea, write its layer: infrastructure, tools for builders, vertical AI or application.
3. Add one sentence explaining your choice. For example: "Vertical, because it serves only veterinary clinics."
4. Mark each idea "possible" or "not realistic" for a two-person team with little funding, and give one reason.
5. Optional: paste your list (with no confidential details) into Claude and ask, "Which of these classifications would you question, and why?" Decide whether you agree.
**What good looks like:** Five ideas, each with a layer, a one-sentence reason and a realistic judgement. Infrastructure ideas are marked "not realistic" for a small team, with a clear reason such as the cost of computing power.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
