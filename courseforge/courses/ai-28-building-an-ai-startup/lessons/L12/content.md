# L12 Funding Options and What Investors Look For

Course: AI-28 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O1, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
"How much money should I raise?" is often the first question new founders ask. A better first question is: "Do I need outside money at all, and what will I give in return?" Every type of funding has a price, even when it looks free.

## Explanation
Here are the main funding routes, from the least to the most outside control:

- **Bootstrapping**: you fund the company with your own savings or with revenue from early customers. You keep full ownership and control, but growth may be slower.
- **Grants**: money from governments, foundations or development organisations that you usually do not repay. In return, they often require reports, specific uses of the money, or a focus on social impact. Applications can take time.
- **Accelerators**: programmes that give a small team training, mentors, contacts and sometimes a small investment, often for a fixed period. Some take a share of your company in return.
- **Angel investors**: individuals who invest their own money in early startups, usually in exchange for **equity**. Equity means part-ownership of the company. Many angels also offer advice and contacts.
- **Venture capital (VC)**: firms that invest other people's money in startups that could grow very large, in exchange for equity. VCs expect very fast growth and a possible large return, for example if the company is later sold.

When you sell equity, your own share becomes smaller. This is called **dilution**. For example, if you own 100% and sell 10% to an angel, you then own 90%.

Funding programmes, their rules and their availability differ a lot by country and change over time. Always check the current rules of any programme directly with the organisation [REGION] [VERIFY].

**What investors usually look for** at this stage:

1. **A real problem**: evidence that customers feel real pain (L02, L03).
2. **Evidence of demand**: sign-ups, pilots, paying customers or letters of intent, not only opinions (L04).
3. **A strong team**: people with the skills and knowledge of the industry to solve this problem.
4. **A path to a defensible business**: a plan to build a moat (L05) and healthy unit economics (L09).

**Analogy:** Choosing funding is like choosing how to travel. Walking (bootstrapping) is slow, but you choose every step. A bus (grants and accelerators) follows its own route and timetable. A fast train (venture capital) gets you far quickly, but you cannot get off whenever you want, and it only goes to large destinations.

## Worked Example
Ravi is a hypothetical founder in Pune, India. His MVP helps small textile workshops check the quality of fabric from phone photos. He has 12 pilot workshops and 3 paying customers. He compares three routes:

| Route | What it gives | What it expects | Fit for Ravi now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping with customer revenue | Full control | Slow growth; Ravi keeps a part-time job | Good for the next 6 months |
| A regional innovation grant for manufacturing [REGION] [VERIFY] | Money that is not repaid | A detailed application, reports, and spending on agreed activities | Good fit, because his product helps small manufacturers |
| An angel investor from the textile industry | Money, contacts, advice | A share of the company; regular updates; clear growth plans | Possible later, once he has more paying customers |

Ravi does not consider venture capital yet. His market may be large, but he does not yet have enough evidence to show the very fast growth that VCs expect. He decides to apply for the grant and to use the next six months to grow from 3 to 15 paying customers.

## Common Mistake
Many founders think raising money is a sign of success. It is not; it is a tool with a cost. Investors expect a return, grants expect reports and specific results, and all funding takes time to raise. Some businesses grow well with customer revenue alone. Another mistake is pitching to investors before collecting evidence of demand. Without evidence, a pitch is mostly opinion.

## Key Takeaways
1. The main routes are bootstrapping, grants, accelerators, angel investors and venture capital, and each asks for something in return.
2. Selling equity dilutes your ownership, and different investors expect different speeds of growth.
3. Investors usually look for a real problem, evidence of demand, a strong team and a path to a defensible business.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** List 3 funding routes that could fit your startup in your region, with what each one requires and expects.
**Tools:** A free spreadsheet or document; a web browser; optional Claude (free plan) to suggest types of programmes to search for.
**Steps:**
1. Write one sentence on how much money you need for the next 6 to 12 months, and what for.
2. Optional: ask Claude, "What types of funding might suit an early-stage [your sector] startup in [your country]?" Use the answer only as a list of types to search for, not as facts.
3. Search for real programmes, investor groups or grants in your region, and read their official pages.
4. For 3 routes, record: what it gives, what it requires, what it expects in return, and deadlines.
5. Mark each as "fit now", "fit later" or "not a fit", with one reason.
6. Note which evidence from your validation work each route would want to see.
**What good looks like:** Three routes with information checked on official pages, clear requirements and expectations, an honest fit judgement, and a link to your own evidence.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
