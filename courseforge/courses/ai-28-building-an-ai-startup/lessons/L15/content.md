# L15 Pitching to Customers and Investors

Course: AI-28 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O6, O7 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
You tell the same story to a shop owner and to an investor. The shop owner asks, "Will this save me time on Monday morning?" The investor asks, "Why can't a large company copy this?" Same product, very different questions. Are you ready for both?

## Explanation
A pitch is a short, clear explanation of why your product matters, told to a specific audience. The facts stay the same, but the **emphasis** changes.

**Customers** care about their own problem:

- Does this solve my problem, and how quickly?
- How much does it cost, and is it worth it?
- Is it safe for my data, and what happens when it is wrong?
- How much work is it to start?

**Investors** care about the business:

- How large can this become?
- Why this team?
- What evidence of demand exists?
- How defensible is it, and are the unit economics healthy?
- What are the main risks?

For customers, lead with the problem, the demo and the price. For investors, lead with the problem and evidence, then the business model, moat and the ask.

**Answering hard questions honestly.** You will be asked questions you cannot fully answer. Good responses:

- **Admit what you do not know**: "I do not know yet. My next test will answer that by the end of next month."
- **Use evidence, not hope**: "Four of the five pilot customers renewed" is stronger than "Everyone loves it".
- **Name the risk and your plan**: "Yes, a large company could copy the basic feature. Our plan is to build an integration with local accounting software and a set of corrected examples they do not have."

Never invent numbers, customers or partnerships. Investors and customers often check, and one discovered exaggeration can end the relationship.

You can practise with Claude playing a **sceptical investor**: someone who doubts your claims and asks difficult questions. Paste only a summary of your pitch, without confidential data.

**Analogy:** A tour guide shows the same old city to schoolchildren and to architects. The streets do not change. For children, she talks about stories and people. For architects, she talks about materials and structure. A good pitch is the same city, guided for a different group.

## Worked Example
Achieng is a hypothetical founder in Kisumu, Kenya. Her MVP helps small fish traders record sales and predict weekly demand using voice notes in Swahili, Dholuo or English.

**Customer pitch (30 seconds):** "You lose money when fish is left unsold. Send a voice note after each sale, and every Sunday you get a message saying how much to buy for each day. It costs less than one kilogram of fish per week [VERSION], and you can stop at any time."

**Investor pitch opening (30 seconds):** "Fish traders lose income from unsold stock. We interviewed 18 traders; 12 joined our pilot and 7 now pay weekly by mobile money. Our moat is a growing record of local sales patterns, collected with traders' consent, and a partnership with a traders' group for distribution."

She practises with Claude using this prompt:

> Act as a sceptical early-stage investor. I will give you a summary of my startup. Ask me one hard question at a time about demand, defensibility, unit economics and risks. After each answer, tell me what was weak and what evidence would make it stronger. Do not be polite for the sake of it.

Claude's hardest question: "Your pilot is small and the traders know you personally. How do you know strangers will pay?" Achieng has no strong answer. She adds a next step to her deck: a test with 20 traders in a market where nobody knows her.

## Common Mistake
Many founders give the same investor-style pitch to customers, full of market size and growth plans. Customers do not care about your market size. Another mistake is defending every weak point instead of admitting it. An honest "we have not tested that yet, and here is how we will" builds more trust than a confident answer without evidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Keep the facts the same, but lead with the problem and value for customers, and with evidence, moat and economics for investors.
2. Answer hard questions honestly: admit what you do not know, use evidence, and name the risk and your plan.
3. Practise with Claude as a sceptical investor, and use its hardest questions to improve your deck.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Capstone step 3: practise your pitch with Claude as a sceptical investor, improve the deck based on its questions, and submit the final concept, demo and deck.
**Tools:** Claude (free plan); your deck from L14; your demo and validation summary from L13; the capstone rubric.
**Steps:**
1. Write a 30-second customer pitch and a 30-second investor opening.
2. Write a one-paragraph summary of your startup, with no personal or confidential data.
3. Start a conversation with Claude using the sceptical-investor prompt above, and paste your summary.
4. Answer at least five questions, one at a time, in your own words.
5. List the three weakest answers and the evidence each one needs.
6. Improve at least two slides based on this list, for example the moat or the ask.
7. Check your work against the capstone rubric and submission checklist.
8. Submit the validation summary, the demo recording and the final deck.
**What good looks like:** Two short pitches for different audiences, a practice record of at least five questions, three named weak points with planned evidence, two improved slides, and a complete capstone submission.
**Time:** about 60 minutes
