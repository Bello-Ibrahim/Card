# L02 Finding a Problem Worth Solving

Course: AI-28 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O3 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
"I want to build something with AI." Many founders start with this sentence. But customers do not pay for AI. They pay to make a problem go away. Today you will learn how to find a problem that is worth your time, and how to tell it apart from one that only sounds interesting.

## Explanation
Starting with the technology is sometimes called "a solution looking for a problem". The founder has a tool and searches for a reason to use it. The result is often a product that works well technically but that nobody needs.

Starting with the problem is the opposite. You first find a group of people, learn what makes their work or life difficult, and only then ask whether AI can help. Sometimes the best answer is not AI at all, and it is useful to learn that early.

A problem worth solving usually scores well on three tests:

- **Pain**: how much does the problem hurt? Does it cost people money, time, customers or sleep? A small annoyance is not enough.
- **Frequency**: how often does it happen? A problem that happens every day is easier to build a business around than one that happens once a year.
- **Willingness to pay**: will people spend money to solve it? A good sign is that they **already** spend money or time on a solution, even a poor one, such as a paid assistant, a spreadsheet they built themselves, or a tool they complain about.

Write each problem as a **problem statement**, a short sentence with a clear structure:

> [Specific group of people] struggle to [task] because [reason], which costs them [time, money or risk].

A specific group matters. "Small businesses" is too wide. "Owners of family-run pharmacies in secondary cities" is a group you can find, talk to and understand.

A good starting point is a group you can **reach**: people from your own job, your family business, your community or an industry you know. You will need to interview them in L03, so reachability is not a small detail.

**Analogy:** A good doctor listens to the patient's symptoms before choosing a treatment. She asks where it hurts, how often and since when. A doctor who prescribes the same medicine to every patient, because she likes that medicine, will help very few people. A founder who begins with "I want to use AI" is like that doctor. Listen to the symptoms first.

## Worked Example
Lan is a hypothetical founder in Da Nang, Vietnam. She used to work as a bookkeeper, so she knows small companies and their accountants well. She writes three problem statements and scores each from 1 (low) to 5 (high).

| Problem statement | Pain | Frequency | Willingness to pay | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small accounting firms struggle to sort clients' photographed receipts because the images are messy and unlabelled, which costs staff several hours each week. | 4 | 5 | 4 | 13 |
| Young professionals struggle to plan a monthly budget because they do not track spending, which causes stress. | 3 | 3 | 1 | 7 |
| Tour guides struggle to write social media posts because they are busy, which reduces bookings. | 2 | 3 | 2 | 7 |

The receipts problem wins. Lan's reasons: accounting firms already pay junior staff to do this work, it happens every week, and mistakes can lead to problems with tax filings. The budgeting problem is real, but many young professionals say they would not pay for help, and many free apps exist. The tour guide problem is not painful enough.

Notice that Lan has not yet mentioned a model or an app. She has a problem, a group she can reach through her old job, and early reasons to believe it matters. These scores are still her guesses. In L03 she will test them in interviews.

## Common Mistake
Founders often choose the problem that excites **them** most, or the one where AI looks most impressive. Another common error is to score "willingness to pay" from hope instead of evidence. Friends say "I would definitely pay for that" to be kind. Better evidence is what people do today: the money, time and tools they already spend on the problem. If nobody currently spends anything, ask yourself why.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with a specific group of people and their problem, not with the technology.
2. A problem worth solving is painful, frequent, and something people will pay to solve.
3. The best evidence of willingness to pay is what people already spend on the problem today.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Write 3 problem statements for people you can reach, and score each on pain, frequency and willingness to pay.
**Tools:** A free spreadsheet or a notes app. Optional: Claude (free plan) to help you make your statements more specific.
**Steps:**
1. List 3 groups of people you can reach and talk to within the next week.
2. For each group, write one problem statement using the structure: [group] struggle to [task] because [reason], which costs them [time, money or risk].
3. Score each statement from 1 to 5 on pain, frequency and willingness to pay, and add the scores.
4. Next to each score, write the evidence you have, or "guess" if you have none.
5. Optional: ask Claude, "Make these problem statements more specific and point out any that are too broad." Do not include real names or confidential details.
6. Choose the statement you will investigate in L03.
**What good looks like:** Three specific problem statements about groups you can reach. Each score has a note on evidence or is honestly marked "guess". The chosen problem has a clear reason.
**Time:** about 25 minutes

## Review Flags
- None. The founder and scores are hypothetical, and no facts, statistics or tool details need checking.
