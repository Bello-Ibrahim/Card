# L04 Writing a Fair Classroom AI Policy

Course: AI-09 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O2, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
One learner uses AI to check their grammar. Another uses it to write the whole essay. A third does not use it at all because they are not sure it is allowed. If your learners cannot tell which of these is acceptable, your policy is not clear enough yet.

## Explanation
A classroom AI policy tells learners when and how they may use AI tools in your course. Without one, learners guess, and different learners guess differently. That is unfair both to the honest learner who avoided AI and to the learner who used it in good faith.

A useful and simple structure is the **traffic-light model**. For each type of task, you choose one of three colours:

- **Red: AI not allowed.** The task checks what the learner can do alone. Example: an in-class written test, or a reflection on their own experience.
- **Amber: AI allowed with disclosure.** Learners may use AI for certain steps, and they must say how. Example: "You may use AI to brainstorm ideas or check grammar. Add a short note saying what you asked and what you changed."
- **Green: AI allowed freely.** AI use is part of the task or does not affect what is being assessed. Example: generating practice questions to revise for a test.

A good policy is **clear**. Each rule names a task type, a colour and a short reason. It uses plain language and gives examples of allowed and not allowed use.

A good policy is **fair**:

- It does not require paid tools. If a task is green or amber, every learner must be able to complete it without AI, or with a free tool available in class.
- It treats learners the same. Everyone knows the rules before the task begins.
- It explains how disclosure works, so honest learners are not punished for telling the truth.
- It does not rely only on detection tools, which can give false results (L03).

A good policy **fits your institution**. Your school, university or company may already have rules on AI use, and local rules may also apply. [REGION] Your course policy should add detail within those rules, never contradict them.

**Analogy:** A traffic-light AI policy works like real traffic lights. Drivers do not need to read a long legal document at each crossing. Red, amber and green give a quick, shared signal that everyone understands. But the lights only work if they are placed at every crossing and mean the same thing each time.

## Worked Example
Aiko Tanaka teaches academic English to first-year students at a university in Osaka, Japan. She drafts a policy for her writing course.

| Task | Colour | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Timed in-class essay | Red | Checks your own writing skills. |
| Take-home essay | Amber | AI may suggest ideas and correct grammar. Add a note on how you used it. |
| Vocabulary practice | Green | Practice only; AI can create extra exercises. |
| Reflection journal | Red | Must describe your own learning experience. |

Aiko then pastes her draft (with no student information) into an AI assistant and asks: "Find rules that are unclear or unfair to students with limited access to technology." The AI gives four suggestions:

1. Define "AI" (does a spelling checker count?). **She accepts** and adds a short definition.
2. Say what the disclosure note must include. **She accepts** and adds a two-line template.
3. Make the take-home essay red to prevent misuse. **She rejects** this; she wants students to learn responsible use, and she already includes a short conversation about each essay.
4. Offer a free option or class time for green tasks. **She accepts** and adds computer-room time.

Her final policy is still one page, and every decision is her own.

## Common Mistake
Many educators write one rule for the whole course, such as "AI is not allowed" or "AI is allowed". A single rule does not fit different tasks, and learners will not know what counts. Another mistake is to accept every AI suggestion about the policy. The AI does not know your learners, your institution's rules or your teaching goals. Use its suggestions as questions to think about, and make the final judgement yourself.

## Key Takeaways
1. A traffic-light policy gives each task type a colour: red (not allowed), amber (allowed with disclosure) or green (allowed freely), with a short reason.
2. A fair policy does not require paid tools, explains disclosure clearly and does not rely only on detection tools.
3. Your course policy must fit your institution's rules and local rules, and you decide which AI suggestions to accept.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Draft a one-page traffic-light AI policy for one course you teach, ask AI to find unclear or unfair points, and decide which suggestions to accept.
**Tools:** A word processor or Google Docs; Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); your institution's existing AI rules, if any.
**Steps:**
1. List 4 to 6 task types in your course, such as tests, projects, homework, presentations or practice.
2. Give each a colour and a one-sentence reason.
3. Add a short definition of "AI tool" and a disclosure template for amber tasks.
4. Check your draft against any institutional rules you have.
5. Paste the policy (with no learner information) into an AI assistant and ask: "Find rules that are unclear or unfair, including to learners with limited access to technology."
6. For each suggestion, write "accept" or "reject" and one reason.
**What good looks like:** A one-page policy with at least four task types, each with a colour and a reason, a definition of AI and a disclosure template. A short list shows each AI suggestion, your decision and your reason, and at least one suggestion is rejected or changed with a clear explanation.
**Time:** about 30 minutes

## Review Flags
- [REGION] Institutional and local rules on AI use in education differ by country and institution; the lesson tells learners to check their own rules instead of stating any law.
