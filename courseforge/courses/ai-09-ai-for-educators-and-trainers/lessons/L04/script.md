# L04 Writing a Fair Classroom AI Policy | Presenter Script

Course: AI-09 · Video: 5 min · Words: 711

## Hook
One learner uses AI to check their grammar. Another uses it to write the whole essay. A third does not use it at all, because they are not sure it is allowed. If your learners cannot tell which is acceptable, your policy is not clear enough.

## Explain
In the last lesson, we looked at privacy and integrity. Now we turn that into rules learners can follow. A classroom AI policy tells learners when and how they may use AI in your course. Without one, learners guess, and different learners guess differently. That is unfair to everyone.

A simple structure is the traffic-light model. For each type of task, you choose one of three colours. Red means AI is not allowed, because the task checks what the learner can do alone, like an in-class test.

Amber means AI is allowed with disclosure. Learners may use it for certain steps, such as brainstorming or checking grammar, and they add a short note on what they asked and what they changed. Green means AI is allowed freely, for example to generate practice questions before a test.

A good policy is clear. Each rule names a task type, a colour and a short reason, in plain language, with examples of allowed and not allowed use.

A good policy is also fair. It never requires paid tools, so every learner can complete green and amber tasks with a free tool or class time. Everyone knows the rules before the task begins. It explains disclosure, so honest learners are not punished for telling the truth. And it does not rely only on detection tools, which can give false results.

And a good policy fits your institution. Your school, university or company may already have rules on AI, and local rules may apply too. Your course policy adds detail within those rules, and never contradicts them.

Why traffic lights? Drivers do not read a long legal document at every crossing. Red, amber and green give a quick, shared signal. But the lights only work if they mean the same thing every time.

## Demonstrate
Let's see an example. Aiko Tanaka teaches academic English to first-year students at a university in Osaka, in Japan. She drafts a policy for her writing course.

The timed in-class essay is red, because it checks the student's own writing. The take-home essay is amber. AI may suggest ideas and correct grammar, with a note on how it was used. Vocabulary practice is green. And the reflection journal is red, because it must describe the student's own learning.

Then she pastes her draft, with no student information, into an AI assistant, and asks it to find rules that are unclear, or unfair to students with limited access to technology. It gives four suggestions.

Define what counts as AI. Does a spelling checker count? She accepts, and adds a short definition. Say what the disclosure note must include. She accepts, and adds a two-line template. Offer a free option or class time for green tasks. She accepts, and adds computer-room time.

But the AI also suggests making the take-home essay red to prevent misuse. She rejects this. She wants students to learn responsible use, and she already includes a short conversation about each essay. Her final policy is still one page, and every decision is her own.

A common mistake is one rule for the whole course, like AI is not allowed. It does not fit different tasks. Another is to accept every AI suggestion. You make the final judgement.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a traffic-light policy gives each task type a colour, red, amber or green, with a short reason. Second, a fair policy does not require paid tools, explains disclosure clearly, and does not rely only on detection tools. Third, your policy must fit your institution's rules, and you decide which AI suggestions to accept.
