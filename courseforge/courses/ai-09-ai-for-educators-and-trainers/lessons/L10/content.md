# L10 Rubrics and Feedback with AI

Course: AI-09 · Module: M3 · Objectives: O5, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Two educators mark the same piece of work. One gives it 72, the other gives it 58. Neither is careless. They simply had different ideas of what "good" meant. A clear rubric makes those ideas visible to both educators and to learners before the work begins.

## Explanation
An **analytic rubric** is a table that breaks a task into criteria and describes each level of quality. Each row is one criterion. Each column is one level, for example "Excellent", "Good", "Developing" and "Not yet".

A good rubric has three features:

1. **Criteria linked to outcomes.** Each criterion checks something the learners were taught and the outcome requires. If the outcome is "give a clear product demonstration", then "use of slides with animations" is probably not a fair criterion.
2. **Clear levels.** The difference between levels is specific and observable. "Explains 3 key features with a customer benefit for each" is clearer than "very good explanation".
3. **Learner-friendly language.** Learners should understand the rubric before they start, so they know what to aim for.

AI is useful for the first draft. Give it the task, the outcomes, the number of levels and the learners, and ask for a table. Then **test the rubric**: mark one sample piece of work with it. If you hesitate between two levels, or a criterion does not fit the work, revise the wording. Use a sample you created yourself or asked AI to create, not real learner work containing personal data.

AI can also help with **feedback**. Read the work yourself and write short notes: "clear opening; feature 2 missing benefit; spoke too fast". Then ask AI to turn the notes into clear, kind, specific comments linked to the rubric. Do not paste learner work that contains names or personal details into a public AI tool.

The **final judgement and grade always stay with the educator.** AI does not know the learner, the context or the effort behind the work, and it can be inconsistent.

**Analogy:** A rubric is like a shared map for a journey. The educator and the learner both see where they are going, what the stages are and how to tell how far they have travelled. Without the map, the learner guesses the destination and the educator judges the journey differently each time.

## Worked Example
Kateryna is a sales trainer at a software company in Kraków, Poland. At the end of her course, new staff give a 5-minute product demonstration. She asks AI for a four-level rubric with criteria linked to her three outcomes: explain key features, link each feature to a customer need, and answer a customer question.

The AI's draft has five criteria. Kateryna removes "creative slide design", because it is not one of her outcomes and not every trainee has design skills. She keeps four criteria.

She then asks AI to write a sample demonstration script with some typical weaknesses, and marks it. Two problems appear. Under "Answering questions", the levels say "good answer" and "very good answer", and she cannot decide between them. She rewrites them: "answers accurately and checks the customer is satisfied" and "answers accurately but does not check". Under "Timing", the rubric does not say what happens if the demonstration is too short, so she adds that.

For feedback, she writes her own notes after each real demonstration and asks AI to turn them into two strengths and one next step. She removes any sentence that does not match what she saw, and she decides every grade herself.

## Common Mistake
The most common mistake is to use an AI rubric without testing it. A rubric can look complete but have vague levels that make fair marking impossible. Always mark one sample with it. Another mistake is to let AI do the marking itself, by pasting learner work and asking for a grade. This risks privacy and hands a professional judgement to a tool that does not know your learners.

## Key Takeaways
1. A good analytic rubric has criteria linked to outcomes, clear observable levels and language that learners understand.
2. Test every AI-drafted rubric by marking a sample, and revise any criterion that was hard to apply.
3. AI can turn your own notes into feedback, but never paste learner work with personal data, and the final grade always stays with you.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Capstone step 2: draft a rubric for your unit's main assessment task with AI, test it on one sample piece of work, and revise any criterion that was hard to apply.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); a word processor or Google Docs.
**Steps:**
1. Write the main assessment task of your unit and its outcomes.
2. Prompt the AI: "Draft an analytic rubric for [task] for [learners]. Criteria linked to these outcomes: [...]. Four levels: Excellent, Good, Developing, Not yet. Use language the learners understand."
3. Remove or change any criterion that is not linked to an outcome.
4. Ask AI for a sample piece of work with some typical weaknesses, or write one yourself.
5. Mark the sample with the rubric and note every point where you hesitated.
6. Revise the unclear criteria and levels.
**What good looks like:** A rubric with 3 to 6 criteria, each clearly linked to an outcome, with specific differences between levels. A short note records the sample marking, at least one difficulty and how you revised the wording.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
