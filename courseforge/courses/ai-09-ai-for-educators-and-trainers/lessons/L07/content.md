# L07 Visual and Accessible Materials with Canva

Course: AI-09 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O4 · Video: 5 min (screen demo)

## Hook
A worksheet can look beautiful and still be hard to use. Light grey text on a pale background, a decorative font, or an important diagram that a screen reader cannot describe can shut some learners out. Today you will make a worksheet that looks good and works for everyone.

## Explanation
Canva is a free online design tool with templates for worksheets, slides and infographics. It includes AI features that can help draft text and designs. The names and availability of these features, and of Canva's education plans, change often, so check what your account offers. [VERSION]

Whatever tool you use, accessible materials follow the same principles. Use this **5-point accessibility checklist**:

1. **Clear fonts:** a simple, readable font, at a size that is easy to read on paper and on screen. Avoid decorative fonts for body text.
2. **Strong colour contrast:** dark text on a light background, or the reverse. Never use colour alone to show meaning (for example "the red answers are wrong"); add a word or symbol too.
3. **Alternative text (alt text) for images:** a short description that a screen reader can read aloud for learners who cannot see the image.
4. **Simple layout:** a clear reading order, headings, enough white space, and not too much on one page.
5. **Plain-language instructions:** short numbered steps, one action per step.

**Good alt text** describes what the image means for the lesson, not every detail. For a chart: "Bar chart: workplace accidents fell each year over four years." For a photo that is only decoration, you can mark it as decorative instead. AI can draft alt text if you describe the image and its purpose, but you check that it is accurate and short.

**Analogy:** An accessible worksheet is like a building with a ramp as well as stairs. The ramp helps wheelchair users, but it also helps people with prams, trolleys or heavy bags. Clear fonts, good contrast and alt text work the same way: designed for some learners, they make materials easier for everyone.

## Worked Example
Mele is a health and safety trainer at a warehouse company in Auckland, New Zealand. She needs a one-page worksheet on safe lifting for new staff, some of whom are still learning English.

The presenter can follow these steps on screen. Menu and button names may be different in the live tool. [VERSION]

1. Open Canva, search the templates for "worksheet", and choose a simple one with plenty of white space. [VERSION]
2. Replace the title with "Safe Lifting: 5 Steps".
3. Open an AI chatbot and prompt: "Write 5 numbered steps for safe lifting for new warehouse staff. Plain English, maximum 12 words per step." Mele checks the steps against her company's safety procedure and corrects one step that does not match.
4. Paste the steps into the worksheet. Select the text and change the font to a simple sans-serif font at a readable size.
5. Check contrast: change pale grey text to dark text on a white background. Replace the instruction "look at the red boxes" with "look at the boxes marked with a warning symbol".
6. Add one illustration from Canva's library showing a person lifting with bent knees. [VERSION]
7. Select the image and find the alternative text option. [VERSION] Mele types: "Worker lifting a box with bent knees and a straight back, keeping the box close to the body."
8. Mark a decorative border as decorative, if the option exists. [VERSION]
9. Download as PDF, and keep the Canva link to share with staff who use screen readers. [VERSION]

Mele then reviews the worksheet against the 5-point checklist and fixes one last problem: two steps were in one text box with an unclear reading order.

## Common Mistake
Many educators add alt text that repeats the file name ("image1.png") or says only "picture". This gives a screen-reader user no information. Another mistake is to trust an AI design because it looks professional. AI-generated layouts often use low-contrast colours or decorative fonts. Check every design against the checklist, whoever or whatever made it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Accessible materials have clear fonts, strong colour contrast, alt text for meaningful images, a simple layout and plain-language instructions.
2. Good alt text explains what an image means for the lesson; AI can draft it, but you check it for accuracy.
3. Canva's AI and education features change often, so check your live account and review every design against the checklist.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Create one worksheet or infographic in Canva for your lesson, check it against the 5-point accessibility checklist, and add alt text for every image.
**Tools:** Canva (free plan) in a browser; Claude or ChatGPT (free tier) for drafting text and alt text.
**Steps:**
1. Open Canva and choose a simple worksheet or infographic template. [VERSION]
2. Draft plain-language text with AI or write it yourself, then check every fact.
3. Set a clear font and readable size, and use dark text on a light background.
4. Add at least one meaningful image.
5. Write alt text for every meaningful image and mark decorative images as decorative, if your version allows it. [VERSION]
6. Check the design against the 5-point checklist and fix any problems.
7. Download or share the design, and save your checklist results.
**What good looks like:** A one-page design that passes all 5 checklist points, with alt text that describes what each image means in one or two sentences. Colour is never the only way to show meaning, and instructions are short numbered steps.
**Time:** about 35 minutes
