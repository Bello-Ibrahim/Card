# L01 How AI Can Support Educators and Trainers

Course: AI-09 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Think about last week. How many hours did you spend writing examples, adapting a text, making questions or typing the same feedback again and again? Some of that work can now start with an AI assistant. The question is which parts, and which parts must stay with you.

## Explanation
An AI assistant such as Claude or ChatGPT is a tool that produces text in answer to your instructions. It has learned patterns from a very large amount of writing, so it can draft many kinds of teaching material in seconds. It does not know your learners, your timetable or your institution.

AI assistants are usually helpful for five kinds of teaching and training work:

- **Planning:** suggesting activities, a sequence for a lesson, or timings for a workshop.
- **Creating examples:** word problems, case studies, role-play situations, practice sentences.
- **Adapting texts:** a simpler version for a learner who is still building reading skills, or a harder version for a fast learner.
- **Writing questions:** first drafts of quiz items, discussion questions or reflection prompts.
- **Drafting feedback:** turning your short notes into clear, polite comments.

They also have clear limits:

- **Exact facts.** An AI assistant can state wrong dates, numbers or definitions in a confident voice. You must check every fact before learners see it.
- **Your specific learners.** It does not know who needs extra support, who is new to the language, or what happened in yesterday's session.
- **Local curriculum details.** It may not know your national syllabus, your exam board's rules or your company's procedures, and it may invent them.
- **Fairness.** Its examples can repeat stereotypes or come from only one culture. You will learn to check for this in L08.

**Analogy:** An AI assistant is like a teaching assistant who works very fast but has never met your class. You can ask them for ten example problems before lunch, and they will deliver. But they do not know that half your group struggles with fractions, or that your company changed its refund policy last month. You give the direction, and you check the work before it reaches learners.

A simple way to decide what to give the AI is to sort your tasks into three groups:

1. **AI can draft:** the AI makes a first version and you edit it. Example: ten practice questions.
2. **AI can help me check:** you make the work and ask the AI to look for gaps or unclear wording. Example: asking whether your instructions are easy to follow.
3. **Keep fully human:** the task needs your knowledge of the learners, your judgement, or personal data. Example: deciding a final grade, or talking to a learner about a difficult situation.

## Worked Example
Mariana teaches history at a secondary school in Curitiba, Brazil. On Sunday evening she lists her tasks for the coming week and sorts them.

- Write a starter activity about the causes of a historical event: **AI can draft.** She will ask for five options and choose one.
- Create a timeline worksheet: **AI can draft**, but she will check every date against the textbook, because dates are exact facts.
- Write instructions for a group project: **AI can help me check.** She writes them, then asks the AI which steps might confuse a 14-year-old.
- Mark 30 essays: **keep fully human.** The essays contain learners' names and personal writing, and the grade is her professional judgement.
- Reply to a parent's email about a learner's progress: **keep fully human.** It involves personal information and a relationship.

Mariana notices that the "AI can draft" tasks are the repetitive ones that take time but not much judgement. The "keep fully human" tasks are the ones where her knowledge of each learner matters most.

## Common Mistake
Many educators believe that if AI writes something quickly and fluently, it is probably correct and ready to use. Fluent language is not the same as accurate content. An AI assistant can produce a well-written worksheet with a wrong definition or a question that has two correct answers. Treat every AI output as a first draft from an assistant who has never met your learners: useful, but always checked and edited by you.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI assistants can help with planning, creating examples, adapting texts, writing questions and drafting feedback.
2. Their main limits are exact facts, knowledge of your learners and local curriculum details, so you always check and adapt their work.
3. Sort your tasks into "AI can draft", "AI can help me check" and "keep fully human", and keep tasks with personal data or final judgement fully human.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** List 10 tasks from your teaching or training week and sort them into three groups.
**Tools:** Pen and paper, or any notes app. Optional: Claude or ChatGPT (free tier) to compare ideas.
**Steps:**
1. Write down 10 tasks you did in a normal week of teaching or training, for example "prepare slides", "write quiz", "answer learner emails".
2. Next to each task, write "AI can draft", "AI can help me check" or "keep fully human".
3. Choose one task and write two or three sentences explaining your choice.
4. Check that every task involving learners' names, grades or personal information is in "keep fully human" or has the personal data removed first.
5. Optional: describe your list to an AI assistant, without any learner details, and ask which tasks it would sort differently. Decide whether you agree.
**What good looks like:** A list of 10 real tasks, each with one label. Repetitive creation tasks are usually "AI can draft", and tasks with personal data, grades or sensitive conversations are "keep fully human". The explanation gives a clear reason linked to accuracy, learners or privacy.
**Time:** about 15 minutes
