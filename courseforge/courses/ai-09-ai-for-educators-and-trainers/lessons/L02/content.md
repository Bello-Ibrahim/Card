# L02 Prompting for Teaching Tasks

Course: AI-09 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O3 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Ask an AI assistant for "an activity about photosynthesis" and you will get something. It may be too long, too easy, or written for a different age group. The problem is usually not the AI. It is the missing information in the request.

## Explanation
A prompt is the instruction you type into an AI assistant. The assistant only knows what you tell it. When a prompt is short and general, the answer is general too. When the prompt describes your real teaching situation, the answer becomes much more useful.

A good teaching prompt has four parts:

1. **The learners:** their age or role, their level, and the language they learn in. For example, "15-year-olds in their second year of secondary science, most learning in English as a second language", or "new call-centre staff in their first week, no technical background".
2. **The learning outcome:** what learners should be able to do at the end. Use an action verb: "explain", "compare", "calculate", "apply". For example, "learners can explain why plants need light to make food".
3. **The time available:** "a 40-minute lesson", "a 10-minute warm-up", "a 90-minute workshop".
4. **The format you want:** a table, numbered steps, a worksheet with answer key, three options to choose from, a maximum length.

You can also add useful extras: materials you have (or do not have), what learners already know, and anything to avoid.

After the first answer, you can continue the conversation. You do not need to start again. Say what to change: "Make step 2 shorter", "Use examples from farming instead of cooking", "Give the instructions at a simpler reading level".

**Analogy:** Prompting is like briefing a substitute teacher who arrives five minutes before your class. If you say only "Teach photosynthesis", they will do something general. If you say "Year 10, mixed level, 40 minutes, they must be able to explain why plants need light, and there is no projector", they can plan something that works for your room.

Remember the privacy rule from the start: describe your learners as a group, never as individuals. Do not include names, grades, health details or other personal information in a prompt.

## Worked Example
Ngozi teaches science at a secondary school in Abuja, Nigeria. She needs an activity on photosynthesis for tomorrow.

Her first prompt is:

```
Give me an activity about photosynthesis.
```

The answer suggests a two-hour experiment with equipment she does not have, and the instructions are written for university students.

She tries again with the four parts:

```
Learners: 30 students aged 14-15, mixed ability, learning
science in English as a second language.
Outcome: students can explain in 2-3 sentences why a plant
needs light, water and carbon dioxide to make food.
Time: 40 minutes, no lab equipment, one board.
Format: a timed plan in a table (step, minutes, what the
teacher does, what students do), plus 3 check questions
with answers. Use short sentences.
```

The new answer is a 40-minute plan: a 5-minute question about why plants on a dark windowsill grow poorly, a 15-minute pair activity sorting cards with "inputs" and "outputs", a 10-minute diagram, and 10 minutes for the check questions.

Ngozi still edits it. She replaces one example with a local crop her students know, and she checks the science in the answer key against her textbook. She then sends a follow-up prompt: "Rewrite the card instructions for students who are still building their English reading skills." The activity now fits her class.

## Common Mistake
A common mistake is to accept the first answer, or to give up and say "AI is not useful for my subject". The first answer is a starting point. Most of the value comes from adding the missing details and asking for one or two specific changes. Another mistake is to add detail by pasting in learner information such as a class list or grades. Add detail about the group and the task, never about identifiable individuals.

## Key Takeaways
1. A strong teaching prompt includes the learners, the learning outcome, the time available and the format you want.
2. Improve results by continuing the conversation with specific changes instead of starting again.
3. Describe your learners as a group, never by name or with personal data, and always check facts and fit before you use the output.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Write a 4-part prompt for an activity you will teach soon, test it, improve it once and compare the two results.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); a notes app or document to save both prompts and results.
**Steps:**
1. Choose a real activity you will teach in the next two weeks.
2. First, type a one-line prompt, such as "Give me an activity about [topic]". Save the result.
3. Now write a 4-part prompt: learners (age or role, level, language), outcome (with an action verb), time, and format. Do not include any names or personal data.
4. Run the new prompt and save the result.
5. Send one follow-up that asks for a specific change, such as a simpler reading level or a local example.
6. Compare the results in a short note: what changed, what is now more useful, and what you would still edit yourself.
**What good looks like:** Two saved prompts and results. The second prompt clearly states all four parts. Your note names at least two concrete improvements (for example, correct timing and right level) and at least one thing you still need to check or change, such as a fact or an example.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
