# L02 Prompting for Teaching Tasks | Presenter Script

Course: AI-09 · Video: 5 min · Words: 684

## Hook
Ask an AI assistant for an activity about photosynthesis, and you will get something. It may be too long, too easy, or written for a different age group. The problem is usually not the AI. It is the missing information in the request.

## Explain
In the last lesson, we sorted teaching tasks into what AI can draft and what stays human. Now we learn how to ask well. A prompt is the instruction you type into an AI assistant. The assistant only knows what you tell it. A short, general prompt gets a general answer.

A good teaching prompt has four parts. First, the learners: their age or role, their level, and the language they learn in. That could be fifteen-year-olds learning science in their second language, or new call-centre staff in their first week.

Second, the learning outcome: what learners should be able to do at the end, with an action verb like explain, compare or apply. Third, the time available, such as a forty-minute lesson or a ninety-minute workshop. And fourth, the format you want, for example a table, numbered steps, or three options to choose from.

After the first answer, you do not need to start again. Continue the conversation, and say exactly what to change. For example, make step two shorter, use examples from farming, or give the instructions at a simpler reading level.

Think of prompting like briefing a substitute teacher who arrives five minutes before your class. If you only say teach photosynthesis, they will do something general.

But if you tell them the year group, the mixed level, the forty minutes, the outcome, and that there is no projector, they can plan something that works for your room.

And one rule from the start. Describe your learners as a group, never as individuals. Do not include names, grades, health details or any other personal information in a prompt.

## Demonstrate
Let's see it in practice. Ngozi teaches science at a secondary school in Abuja, in Nigeria. She needs an activity on photosynthesis for tomorrow.

Her first prompt is one short line asking for an activity about photosynthesis. The answer suggests a two-hour experiment, with equipment she does not have, and instructions written for university students.

So she tries again with the four parts. Thirty students aged fourteen to fifteen, mixed ability, learning science in English as a second language. The outcome: they can explain in two or three sentences why a plant needs light, water and carbon dioxide. Forty minutes, no lab equipment. And a timed plan in a table, plus three check questions with answers.

The new answer is a forty-minute plan. A five-minute question about why plants on a dark windowsill grow poorly. A fifteen-minute pair activity sorting cards into inputs and outputs. Ten minutes for a diagram, and ten minutes for the check questions.

Ngozi still edits it. She replaces one example with a local crop her students know, and she checks the science in the answer key against her textbook. Then she asks for the card instructions to be rewritten for students still building their English reading skills. Now the activity fits her class.

A common mistake is to accept the first answer, or to give up and decide AI is not useful for your subject. The first answer is only a starting point. And never add detail by pasting a class list or grades.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a strong teaching prompt includes the learners, the learning outcome, the time available and the format you want. Second, improve results by continuing the conversation with specific changes. Third, describe learners as a group, never by name, and always check facts and fit before you use the output.
