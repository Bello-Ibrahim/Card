# L03 Privacy, Integrity and Ethics | Presenter Script

Course: AI-09 · Video: 5 min · Words: 698

## Hook
You have sixty reports to mark. Pasting them into a chatbot for grades would save hours. But first, ask two questions. Whose information is in those reports? And where does it go when you paste it?

## Explain
Last time, we learned to write better prompts. Today, we look at the responsibility side of AI in teaching: learner privacy, academic integrity, and fairness.

Start with privacy. When you type or paste something into an AI tool, it leaves your device and is processed by the company that runs the tool. Depending on the tool and your settings, it may be stored, or used to improve the product.

So the basic rule is simple. Never paste learners' names, grades, health information, contact details, photos or other personal data into a public AI tool. That includes essays and reports that contain names or personal stories.

If you want AI help with learner work, remove everything that could identify a person, or use only your own notes. And if your institution provides an approved AI tool with extra data protection, use it and follow its rules.

Rules on learner data, and on whether minors may use AI tools at all, differ between countries, regions and institutions. AI companies also set their own minimum ages and terms. So before you ask learners to use any AI tool directly, check your institution's policy, your local rules, and the tool's current terms.

Here is a picture to remember. A public AI tool is like a busy shared office photocopier. It is useful, but you would not leave confidential learner files on it. You remove the private pages first, or you use a secure machine.

Next, academic integrity. Learners can use AI to produce work that is not their own, and some institutions use detection tools to find this. Be careful. Detection tools can give false results, including marking a learner's own writing as AI-generated. So a detector score should never be the only evidence of misconduct.

Task design often helps more than detection. Tasks are harder to hand to AI when they connect to something that happened in your class or workplace, ask for drafts or steps, include a short conversation about the work, or ask for reflection on a real experience.

Finally, fairness. Learners do not all have the same devices, internet or paid tools. If a task needs AI, make sure everyone can do it in class, or with a free option.

## Demonstrate
Let's meet Ali. He lectures in business studies at a university in Lahore, in Pakistan, and he wants help with feedback on forty-five short case-study answers.

His first idea is to paste all forty-five answers, with names and ID numbers, into a free chatbot. He changes his plan. He reads the answers himself and writes short notes, such as good use of the case, but no clear recommendation. Then he asks the AI to turn his notes into two polite, specific feedback sentences each.

Later, a detector report says one essay is ninety percent AI. Ali does not treat this as proof. He asks the learner to explain how they wrote it and to show their notes. The learner explains the argument clearly and shows early drafts, so Ali closes the matter.

A common mistake is to think that removing the name is enough. An ID number, a rare medical condition, a small class or a personal story can still point to one person. Remove all of it.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, never paste learners' personal data into a public AI tool. Remove identifying details, or use your own notes. Second, detection tools can give false results, so never use them as the only evidence. Task design and conversation work better. Third, rules differ by country and institution, so check local rules, your policy and the tool's terms first.
