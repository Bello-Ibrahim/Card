# L05 Planning Lessons with Clear Outcomes

Course: AI-09 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O3 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Have you ever finished a lesson where the activities were enjoyable, the learners were busy, and yet you could not say what they had actually learned? A clear outcome at the start of planning prevents that problem, and it also makes AI much more useful.

## Explanation
Many educators start planning with activities: "I will do a group discussion, then a video." A stronger method is to **start from the learning outcome and work backwards**. This is sometimes called backward design.

It has three steps:

1. **Write the outcome.** What will learners be able to do at the end? Use an action verb that you can observe: "calculate", "explain", "handle", "compare". Avoid verbs you cannot see, such as "understand" or "know". For example: "By the end of the workshop, participants can respond to a complaining customer using the four-step response model."
2. **Decide the evidence.** How will learners show they have reached the outcome? This is your check for understanding. For example: a short role-play, three questions, a completed example or an exit ticket.
3. **Plan the activities.** Now choose activities that prepare learners for that evidence. Every activity should move learners towards the outcome.

AI can help at each step. You can ask it to turn a vague aim into an observable outcome, to suggest ways to check understanding, and to propose an activity sequence with timings. A useful plan format is a table with the step, the minutes, what the educator does and what learners do.

But AI does not know your room, your group or your constraints. You adapt its plan. Typical changes include: adjusting timings to your real group, replacing examples with ones from your context, removing activities that need equipment you do not have, and adding support for learners who need it.

**Analogy:** Backward design is like planning a journey. You choose the destination first, then decide how you will know you have arrived, and only then choose the roads. An AI assistant is like a route planner. It can suggest good roads quickly, but it does not know that one bridge is closed today. You check the route against what you know.

## Worked Example
Tomás is a corporate trainer at an insurance company in Madrid, Spain. He must run a 60-minute workshop for new customer-service staff on handling complaints.

He writes his outcome first: "Participants can respond to a complaining customer using four steps: listen, acknowledge, solve, confirm." His evidence is a 3-minute role-play with a checklist.

He prompts the AI with the four parts from L02: learners (12 new staff, no customer-service experience), outcome, time (60 minutes, one room, no projector) and format (a timed table plus a role-play checklist). The AI suggests:

| Step | Minutes | Trainer | Participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening question | 5 | Asks about a bad service experience | Share in pairs |
| Model | 10 | Explains and demonstrates 4 steps | Watch and note steps |
| Practice with scripts | 15 | Gives scripted scenarios | Practise in pairs |
| Role-play | 20 | Observes with checklist | Role-play in threes |
| Review | 10 | Leads discussion | Name one thing to improve |

Tomás makes three changes. First, he replaces the AI's retail examples with real insurance situations, such as a delayed claim, with all customer details invented. Second, he moves 5 minutes from the opening to the role-play, because his groups always need more practice time. Third, he adds a printed card with the four steps for participants who find it hard to remember a sequence under pressure. The plan now fits his outcome, his time and his learners.

## Common Mistake
The most common mistake is to accept an AI plan that contains attractive activities that do not lead to the outcome. For example, the AI may suggest a creative poster task in a workshop where the outcome is to handle a spoken conversation. Check each activity with one question: "Does this help learners produce the evidence I planned?" If not, remove or replace it. A second mistake is to keep the AI's timings without checking them against your real group.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start from an observable learning outcome, then decide the evidence of learning, and only then plan the activities.
2. AI can suggest outcomes, checks for understanding, activities and timings quickly, especially in a timed table format.
3. Always adapt the plan to your context: timings, examples, equipment and support, and remove activities that do not lead to the outcome.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Use AI to draft a lesson plan with a clear outcome, a timed activity sequence and a short check for understanding, then change at least 3 things to fit your learners.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); a word processor or Google Docs.
**Steps:**
1. Choose one lesson or session you will teach soon.
2. Write one outcome with an observable action verb.
3. Decide how learners will show the outcome (your check for understanding).
4. Prompt the AI with the learners (as a group, no personal data), the outcome, the evidence, the time and the format "timed table with step, minutes, educator, learners".
5. Check every activity against the outcome and remove any that do not help.
6. Make at least 3 changes for your context and mark each change in a different colour or with a note.
**What good looks like:** A one-page plan with one observable outcome, a timed sequence that adds up to the real lesson length, and a clear check for understanding. At least 3 changes are marked, each with a short reason such as "local example", "more practice time" or "extra support".
**Time:** about 30 minutes
