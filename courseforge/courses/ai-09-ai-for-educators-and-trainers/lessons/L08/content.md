# L08 Checking AI Materials for Accuracy and Bias

Course: AI-09 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
An AI-generated story reads well, the questions look fine, and the layout is ready to print. But if every character comes from one culture, every doctor is a man, or one date is wrong, your learners will notice. Some will learn the error. Others will feel that the material is not for them.

## Explanation
AI assistants learn from large amounts of existing text. That text contains errors, old information and the same stereotypes that exist in society. So AI materials can contain:

- **Factual errors:** wrong dates, numbers, definitions or explanations, often written confidently.
- **Outdated information:** rules, figures or practices that have changed.
- **Cultural stereotypes:** for example, certain jobs always linked to one gender, or some countries only described through poverty or conflict.
- **Exclusion:** examples, names, foods, holidays or family types from only one culture, or situations some learners cannot relate to.
- **Poor fit:** content at the wrong level, or not matching your curriculum or your company's procedures.

Use a **5-part review checklist** on every AI material before learners see it:

1. **Facts:** Is every fact, number and definition correct? Check against a reliable source, such as your textbook, curriculum document or official procedure.
2. **Level:** Is the language and difficulty right for these learners?
3. **Cultural fairness:** Are people, places and cultures shown respectfully and without stereotypes?
4. **Representation:** Do the examples include a range of names, genders, backgrounds, abilities and family types? Could all your learners see themselves in this material?
5. **Curriculum fit:** Does it match your outcome and your syllabus or procedure?

For quiz questions, add one more check: does each question have exactly one correct answer? You will practise this in L09.

You can also ask the AI to review its own work, for example: "List any stereotypes or one-sided examples in this text." This can find some problems, but it is not a replacement for your own review, because the same patterns that created the problem can also hide it.

**Analogy:** Reviewing AI materials is like checking food before serving it to guests. It may look good on the plate, but you still check that it is cooked, that it is fresh, and that nothing in it will harm a guest with an allergy. You check because you are the one serving it.

## Worked Example
Leila teaches Year 4 at a primary school in Amman, Jordan. She asks an AI assistant for a short story about a class preparing a science fair, with five comprehension questions.

She reviews it with the checklist:

- **Facts:** The story says that plants use oxygen to make their food. This is wrong: in photosynthesis they use carbon dioxide, so she corrects it.
- **Level:** Two sentences are too long for 9-year-olds, so she splits them.
- **Cultural fairness and representation:** All six children's names come from one culture, and the only character who is good at science is a boy. Leila changes the names to reflect the range of backgrounds in her school and in the wider world, and makes a girl the child who explains the experiment.
- **Curriculum fit:** The story mentions a topic her class will not study until next year. She removes it.
- **Questions:** Question 4 asks "Why did the team win?", but the story gives two possible reasons. She rewrites it so that there is one clear answer.

She records each change in a small table so she can see patterns and write better prompts next time, such as "use a range of names from different cultures".

## Common Mistake
Many educators check only the facts and stop there. Bias and exclusion are harder to see, especially when the material matches our own background. Read the material as a learner from a different background would, and ask: "Who is missing? Who is always in the same role?" A second mistake is to think that because you asked for "diverse examples" in the prompt, the result is automatically fair. Always check the output.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI materials can contain factual errors, outdated information, stereotypes and examples that exclude some learners.
2. Use a 5-part review before learners see any material: facts, level, cultural fairness, representation and curriculum fit.
3. AI can help point out possible problems, but it does not replace your own review, and a good prompt does not guarantee a fair result.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Review an AI-generated reading passage and 5 questions on a topic you know well, record every error or bias you find, and write a corrected version.
**Tools:** Claude or ChatGPT (free tier); a reliable source for your topic, such as a textbook or official procedure; a word processor.
**Steps:**
1. Ask an AI assistant for a 200-word reading passage and 5 questions on a topic you know well, for a group of learners you teach.
2. Review the passage and questions with the 5-part checklist.
3. Check every question has exactly one correct answer.
4. Record each problem in a table: location, type (fact, level, fairness, representation, fit), and your correction.
5. Write the corrected passage and questions.
**What good looks like:** A table with every problem found, including at least one fact or level issue and a clear note on representation, even if the note is "no problems found, because...". The corrected version fixes each recorded problem and still matches the outcome.
**Time:** about 30 minutes

## Review Flags
- None. The review checklist is general guidance, the example is hypothetical, and the science fact used (plants take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis) is standard school content.
