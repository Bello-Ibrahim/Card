## L05 Planning Lessons with Clear Outcomes

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M2_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (675 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. Backward design is presented as a general method with a hypothetical example.

```text
Have you ever finished a lesson where the activities were fun, the learners were busy, and yet you could not say what they had actually learned? A clear outcome at the start of planning prevents that. It also makes AI much more useful.

Welcome to week two, where we design lessons and materials. Many educators start planning with activities. I will do a group discussion, then a video. A stronger method is to start from the learning outcome and work backwards. This is sometimes called backward design.

Step one, write the outcome. What will learners be able to do at the end? Use an action verb you can observe, like calculate, explain, handle or compare. Avoid verbs you cannot see, such as understand or know.

Step two, decide the evidence. How will learners show they have reached the outcome? This is your check for understanding, such as a short role-play, three questions, or an exit ticket. Step three, plan the activities. Every activity should prepare learners for that evidence.

AI can help at each step. It can turn a vague aim into an observable outcome, suggest ways to check understanding, and propose activities with timings. A useful format is a table with the step, the minutes, what the educator does, and what learners do.

But AI does not know your room, your group or your limits. So you adapt its plan. Adjust timings to your real group, replace examples with ones from your context, remove anything that needs equipment you do not have, and add support where learners need it.

Backward design is like planning a journey. You choose the destination first, then decide how you will know you have arrived, and only then choose the roads.

An AI assistant is like a route planner. It suggests good roads quickly, but it does not know that one bridge is closed today. You check the route against what you know.

Let's move from the classroom to the workplace. Tomás is a corporate trainer at an insurance company in Madrid, in Spain. He must run a sixty-minute workshop for new customer-service staff on handling complaints.

He writes his outcome first. Participants can respond to a complaining customer in four steps: listen, acknowledge, solve and confirm. His evidence is a three-minute role-play, marked with a checklist.

Then he prompts the AI with the four parts from lesson two. Twelve new staff with no customer-service experience, his outcome, sixty minutes in one room with no projector, and a timed table plus a checklist. The AI suggests five steps. An opening question, a model of the four steps, practice with scripts, a role-play in groups of three, and a review.

Tomás makes three changes. He replaces the AI's retail examples with insurance situations, like a delayed claim, with every customer detail invented. He moves five minutes from the opening to the role-play, because his groups need more practice. And he adds a card with the four steps, for people who struggle to remember a sequence under pressure. The plan now fits his outcome, his time and his learners.

A common mistake is to keep attractive activities that do not lead to the outcome, like a poster task when the outcome is a spoken conversation. Ask one question of every activity. Does this help learners produce the evidence I planned?

Let's recap. First, start from an observable outcome, then decide the evidence, and only then plan the activities. Second, AI can suggest outcomes, checks, activities and timings quickly, especially in a timed table. Third, always adapt the plan to your context, and remove activities that do not lead to the outcome.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, write one observable outcome for a lesson you will teach soon, and use AI to draft a timed plan. Describe your learners as a group, with no personal data. Then change at least three things to fit your learners. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at differentiating materials for different learners. See you there.
```

## L06 Differentiating Materials for Different Learners

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (692 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The flood causes are general, well-established geography.

```text
In one room, you may have a learner who reads fluently, one who is still learning the language of instruction, and one who finishes early and waits. One worksheet cannot serve all three. Making three versions by hand takes hours. With AI, first drafts take minutes.

Last time, we planned lessons from a clear outcome. Now we adapt the materials. Differentiation means giving learners different routes to the same learning outcome. The content and the outcome stay the same. What changes is the level of support or challenge.

A common approach uses three versions. The support version has shorter sentences, simpler words, key terms explained, and sentence starters or a glossary. The core version is the standard one for most of the group. The challenge version has fewer hints and an extra question that asks learners to apply, compare or evaluate.

AI can also help learners studying in a second language. It can simplify the reading level while keeping subject terms, or add a key-word list in their first language. Check any translation with a fluent speaker if you can, because AI translations can contain errors.

Plain language, short paragraphs and clear headings help many learners, including those with reading difficulties such as dyslexia. You can ask the AI directly for a simpler reading level, with sentences under fifteen words, and name the terms it must keep.

Here is the key rule. Every version must keep the same key content and outcome. AI simplification can remove important ideas or introduce errors, so compare every version against a short list of key points.

Picture different paths up to the same viewpoint on a hill. One is long and gentle, with rails to hold. One is the normal path. One is steep and direct. Everyone reaches the same view. A path to a different hill is a different lesson.

Be careful with labels too. Easy and hard can make learners feel judged. Neutral names, like colours or version A, B and C, are kinder, and you can let learners move between versions.

Let's see it in a classroom. Nadia teaches geography at a secondary school in Dhaka, in Bangladesh. Her thirteen-year-olds are studying why rivers flood. Some read well in English, and several are still building their reading skills.

First, she writes her four key points. Heavy rain increases river water. Melting snow upstream adds water. Hard or paved ground makes water run off faster. And floods can damage homes and farms, but also bring fertile soil.

She pastes her own two-hundred-word text into the AI, and asks for three versions. The support version keeps the terms run-off and upstream, with a short explanation and a glossary. The challenge version adds a question comparing two causes. And all versions must keep her four points.

Then she checks each version against her list. The support version has dropped the point about fertile soil, so she adds it back in one simple sentence. The challenge version says floods are always caused by rain. That is wrong, so she corrects it. She names the versions Blue, Green and Orange, and lets learners change version if theirs feels too easy or too hard.

A common mistake is to simplify the content instead of the language. A support version without the difficult ideas teaches less, not differently. Keep the ideas, and change the route. A second mistake is to trust the challenge version without checking it. Adding complexity is also where AI adds errors.

Let's recap. First, differentiation gives learners different routes to the same outcome, with support, core and challenge versions. Second, every version must keep the same key points, so check each one against your list and correct anything missing or wrong. Third, adjust reading level and language support, and use neutral names for your versions.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, take one short text you wrote, remove any learner names, and ask AI for support, core and challenge versions. Check all three against your key points, and correct any errors. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we create visual and accessible materials with Canva. See you there.
```

## L07 Visual and Accessible Materials with Canva

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (699 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A worksheet can look beautiful and still be hard to use. Light grey text on a pale background, a decorative font, or a diagram a screen reader cannot describe can shut some learners out. Today, you make a worksheet that looks good and works for everyone.

Last time, we adapted texts for different learners. Now we make materials visual and accessible. Canva is a free online design tool with templates for worksheets, slides and infographics. It has AI features that help draft text and designs, but these features change often, so check what your own account offers.

Whatever tool you use, accessible materials follow the same five points. One, clear fonts: simple and readable, never decorative for body text. Two, strong colour contrast, like dark text on a light background. And never use colour alone to show meaning. Add a word or a symbol too.

Three, alternative text, or alt text, for images. That is a short description a screen reader reads aloud for learners who cannot see the image. Four, a simple layout, with a clear reading order, headings and white space. And five, plain-language instructions, in short numbered steps with one action each.

Good alt text describes what the image means for the lesson, not every detail. For a chart, you might write: bar chart showing that workplace accidents fell each year over four years. A purely decorative image can be marked as decorative instead. AI can draft alt text if you describe the image and its purpose, but you check it.

Think of a building with a ramp as well as stairs. The ramp helps wheelchair users, but also people with prams or heavy bags. Accessible design works the same way. It is designed for some learners, and it helps everyone.

Now let's build one. Mele is a health and safety trainer at a warehouse company in Auckland, in New Zealand. She needs a one-page worksheet on safe lifting for new staff, some of whom are still learning English.

She opens Canva, searches the templates for worksheet, and chooses a simple design with plenty of white space. Then she changes the title to Safe Lifting, Five Steps.

Next, she asks an AI chatbot for five numbered steps for safe lifting, in plain English, with a maximum of twelve words per step. She shares no staff names or personal details. She checks the steps against her company's safety procedure, and she corrects one step that does not match.

She pastes the steps into the worksheet, and changes the font to a simple sans-serif at a readable size. Then she checks contrast. She changes pale grey text to dark text on white. And the instruction look at the red boxes becomes look at the boxes marked with a warning symbol.

She adds an illustration from the library, showing a person lifting with bent knees. She selects the image and finds the alternative text option. She types: worker lifting a box with bent knees and a straight back, keeping the box close to the body. Then she marks the decorative border as decorative.

Finally, she reviews the page against the five-point checklist. She finds one last problem. Two steps are in one text box, with an unclear reading order, so she splits them. She downloads a PDF, and keeps the Canva link for staff who use screen readers.

A common mistake is alt text that says only picture, or repeats the file name. Another is trusting an AI design because it looks professional. Check every design against the checklist.

Let's recap. First, accessible materials have clear fonts, strong contrast, alt text, a simple layout and plain-language instructions. Second, good alt text explains what an image means for the lesson. AI can draft it, but you check it. Third, Canva's features change often, so check your live account, and review every design against the checklist.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, create one worksheet or infographic in Canva for your lesson. Check it against the five-point checklist, and add alt text for every meaningful image. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we check AI materials for accuracy and bias. See you there.
```

## L08 Checking AI Materials for Accuracy and Bias

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (708 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The science fact used (plants take in carbon dioxide for photosynthesis) is standard school content.

```text
An AI-generated story reads well, and the questions look fine. But if every character comes from one culture, every doctor is a man, or one date is wrong, your learners will notice. Some will learn the error. Others will feel it is not for them.

Last time, we made materials accessible. Now we check them for accuracy and bias. AI assistants learn from large amounts of existing text. That text contains errors, old information, and the same stereotypes that exist in society. So AI materials can contain them too.

Watch for five kinds of problem. Factual errors, often written confidently. Outdated information, like rules that have changed. Cultural stereotypes, such as certain jobs always linked to one gender. Exclusion, where names, foods or family types come from only one culture. And poor fit, with the wrong level or content that does not match your curriculum or procedures.

So use a five-part review on every AI material before learners see it. Facts: is every fact, number and definition correct? Check against a reliable source, like your textbook or an official procedure. Level: is the language right for these learners?

Cultural fairness: are people and places shown respectfully, without stereotypes? Representation: do the examples include a range of names, genders, backgrounds, abilities and family types? Could all your learners see themselves here? And curriculum fit: does it match your outcome and syllabus? For quiz questions, also check there is exactly one correct answer.

You can also ask the AI to list stereotypes or one-sided examples in its own text. This can find some problems. But it does not replace your own review, because the same patterns that created a problem can also hide it.

Reviewing AI materials is like checking food before you serve it to guests. It may look good on the plate, but you still check it is cooked, fresh, and safe for a guest with an allergy. You check because you are the one serving it.

Let's go back to a primary classroom. Leila teaches Year Four at a primary school in Amman, in Jordan. She asks an AI assistant for a short story about a class preparing a science fair, with five comprehension questions.

She starts with facts. The story says plants use oxygen to make their food. That is wrong. In photosynthesis they use carbon dioxide, so she corrects it. Then level. Two sentences are too long for nine-year-olds, so she splits them.

Next, fairness and representation. All six children's names come from one culture, and the only child who is good at science is a boy. Leila changes the names to reflect the range of backgrounds in her school and in the wider world. And she makes a girl the child who explains the experiment.

Curriculum fit. The story mentions a topic her class will not study until next year, so she removes it. And question four asks why the team won, but the story gives two possible reasons. She rewrites it, so there is one clear answer.

She records each change in a small table, so she can see patterns and write better prompts next time. For example, use a range of names from different cultures.

A common mistake is to check only the facts and stop there. Bias is harder to see, especially when the material matches our own background. So ask: who is missing, and who is always in the same role? And asking for diverse examples does not guarantee a fair result.

Let's recap. First, AI materials can contain factual errors, outdated information, stereotypes, and examples that exclude some learners. Second, use a five-part review before learners see anything: facts, level, cultural fairness, representation and curriculum fit. Third, AI can point out possible problems, but it does not replace your review, and a good prompt does not guarantee a fair result.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, ask AI for a short reading passage and five questions on a topic you know well, describing your learners only as a group. Review them with the checklist, record every error or bias, and write a corrected version. Next week, your capstone begins. In the next lesson, we build quizzes with AI and Google Forms. See you there.
```
