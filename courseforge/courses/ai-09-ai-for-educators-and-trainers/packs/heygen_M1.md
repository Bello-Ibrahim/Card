## L01 How AI Can Support Educators and Trainers

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (735 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. Uses and limits are general, and the example is hypothetical.

```text
Think about last week. How many hours did you spend writing examples, adapting a text, making questions, or typing the same feedback over again? Some of that work can now start with an AI assistant. The question is which parts, and which parts must stay with you.

Hi, and welcome to AI for Educators and Trainers. Whether you teach in a school, lecture at a university or run training at work, this course is for you. In this first lesson, we look at where AI really helps in teaching work, and where it does not.

An AI assistant, such as Claude or ChatGPT, is a tool that produces text in answer to your instructions. It has learned patterns from a very large amount of writing, so it can draft many kinds of teaching material in seconds. But it does not know your learners, your timetable or your institution.

AI assistants are usually helpful for five kinds of work. Planning, such as suggesting activities or timings for a workshop. Creating examples, like word problems or role-play situations. Adapting texts to a simpler or harder level. Writing first drafts of questions. And drafting feedback, by turning your short notes into clear, polite comments.

They also have clear limits. First, exact facts. An AI assistant can state a wrong date or definition in a confident voice. Second, your specific learners. It does not know who needs extra support. Third, local details, like your national syllabus or your company's procedures. It may invent them. And fourth, fairness, because its examples can repeat stereotypes.

Here is a simple way to picture it. An AI assistant is like a teaching assistant who works very fast but has never met your class. You can ask for ten example problems before lunch, and they will deliver.

But they do not know that half your group struggles with fractions, or that your company changed its refund policy last month. You give the direction, and you check the work before it reaches learners.

A simple way to decide what to give the AI is to sort your tasks into three groups. AI can draft, where the AI makes a first version and you edit it. AI can help me check, where you do the work and ask the AI to look for gaps. And keep fully human, for tasks that need your judgement, your knowledge of the learners, or personal data.

Let's see this in practice. Mariana teaches history at a secondary school in Curitiba, in Brazil. On Sunday evening, she lists her tasks for the coming week and sorts them into the three groups.

A starter activity about the causes of a historical event? AI can draft. She will ask for five options and choose one. A timeline worksheet? AI can draft too, but she will check every date against the textbook, because dates are exact facts.

Instructions for a group project? AI can help her check. She writes them herself, then asks the AI which steps might confuse a fourteen-year-old.

Marking thirty essays? Keep fully human. The essays contain learners' names and personal writing, and the grade is her professional judgement. A reply to a parent about a learner's progress? Also fully human, because it involves personal information and a relationship.

Mariana notices a pattern. The draft tasks are the repetitive ones that take time but not much judgement. The fully human tasks are the ones where her knowledge of each learner matters most.

One common mistake is to believe that if AI writes something quickly and fluently, it is probably correct. Fluent language is not the same as accurate content. A well-written worksheet can still have a wrong definition, or a question with two correct answers. So treat every output as a first draft.

Let's recap. First, AI assistants can help with planning, examples, adapting texts, writing questions and drafting feedback. Second, their main limits are exact facts, knowledge of your learners and local details, so you always check and adapt their work. Third, sort your tasks into three groups, and keep anything with personal data or final judgement fully human.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, list ten tasks from your own teaching or training week, and sort them into the three groups. Then explain one choice. It takes about fifteen minutes. In the next lesson, we look at prompting for teaching tasks. See you there.
```

## L02 Prompting for Teaching Tasks

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (672 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The prompt structure is general advice and the example is hypothetical.

```text
Ask an AI assistant for an activity about photosynthesis, and you will get something. It may be too long, too easy, or written for a different age group. The problem is usually not the AI. It is the missing information in the request.

In the last lesson, we sorted teaching tasks into what AI can draft and what stays human. Now we learn how to ask well. A prompt is the instruction you type into an AI assistant. The assistant only knows what you tell it. A short, general prompt gets a general answer.

A good teaching prompt has four parts. First, the learners: their age or role, their level, and the language they learn in. That could be fifteen-year-olds learning science in their second language, or new call-centre staff in their first week.

Second, the learning outcome: what learners should be able to do at the end, with an action verb like explain, compare or apply. Third, the time available, such as a forty-minute lesson or a ninety-minute workshop. And fourth, the format you want, for example a table, numbered steps, or three options to choose from.

After the first answer, you do not need to start again. Continue the conversation, and say exactly what to change. For example, make step two shorter, use examples from farming, or give the instructions at a simpler reading level.

Think of prompting like briefing a substitute teacher who arrives five minutes before your class. If you only say teach photosynthesis, they will do something general.

But if you tell them the year group, the mixed level, the forty minutes, the outcome, and that there is no projector, they can plan something that works for your room.

And one rule from the start. Describe your learners as a group, never as individuals. Do not include names, grades, health details or any other personal information in a prompt.

Let's see it in practice. Ngozi teaches science at a secondary school in Abuja, in Nigeria. She needs an activity on photosynthesis for tomorrow.

Her first prompt is one short line asking for an activity about photosynthesis. The answer suggests a two-hour experiment, with equipment she does not have, and instructions written for university students.

So she tries again with the four parts. Thirty students aged fourteen to fifteen, mixed ability, learning science in English as a second language. The outcome: they can explain in two or three sentences why a plant needs light, water and carbon dioxide. Forty minutes, no lab equipment. And a timed plan in a table, plus three check questions with answers.

The new answer is a forty-minute plan. A five-minute question about why plants on a dark windowsill grow poorly. A fifteen-minute pair activity sorting cards into inputs and outputs. Ten minutes for a diagram, and ten minutes for the check questions.

Ngozi still edits it. She replaces one example with a local crop her students know, and she checks the science in the answer key against her textbook. Then she asks for the card instructions to be rewritten for students still building their English reading skills. Now the activity fits her class.

A common mistake is to accept the first answer, or to give up and decide AI is not useful for your subject. The first answer is only a starting point. And never add detail by pasting a class list or grades.

Let's recap. First, a strong teaching prompt includes the learners, the learning outcome, the time available and the format you want. Second, improve results by continuing the conversation with specific changes. Third, describe learners as a group, never by name, and always check facts and fit before you use the output.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, write a one-line prompt and then a four-part prompt for an activity you will teach soon. Test both in Claude or ChatGPT, improve the result once, and compare. It takes about twenty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we look at privacy, integrity and ethics. See you there.
```

## L03 Privacy, Integrity and Ethics

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (694 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** [VERIFY] Scenes 8, 13 and 15 say that detection tools can give false results and should never be the only evidence of misconduct. Check this against current sources before recording; the wording is kept cautious on purpose.

```text
You have sixty reports to mark. Pasting them into a chatbot for grades would save hours. But first, ask two questions. Whose information is in those reports? And where does it go when you paste it?

Last time, we learned to write better prompts. Today, we look at the responsibility side of AI in teaching: learner privacy, academic integrity, and fairness.

Start with privacy. When you type or paste something into an AI tool, it leaves your device and is processed by the company that runs the tool. Depending on the tool and your settings, it may be stored, or used to improve the product.

So the basic rule is simple. Never paste learners' names, grades, health information, contact details, photos or other personal data into a public AI tool. That includes essays and reports that contain names or personal stories.

If you want AI help with learner work, remove everything that could identify a person, or use only your own notes. And if your institution provides an approved AI tool with extra data protection, use it and follow its rules.

Rules on learner data, and on whether minors may use AI tools at all, differ between countries, regions and institutions. AI companies also set their own minimum ages and terms. So before you ask learners to use any AI tool directly, check your institution's policy, your local rules, and the tool's current terms.

Here is a picture to remember. A public AI tool is like a busy shared office photocopier. It is useful, but you would not leave confidential learner files on it. You remove the private pages first, or you use a secure machine.

Next, academic integrity. Learners can use AI to produce work that is not their own, and some institutions use detection tools to find this. Be careful. Detection tools can give false results, including marking a learner's own writing as AI-generated. So a detector score should never be the only evidence of misconduct.

Task design often helps more than detection. Tasks are harder to hand to AI when they connect to something that happened in your class or workplace, ask for drafts or steps, include a short conversation about the work, or ask for reflection on a real experience.

Finally, fairness. Learners do not all have the same devices, internet or paid tools. If a task needs AI, make sure everyone can do it in class, or with a free option.

Let's meet Ali. He lectures in business studies at a university in Lahore, in Pakistan, and he wants help with feedback on forty-five short case-study answers.

His first idea is to paste all forty-five answers, with names and ID numbers, into a free chatbot. He changes his plan. He reads the answers himself and writes short notes, such as good use of the case, but no clear recommendation. Then he asks the AI to turn his notes into two polite, specific feedback sentences each.

Later, a detector report says one essay is ninety percent AI. Ali does not treat this as proof. He asks the learner to explain how they wrote it and to show their notes. The learner explains the argument clearly and shows early drafts, so Ali closes the matter.

A common mistake is to think that removing the name is enough. An ID number, a rare medical condition, a small class or a personal story can still point to one person. Remove all of it.

Let's recap. First, never paste learners' personal data into a public AI tool. Remove identifying details, or use your own notes. Second, detection tools can give false results, so never use them as the only evidence. Task design and conversation work better. Third, rules differ by country and institution, so check local rules, your policy and the tool's terms first.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will read five short scenarios, like a teacher pasting student essays into a chatbot. Decide if each is acceptable, acceptable with changes, or not acceptable, and give a reason. It takes about twenty minutes. In the next lesson, we write a fair classroom AI policy. See you there.
```

## L04 Writing a Fair Classroom AI Policy

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.1 minutes (700 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
One learner uses AI to check their grammar. Another uses it to write the whole essay. A third does not use it at all, because they are not sure it is allowed. If your learners cannot tell which is acceptable, your policy is not clear enough.

In the last lesson, we looked at privacy and integrity. Now we turn that into rules learners can follow. A classroom AI policy tells learners when and how they may use AI in your course. Without one, learners guess, and different learners guess differently. That is unfair to everyone.

A simple structure is the traffic-light model. For each type of task, you choose one of three colours. Red means AI is not allowed, because the task checks what the learner can do alone, like an in-class test.

Amber means AI is allowed with disclosure. Learners may use it for certain steps, such as brainstorming or checking grammar, and they add a short note on what they asked and what they changed. Green means AI is allowed freely, for example to generate practice questions before a test.

A good policy is clear. Each rule names a task type, a colour and a short reason, in plain language, with examples of allowed and not allowed use.

A good policy is also fair. It never requires paid tools, so every learner can complete green and amber tasks with a free tool or class time. Everyone knows the rules before the task begins. It explains disclosure, so honest learners are not punished for telling the truth. And it does not rely only on detection tools, which can give false results.

And a good policy fits your institution. Your school, university or company may already have rules on AI, and local rules may apply too. Your course policy adds detail within those rules, and never contradicts them.

Why traffic lights? Drivers do not read a long legal document at every crossing. Red, amber and green give a quick, shared signal. But the lights only work if they mean the same thing every time.

Let's see an example. Aiko Tanaka teaches academic English to first-year students at a university in Osaka, in Japan. She drafts a policy for her writing course.

The timed in-class essay is red, because it checks the student's own writing. The take-home essay is amber. AI may suggest ideas and correct grammar, with a note on how it was used. Vocabulary practice is green. And the reflection journal is red, because it must describe the student's own learning.

Then she pastes her draft, with no student information, into an AI assistant, and asks it to find rules that are unclear, or unfair to students with limited access to technology. It gives four suggestions.

Define what counts as AI. Does a spelling checker count? She accepts, and adds a short definition. Say what the disclosure note must include. She accepts, and adds a two-line template. Offer a free option or class time for green tasks. She accepts, and adds computer-room time.

But the AI also suggests making the take-home essay red to prevent misuse. She rejects this. She wants students to learn responsible use, and she already includes a short conversation about each essay. Her final policy is still one page, and every decision is her own.

A common mistake is one rule for the whole course, like AI is not allowed. It does not fit different tasks. Another is to accept every AI suggestion. You make the final judgement.

Let's recap. First, a traffic-light policy gives each task type a colour, red, amber or green, with a short reason. Second, a fair policy does not require paid tools, explains disclosure clearly, and does not rely only on detection tools. Third, your policy must fit your institution's rules, and you decide which AI suggestions to accept.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, draft a one-page traffic-light policy for a course you teach. Then ask Claude or ChatGPT to find unclear or unfair points, with no learner information, and decide which suggestions to accept. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, we start planning lessons with clear outcomes. See you there.
```
