## L09 Building Quizzes with AI and Google Forms

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M3_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (684 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
AI can write twenty quiz questions in less than a minute. But how many actually test what you taught? Some will be too easy, some will have two correct answers, and some will test something you never taught. Today, you find and fix them.

Welcome to week three, where your capstone unit comes together. A quiz is only useful if each question measures the learning outcome. So when you ask AI for questions, ask for a mix of three levels.

Recall means remembering a fact or term, like what does depreciation mean. Understanding means explaining an idea in new words. And application means using the idea in a new situation. For example, a company buys a van for twenty thousand, and uses the straight-line method over five years. What is the yearly depreciation? Also ask for an answer key and a short explanation for each question.

Then analyse every question with three checks. Does it test the outcome? If the outcome is to calculate, recall questions alone are not enough. Is there exactly one correct answer? AI often writes options that are partly true. And are the wrong options believable? Good wrong options come from real learner mistakes.

Also look for clues. The correct answer is often the longest option, or the only one that repeats words from the question. Remove these clues.

A quiz question is like a measuring tool. A kitchen scale is excellent, but it cannot measure temperature. A question can be well written and still measure the wrong thing, like reading speed, or a trick in the wording.

Google Forms is a free tool that can turn a form into a self-marking quiz, with an answer key, points and feedback. One reminder: collect only the learner data you need, and check your institution's rules before you collect names or email addresses.

Let's build one. Chen Wei teaches introductory accounting at a college in Kuala Lumpur, in Malaysia. His outcome is that students can calculate straight-line depreciation. He asks the AI for ten questions at three levels, with answer keys and explanations.

His review finds three weak items. One has two correct options, so he fixes it. One asks for the year a method was invented, which he never taught, so he replaces it with an application question. And one has the correct answer as the only long option, so he rewrites the options to similar lengths.

Now he opens Google Forms and starts a blank form called Depreciation Check. In the settings, he turns on the option to make it a quiz, and chooses when learners see their marks and feedback.

He adds his first question as multiple choice, and pastes in the question and four options. Then he opens the answer key, selects the correct option, and sets the points.

He adds feedback for correct and incorrect answers, using the AI's explanation after checking it himself. He repeats this for all ten questions, and turns on shuffle option order, so learners cannot copy the order.

Finally, he uses preview to take the quiz himself, and checks that the marking works. Then he shares the link with his students.

A common mistake is to copy AI questions straight into the form because they look correct. Many AI quizzes are mostly recall, even when the outcome is to calculate. Count your levels. And check the wrong options too, not only the right one.

Let's recap. First, ask AI for recall, understanding and application questions, with answer keys and explanations. Second, analyse every item. Does it test the outcome, does it have exactly one correct answer, and are the wrong options believable without clues? Third, Google Forms can mark the quiz for you. Check the live settings, and collect only the learner data you need.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone unit. Generate ten quiz questions for your unit with AI, fix or remove any weak items, and build the final quiz in Google Forms. It takes about forty minutes. In the next lesson, we look at rubrics and feedback with AI. See you there.
```

## L10 Rubrics and Feedback with AI

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M3_L10_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (684 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The lesson gives general assessment guidance with a hypothetical example.

```text
Two educators mark the same piece of work. One gives it seventy-two. The other gives it fifty-eight. Neither is careless. They simply had different ideas of what good meant. A clear rubric makes those ideas visible, before the work begins.

Last time, we built a quiz. Now we assess bigger tasks. An analytic rubric is a table that breaks a task into criteria and describes each level of quality. Each row is one criterion. Each column is one level, for example excellent, good, developing, and not yet.

A good rubric has three features. First, criteria linked to outcomes. Each one checks something learners were taught and the outcome requires. Second, clear levels. Explains three key features, with a customer benefit for each, is much clearer than very good explanation. And third, language learners understand before they start.

AI is useful for the first draft. Give it the task, the outcomes, the number of levels and the learners, and ask for a table. Then test the rubric. Mark one sample piece of work with it. If you hesitate between two levels, revise the wording.

Use a sample you wrote yourself, or one the AI created. Never paste real learner work with names or personal details into a public AI tool.

AI can also help with feedback. Read the work yourself and write short notes, such as clear opening, feature two missing a benefit, spoke too fast. Then ask the AI to turn the notes into clear, kind, specific comments linked to the rubric. AI does not know the learner, the context, or the effort behind the work. So the final judgement and the grade always stay with you.

A rubric is like a shared map for a journey. The educator and the learner both see where they are going, what the stages are, and how far they have travelled. Without the map, the learner guesses the destination, and the educator judges the journey differently each time.

Let's go to a workplace. Kateryna is a sales trainer at a software company in Kraków, in Poland. At the end of her course, new staff give a five-minute product demonstration.

She asks the AI for a four-level rubric, with criteria linked to her three outcomes. Explain key features, link each feature to a customer need, and answer a customer question. The draft has five criteria. She removes creative slide design, because it is not one of her outcomes, and not every trainee has design skills.

Then she asks the AI for a sample demonstration script with some typical weaknesses, and she marks it. Two problems appear. Under answering questions, the levels say good answer and very good answer, and she cannot decide between them.

So she rewrites them. Answers accurately and checks the customer is satisfied, and answers accurately but does not check. Under timing, the rubric does not say what happens if the demonstration is too short, so she adds that.

For feedback, she writes her own notes after each real demonstration, and asks the AI to turn them into two strengths and one next step. She removes any sentence that does not match what she saw, and she decides every grade herself.

A common mistake is to use an AI rubric without testing it. Another is to paste learner work into a chatbot and ask for a grade. That risks privacy, and hands your judgement to a tool.

Let's recap. First, a good rubric has criteria linked to outcomes, clear levels you can observe, and language learners understand. Second, test every AI-drafted rubric by marking a sample, and revise any criterion that was hard to apply. Third, AI can turn your own notes into feedback, but never paste learner work with personal data, and the final grade always stays with you.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step two of your capstone. Draft a rubric for your unit's main assessment task with AI, test it on one sample you created, and revise any unclear criterion. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the next lesson, we start the capstone: planning your unit. See you there.
```

## L11 Capstone: Planning Your Unit

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M3_L11_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (681 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The lesson uses general planning methods and a hypothetical example.

```text
A single good lesson is useful. But a unit where every lesson builds on the last, and the assessment checks exactly what was taught, is much more powerful. Today, you bring together everything from this course, and plan your own unit.

You have already built a quiz and a rubric. Now you plan the unit around them. A unit of study is a short sequence of lessons, usually three to five, that leads learners to a set of connected outcomes. You plan it with backward design, just like a single lesson, but at a larger scale.

There are four parts. First, two to four observable unit outcomes. Second, the assessment, which is your quiz and your main task with its rubric. Third, a lesson sequence, showing which outcome each lesson teaches. And fourth, the key activities and materials for each lesson, including your differentiated and accessible materials.

Then run an alignment check, so every outcome is taught and assessed. A simple alignment table has one row per outcome, and columns for taught in and assessed in.

AI is good at this check. Paste your plan, with no learner data, and ask which outcomes are not taught or not assessed, and which activities do not support any outcome. Then you decide what to change.

Planning a unit is like training for a five-kilometre run. You set the goal first. Each week builds part of it, and the final run shows whether it worked. A week of only arm exercises may be good training, but it is not aligned with the goal.

Now, the capstone brief. Build a complete AI-assisted unit, with lesson plans, differentiated materials, a quiz, a rubric and a unit AI policy, reviewed for accuracy, bias and accessibility. The rubric scores planning and alignment, materials, quiz and rubric quality, your review and policy, and the complete pack with your AI-use note.

Use the unit-plan template below this video. It has space for your learners as a group, with no names, your outcomes, the assessment, the lesson sequence, the alignment table, your AI policy, and your AI-use note.

Let's see a unit plan. Samuel teaches mathematics at a secondary school in Kisumu, in Kenya. His unit is on percentages for fourteen-year-olds, with four lessons of forty minutes.

He has three unit outcomes. Convert between fractions, decimals and percentages. Calculate a percentage of an amount. And solve percentage problems about prices and discounts.

He drafts the lesson sequence with AI. It proposes five lessons, including one on compound interest. Samuel removes it, because it is outside his syllabus for this age group, and he merges two short lessons to fit four.

Then he asks for an alignment check. The AI points out that outcome three is taught in lesson four, but his quiz has only one question on it. So he adds two application questions about market prices. He also notices himself that lesson two has no check for understanding, so he adds a three-question exit ticket.

Finally, he writes full plans for lessons one and four in the timed-table format. He adapts timings for his large class, and replaces the AI's shopping examples with invented prices from a local market.

A common mistake is to plan the lessons first, and add the assessment at the end. Fill in the alignment table before you write full lesson plans. And do not let AI decide the scope of your unit.

Let's recap. First, a unit plan starts with two to four outcomes, then the assessment, then a lesson sequence with activities, materials and checks. Second, an alignment table shows where each outcome is taught and assessed. AI can check it, but you decide the changes. Third, use the template, and adapt AI drafts to your syllabus, learners and context.

Now it is your turn. This is step three of your capstone. Write your unit plan with outcomes, a lesson sequence and full plans for at least two lessons, drafted with AI and edited by you. It takes about an hour. In the final lesson, we complete and review your unit. See you there.
```

## L12 Capstone: Completing and Reviewing Your Unit

- **Filename:** `ai-09-ai-for-educators-and-trainers_M3_L12_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (677 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** No facts to verify: content.md Review Flags say None. The tools are already flagged in L07 (Canva) and L09 (Google Forms).

```text
Your unit now has a plan, a quiz and a rubric. The last step is the one that makes it safe and fair to use with real learners. You put all the pieces together, and review them as a whole, with the eyes of a careful colleague.

In this final lesson, you complete your unit pack and review it. The pack has six parts. Your unit plan, with at least two full lesson plans. Differentiated materials in support, core and challenge versions. One accessible visual material. Your quiz. The rubric for your main task. And a unit AI policy, using the traffic-light model.

Then you run a final review in four areas. Accuracy: check every fact and answer key against a reliable source. Bias and representation: look across the whole pack. Does one group appear again and again in the same role?

Accessibility: clear fonts, contrast, alt text, simple layout, plain instructions, and suitable reading levels, including for second-language learners. And alignment: every outcome is taught, practised and assessed, and every activity supports an outcome. One more check. Make sure no learner names, grades or personal data appear anywhere in the pack.

Last, write a short AI-use note. It tells a reader where you used AI, what you checked, and what you changed. It is the same honesty you ask of learners in an amber task. A simple table works well.

The final review is like a pilot's checklist before take-off. The pilot knows how to fly, but the checklist catches the small thing that is easy to miss when you are busy. Your checklists do the same for facts, fairness and access.

Let's see a final review. Rosa teaches at a technical institute in Cusco, in Peru. Her unit, Responsible Tourism, has four lessons for adult students training to become tour guides. She collects all six parts in one shared folder, and then she reviews.

Accuracy first. One reading text, drafted with AI, gives a visitor number for a local site. She cannot confirm it in an official source, so she removes the number, and uses a general statement instead.

Then bias. Across the pack, every tourist in her examples is from Europe or North America, and every local person is a seller. So she changes several examples. A tourist from Brazil, a group from Japan, and a local guide who manages a business.

Accessibility. Her infographic uses green and red to show good and bad practice. She adds a tick and a cross, because colour alone does not work for every learner. She also adds alt text to two photos she had missed.

And alignment. Her outcome on handling a difficult visitor is taught in lesson three, but it is not in the rubric. So she adds a criterion. Her AI-use note then records each part, how AI helped, and what she changed. For example, AI drafted twelve quiz questions, and she removed three and rewrote four wrong options.

A common mistake is to review each part alone, never looking at the pack as a whole. Some problems appear only across the unit, such as one kind of person always in the same role, or a policy that marks a task red while a lesson plan tells learners to use AI for it. Read the whole pack as a learner would. And make your AI-use note specific.

Let's recap. First, the unit pack has six parts: the unit plan, differentiated materials, an accessible visual, the quiz, the rubric and a unit AI policy. Second, review the whole pack for accuracy, bias, accessibility and alignment, not only each part alone. Third, a short, specific AI-use note shows where AI helped, and what you checked and changed.

Congratulations. You have reached the end of AI for Educators and Trainers. Your last step is step four of the capstone. Complete your unit pack, review it with the checklists from lessons seven and eight, and write your AI-use note. Then submit your unit for assessment. Thank you for learning with us, and enjoy using your unit with real learners.
```
