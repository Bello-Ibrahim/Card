## L06 Cleaning Up Recordings in Audacity

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M2_L06_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (680 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Hina and her Lahore poetry podcast are fictional. Pronunciation: Hina = HEE-na; Lahore = la-HOR.

```text
You recorded a great interview. But when you play it back, you hear a humming fan, long gaps, and some words louder than others. Listeners notice this in seconds. The good news is, a free editor and a simple order of steps can fix most of it.

Welcome to week two. Audacity is a free, open-source audio editor. We use it for a clean-up chain. That is a fixed order of steps that you apply to every voice recording. The order matters, because each step prepares the audio for the next one.

Here is the chain. One, listen and look at the waveform, and find a quiet section. Two, remove background noise. Three, cut mistakes and long silences. Four, even out the levels. Five, reduce harsh sounds. And six, compare with the original and export.

Three tools do most of the work. Noise Reduction lowers background sound, but only with gentle settings, or the voice sounds thin and metallic. A compressor reduces the gap between loud and quiet words. And an equaliser, or EQ, can gently lower harsh high sounds, like strong s sounds.

Audacity can also use optional AI plugins, for example for noise suppression. But the basic chain in this lesson is all you need for this course.

Think of it like editing a photo. You fix the light and remove dust first, and only then add filters. If you add a filter to a dark, dusty photo, the problems become stronger. In audio, you remove noise and fix levels before anything creative.

Let's clean a real recording. Hina records a poetry podcast at home in Lahore, in Pakistan. Her one-minute test has a ceiling fan hum, two long pauses and some quiet lines.

She opens Audacity, drags in her recording, and saves a copy of the original first. Then she listens with headphones, and looks at the waveform. Before she starts speaking, there are two seconds of fan noise. That is her noise floor.

Noise Reduction works in two passes. She selects the two quiet seconds and lets the effect learn the noise profile. Then she selects the whole track, keeps gentle settings, and previews before she applies it. The fan hum becomes almost silent, and her voice still sounds natural.

Next, she deletes a false start, where she began a line and stopped. She uses Truncate Silence to shorten the two long pauses to under one second. But she keeps short, natural pauses, because speech with none sounds rushed.

To even out the levels, she applies Compressor with the default settings. This reduces the difference between loud and quiet words. Then she uses Normalize, so the loudest point stays just below the top. You will set the final loudness in lesson nine.

Her s sounds are sharp on headphones, so she uses Filter Curve EQ to lower the high frequencies slightly. Small changes only. Finally, she plays the original and the cleaned version one after the other, and exports a WAV file.

Hina writes one note for each step. Noise reduction removed the fan. Truncate Silence made the pace tighter. Compressor and Normalize made quiet lines easier to hear. And the EQ made the s sounds softer.

The most common mistake is too much noise reduction. Push the settings high, and the voice starts to sound robotic, thin or underwater. A little background noise is much less distracting than a damaged voice. So use gentle settings, preview, and compare.

Let's recap. First, use a fixed chain: listen, remove noise, cut mistakes and silences, even out levels, reduce harsh sounds, then compare and export. Second, noise reduction learns the noise from a quiet section, then removes it from the whole file. Third, always keep the original, and compare before and after.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will clean up a noisy one-minute voice recording in Audacity. Use your own, or the practice file on the course page. Then describe what each step changed. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, Generating Music Beds with AI, we add music.
```

## L07 Generating Music Beds with AI

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M2_L07_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (687 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Valentina and her Medellín podcast are fictional. Pronunciation: Medellín = meh-deh-YEEN.

```text
The first ten seconds of a podcast set the mood. A calm piano says relax and learn. A fast drum beat says something exciting is coming. Finding the right music used to take hours. Now you can describe it and hear several options in minutes.

In the last lesson, you cleaned up a voice. Now, let's give it music. An AI music tool creates new music from a text description, called a prompt. We show Suno on its free plan. Free plans usually limit how many tracks you make and how you may use them.

For podcasts and narrated videos, we need a music bed. That is music that sits quietly under a voice. A good music bed is simple and steady, and does not compete with speech. It usually has no singing, because lyrics fight with the spoken words.

Describe the music with five parts. The mood, like calm or hopeful. The genre, like acoustic folk or ambient. The tempo, slow, medium or fast, or a number of beats per minute. The instruments, plus the words instrumental, no vocals. And the length and shape, like thirty seconds with a gentle ending.

And one firm rule. Never name a living artist or a specific song in your prompt. Asking a tool to copy a real musician can raise legal and ethical problems. Describe the sound instead.

AI music varies each time, so always generate several options. Then compare them for four things. Mood fit, does it match the message? Tempo fit, does it match the pace of the voice? Busy parts, like fast melodies or sudden drums under speech. And the ending. Does it finish cleanly, or stop suddenly?

Choosing a music bed is like choosing a tablecloth for a special meal. It should support the food, not distract from it. A bright pattern may look beautiful alone, but on the table it makes the dishes hard to see.

Let's try it. Valentina produces an education podcast in Medellín, in Colombia, about science for teenagers. She needs a thirty-second intro bed that feels calm but curious. Her spoken intro is warm, at a medium pace.

She opens Suno and checks the free plan limits and the licence page first. Then she types a prompt with all five parts. Calm and curious, light Latin folk, about ninety beats per minute, nylon-string guitar and soft marimba, a light shaker, instrumental, no vocals, thirty seconds, gentle ending.

She generates three options and downloads each one. Each option sounds a little different, even from the same prompt. Then she imports her voiceover and each option into Audacity, on separate tracks. She lowers the music track, so she can hear her voice clearly.

Now she plays each option under her voice. Option A has a good mood, but a loud marimba melody starts exactly when she says the podcast name. Option C is too slow, and a little sad for a curious science show.

Option B has steady, warm guitar. Its tempo matches her speaking pace, and it has a clean ending. So she chooses option B, and writes the prompt, the tool, the plan and the date in her project notes.

A common mistake is to choose the track you like best when you hear it alone. Under the voice, the melody fights with the words. So always judge a music bed with your voiceover playing. And avoid vague prompts like nice music, which give random results.

Let's recap. First, describe music by mood, genre, tempo, instruments and length, and never by the name of an artist or a song. Second, a good music bed is simple, steady and usually instrumental. Third, generate several options, and judge each one under your voiceover for mood, tempo, busy parts and the ending.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will generate three short music beds for the same intro and choose one. Then explain how its mood and tempo fit your voiceover. It takes about thirty minutes. Save your prompt and notes, because in the next lesson, Licences for AI Voices and Music, we start your rights log.
```

## L08 Licences for AI Voices and Music

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M2_L08_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (689 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Aroha's example terms are hypothetical. Do not show any real tool's logo, name or terms page on the Aroha slides. Aroha and the Wellington tourism client are fictional. Pronunciation: Aroha = ah-ROH-ha.

```text
You made a voiceover and a music bed with free tools. Can you use them in a video that earns money? Can a client use them in an advert? The answers are not in the audio file. They are in the licence.

In the last lesson, you made a music bed. Today, we check what you are allowed to do with it. A licence is the set of permissions that tells you what you may do with something you did not fully create or own. For AI voices and music, it usually comes from the tool's terms of service.

A licence answers four main questions. The first is personal or commercial use. Personal use means projects that do not earn money, like a class assignment. Commercial use means anything connected to earning money, like a sponsored podcast, an advert or client work.

Free and paid plans can have different terms. So do not assume. Read the terms for the plan you actually used.

The second question is attribution. Some licences require you to credit the tool, for example in your show notes. If a credit is required, it is part of the licence, not a polite extra. The third question is platform rules. Video and podcast platforms have their own rules, and your content must follow them too.

The fourth question is who owns the output. Some tools say you own what you create. Others keep some rights, or give you a licence to use it. There is also a wider legal question about whether music made mostly by AI can be protected at all. This course gives no legal advice, so check current official guidance in your country.

Here is a way to picture it. A licence is like the rules for a rented flat. The contract says whether you can run a business from it, or rent it to someone else. Keeping a signed copy means you can prove what was agreed.

That signed copy is your rights log. It is a simple table with one row for each asset. It records the tool and plan, a short summary of the licence, whether you need a credit, any consent for a real person's voice, the date you checked, and a link or screenshot.

Let's see it in use. Aroha is a video creator in Wellington, in New Zealand. She has just signed her first paid project, for a local tourism business. Her video uses a text-to-speech voiceover and an AI music bed, both made on free plans. So she checks each asset.

In her example, the voice tool's free plan does not allow commercial use, so she records her own voice instead. The music tool's free plan allows online videos, but requires a credit, so she adds a credit line. Then she checks the platform's current rules on AI labels, and adds the label that applies.

She adds a row for each asset to her rights log, with the date and a screenshot of the terms. She also tells the client that the music is AI-generated. Remember, these terms are only examples. Real tools have different terms, so always read your own.

A common mistake is to think that free to use means free for any use. It usually means free to create. What you may do with the result is in the licence. And check again before each new commercial project, because terms change.

Let's recap. First, a licence tells you if you can use an asset commercially, if you must credit the tool, and what the tool says about ownership. Second, plans can have different terms, and platforms have their own rules. Third, keep a rights log for every voice and music asset, with the tool, plan, terms, consent and date.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will start a rights log for your project, with each voice and music asset, the tool, the plan, the licence terms and the date you checked. It takes about twenty-five minutes. You will submit this log with your capstone. In the next lesson, Mixing Voice and Music, we put it all together.
```

## L09 Mixing Voice and Music

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M2_L09_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (687 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Marisol and her Cebu small-business podcast are fictional. Stock footage must not show a real podcast name or logo. Pronunciation: Marisol = mah-ree-SOL; Cebu = seh-BOO.

```text
You have a clean voice and a music bed you like. Put them together without care, and the music may cover your words, or suddenly stop. Put them together well, and the episode sounds finished and professional. The difference is a few simple steps.

In the last lesson, you started your rights log. Now, we mix. Mixing means combining separate audio tracks into one balanced file. For a podcast or narrated video, the main rule is simple. The voice is on top. Every other sound supports it.

A basic mix in Audacity has five steps. First, arrange the tracks. Put the voice on one track and the music on another, and start the music a few seconds before the first word. That gives the listener time to settle.

Second, lower the music under speech. You can do this by hand, or with the Envelope tool, which lets you draw volume changes over time. Audacity also has an effect called Auto Duck, which lowers the music whenever the voice is playing. The music can be louder in the intro and outro.

Third, fade in and fade out. A fade is a smooth change in volume. Use a short fade-in and a longer fade-out. Fourth, set consistent loudness. Follow the platform's current guidance, and apply Loudness Normalization to the final mix, not to each track. Fifth, check on two systems, then export.

Never let music stop suddenly, unless the track has a clean ending of its own. And remember, listeners should not need to change the volume between your episode and the next one.

Why two systems? Headphones show small details, like clicks and noise. A phone speaker shows whether the voice is still clear when the bass disappears and the room is noisy.

Think of a dinner party with soft music in the room. The music helps the mood. But when someone speaks, everyone can hear them. If the music is too loud, guests lean in and stop listening.

Let's see a full mix. Marisol hosts a small-business podcast in Cebu, in the Philippines. Her three-minute episode has a cleaned recording of her voice, an AI music bed for the intro and outro, and a short text-to-speech announcement at the end.

She imports the voice, the music and the announcement onto three tracks, and moves the voice so it starts four seconds after the music. She applies Auto Duck to the music, controlled by the voice track. But the music is still too strong, so she lowers it a little more by hand.

With the Envelope tool, she brings the music up again for five seconds after her last sentence, then adds a three-second fade-out. The announcement is louder than her voice, so she lowers that track until they sound equal.

She exports a mixed file, applies Loudness Normalization using her podcast host's current guidance, and exports the final version. Then she listens on headphones, and on her phone. On the phone, the music hides one quiet word at one minute twelve. So she lowers the music there, and exports again.

Finally, she adds the final file name and the date to her rights log. The draft is ready for review.

A common mistake is to mix only on good headphones, or only on laptop speakers. The mix then fails somewhere else. Another is making the music too loud because you like it. If a listener has to work to hear the words, the music is too loud.

Let's recap. First, the voice is always on top, so lower the music under speech by hand, with the Envelope tool or with Auto Duck. Second, use fades at the start and end, and set the final loudness by following the platform's current guidance. Third, check the mix on headphones and on a phone speaker before you export.

Now it is your turn. This exercise is step one of your capstone. You will mix your voiceover or recording with a music bed in Audacity, and export a first draft. It takes about thirty-five minutes. In the final lesson, Capstone: Producing and Reviewing Your Episode, you will finish and submit it.
```

## L10 Capstone: Producing and Reviewing Your Episode

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M2_L10_presenter.mp4`

```text
You now have every part. A script for the ear, a voice, clean audio, a music bed, captions and a rights log. The last step is to bring them together, review them with fresh ears, and publish something you can be proud of.

In the last lesson, you mixed your first draft. Now, the capstone. You produce a two to four minute podcast episode or narrated video, with AI-assisted voice, clean-up and music. You submit it with captions, a completed rights log and a disclosure line. You choose the topic.

AI-assisted means at least two of these: a text-to-speech voiceover, Whisper captions, AI or plugin clean-up, and an AI music bed. You may use your own recorded voice for the narration. But you must not use a cloned voice of anyone else. If you use a clone of your own voice, record it in your rights log.

Before you export, review five areas in one full listen, without stopping to fix things. Clarity, can you understand every word on a phone? Pacing, is there time to breathe? Captions, do they match the final audio? Consent, does every real voice have written permission? And licences, does every row in your log allow this use?

Take notes as you listen. Then fix what you found, and listen to the whole piece again from the start.

Then write a disclosure line. In one or two plain sentences, tell your audience where AI was used. For example, the narration uses a text-to-speech voice, music was made with an AI music tool, and captions were created with Whisper and checked by hand. Also check whether your platform asks for its own label.

Think of a pilot's checklist before take-off. The pilot knows the plane well, but still checks every item. The list catches the small problems that experience can miss when you are busy. The final review works the same way.

Let's watch a final review. Dilnoza is a geography teacher in Tashkent, in Uzbekistan. She makes a three-minute narrated video about the Aral Sea region for her students, using her own photos. Every part of the capstone is in it, so her review is a good model for yours.

She works through a production checklist. The script is written for the ear. The voice is her own, cleaned in Audacity. The music bed is instrumental, with no artist names in the prompt. The mix has the voice on top. The captions are checked by hand, and every asset has a row in her rights log.

Her review still finds two problems. First, she cut one sentence from the final mix, but it is still in the caption file. So after one minute forty, the captions drift out of time. She makes the captions again from the final audio, and checks them.

Second, her rights log shows that the music tool requires a credit, which she had not added. She adds it to the video description, next to her disclosure line. Without the log, she might have missed it.

Two small problems, both caught before publishing. That is exactly what the checklist is for.

A common mistake is to make captions early, and then keep editing the audio. Always make captions from the final audio. Another is a vague disclosure line, like AI may have been used. Be specific about what AI did, so your audience can trust you.

Let's recap. First, the capstone is a two to four minute AI-assisted episode or narrated video, with captions, a rights log and a disclosure line. Second, review clarity, pacing, captions, consent and licences in one full listen, then fix and listen again. Third, make captions from the final audio, and say specifically where AI was used.

Congratulations. You have completed AI Voice, Audio and Music. Now it is time for step two of your capstone. Export your final episode or video with captions. Then submit it with your rights log, your disclosure line and your checklist. Allow about an hour, and use the rubric to check your work first. I cannot wait to hear what you make. Well done.
```
