## L01 How AI Hears and Speaks

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (722 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Close your eyes and listen to a short advert on the radio. Was the voice a real person in a studio, or a computer? Was the music played by a band, or made by software in seconds? Today, it can be hard to tell.

Hi, and welcome to AI Voice, Audio and Music. In this first lesson, we look inside the tools that make these sounds. In this course, we use three families of audio AI, and each one does a different job.

The first family is text-to-speech. It turns written text into spoken audio. You type a sentence, choose a voice, and the tool reads it aloud. Each voice comes from a voice model. That is a system that has learned how one voice, or one style of voice, sounds.

The second family is speech-to-text, also called speech recognition. It does the opposite. It listens to spoken audio and writes down the words. The result is a transcript. When each line of the transcript has timings, so it appears on screen at the right moment, it becomes captions.

The third family is audio generation. It creates new sound from a description. The type we use most is AI music. You describe the mood, genre, tempo and instruments, and the tool makes a short piece. A quiet piece of music that plays under a voice is called a music bed.

All three families work in a similar way. They are trained on many hours of recorded sound. But the system does not store the recordings like a library. It learns patterns. For example, how a voice rises at the end of a question, or how a calm piano piece usually moves.

Think of a language learner who has listened to thousands of speakers on the radio for years. Now they can understand almost anyone. And they can also imitate natural speech, with the right rhythm and tone. They did not memorise every programme. They learned the patterns.

Speech-to-text is the understanding side of this learner. Text-to-speech is the imitating side.

One more term will help you later. The noise floor is the level of quiet background sound in a recording when nobody is speaking. That could be a fan, traffic or an electrical hum. A low noise floor means a clean recording. AI clean-up tools try to lower it without damaging the voice.

Let's see how this helps in real work. Tomás produces a weekly community radio programme in Valparaíso, in Chile. He wants AI to save him time. But first, he wants to know which tool does which job. So he writes down his three tasks.

First, he writes a weekly weather and events update, but has no time to record it. Text goes in, and a voice comes out. That is text-to-speech. Second, he interviews local fishermen and wants a written version for the station's website. Audio goes in, and a transcript comes out. That is speech-to-text.

Third, he needs a gentle twenty-second music bed with acoustic guitar under his introduction. A description goes in, and new music comes out. That is audio generation.

Then Tomás lists one thing to check for each task. For the update, he will listen for place names the voice says wrongly. For the transcript, he will check names and numbers by hand. And for the music, he will check that it is calm enough to sit under speech.

Now, a common mistake. Many beginners think an AI voice understands the text. It does not. It predicts how the words probably sound. So it may read S T dot as street when you meant saint. The fix is to write clearly for the ear, and always listen to the full result.

Let's recap. First, text-to-speech turns text into voice, speech-to-text turns voice into a transcript, and audio generation creates new sound from a description. Second, these tools learn patterns from many hours of sound, but they do not understand meaning. Third, your key terms are voice model, transcript, captions, noise floor and music bed.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will listen to four short clips and decide if each one is a human recording, AI speech or AI music. It takes about fifteen minutes. In the next lesson, we learn about writing for the ear. See you there.
```

## L02 Writing for the Ear

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (691 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Pronunciation guide for the presenter voice: Aten = AH-ten; Akhenaten = ak-en-AH-ten; Amarna = a-MAR-na. If HeyGen stresses these wrongly, use phonetic spellings in the HeyGen text only.

```text
Try to read this aloud. The collection, which was assembled between nineteen twenty-two and nineteen thirty-one by a team of fourteen specialists, comprises two thousand four hundred and fifty objects. Did you run out of breath? A listener cannot go back and read it again.

In the last lesson, you met the three families of audio AI. Today, we focus on the words you give a voice. A reader controls the speed. They can look back, read a long sentence twice, and see brackets and headings.

A listener cannot do any of this. They hear each word once, in order, at the speed of the voice. This is true for a human voice, and even more true for a text-to-speech voice, which reads exactly what you write. So here are five habits that help.

One, short sentences. Aim for about ten to twenty words. If a sentence has which, although or brackets in the middle, split it. Two, one idea at a time. Give each sentence one main point, and put the most important words at the end, where the voice naturally lands.

Three, spoken numbers. Round them when you can, and write them the way you want them said. About two and a half thousand objects is easier to hear than the exact figure. Four, clear signposts. First. Next. Now look to your left. Signposts replace the headings a reader would see.

Five, help with pauses and pronunciation. Use full stops and paragraph breaks where you want a pause. For a difficult name, add a phonetic spelling. That is a simple respelling that shows how to say the word. And remove what the ear cannot use, like links, abbreviations and symbols.

Here is a way to picture it. Written text is like a map. The reader sees the whole route at once. A spoken script is like a guide walking beside you. The guide must say each turn at the right moment, one step at a time, because you cannot see the whole route.

Let's try it. Nour works for a museum in Cairo, in Egypt. She is preparing an audio guide for one display case. The original label is thirty-five words in one sentence, with a bracket, an abbreviation, a date and two difficult names.

You can see it on screen now. It works on a printed label. But try to hear it, and the listener is lost before the end. So Nour rewrites it for the ear.

Look at the flat stone in front of you. It is called a stela. It is more than three thousand years old. It shows a royal family. Above them, you can see long lines coming down from a sun. That sun is the Aten. King Akhenaten made the Aten the centre of religious life in Egypt.

Notice what changed. Each sentence has one idea. It starts with a signpost, look at. And the old date became more than three thousand years old, which a listener understands at once. The discovery date moved to the printed label.

Nour kept the phonetic spellings as notes and left them out of the text she typed into the text-to-speech tool. She only added them when the tool said a name wrongly.

A common mistake is to paste written text straight into a voice tool. The voice may be good, but the script is not. Rewrite first, then generate. And read it aloud yourself. If you stumble, the listener will stumble too.

Let's recap. First, listeners hear each word once, so use short sentences, one idea at a time and clear signposts. Second, write numbers, dates and symbols the way you want them said, and round numbers when you can. Third, use full stops for pauses, and phonetic spellings for difficult names.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will rewrite a written paragraph of about one hundred and fifty words as a spoken script. Then you read it aloud and mark where you need to breathe. It takes about twenty minutes. Keep your script. In the next lesson, Making a Voiceover with Free Text-to-Speech, you will give it a voice.
```

```text
You have a clear script. Now you want it spoken in a calm, natural voice, without a studio or a voice actor. A free text-to-speech tool can make a first version in minutes. The skill is not pressing generate. The skill is listening and fixing.

In the last lesson, you wrote a script for the ear. Today, you give it a voice. Most free text-to-speech tools follow the same workflow, even though their screens look different. We show ElevenLabs on its free plan, but these steps work in most tools.

There are six steps. Choose the language, the accent and the voice. Set the speed. Add pauses. Fix pronunciation. Listen for problems. And export. When you choose a voice, test three or four with your own script, not only the demo sentence.

When you listen, check for five things. Robotic rhythm, where every sentence has the same shape. Wrong stress on a word or syllable. Wrong pronunciation. Pace that is too fast, too slow or rushed at the end. And odd sounds, like clicks or a strange ending.

A few tips for the middle steps. For teaching content, a slightly slower speed is often clearer, so change it in small steps. Full stops and new paragraphs usually create pauses. And when a name is wrong, try a phonetic spelling first, then a hyphen or space to split the word, then the tool's pronunciation dictionary if it has one.

Here is a way to think about it. Working with a voice tool is like directing a talented actor who has never seen your script. The actor reads well, but does not know which word matters most, or how to say your company's name. So you give short, clear directions and try again.

Let's watch this in action. Agnieszka runs training at a logistics company in Poland. She needs a sixty-second English voiceover for a warehouse safety video. The script mentions a depot in Wrocław, and a rule to keep one and a half metres from moving forklifts.

She signs in and pastes her script into the text box. She has already rewritten it for the ear. She sets the language to English, and tests four voices on her first paragraph. She chooses a calm, mid-pitch voice with a neutral international accent.

Now she generates the full script and listens with headphones. She writes down three problems, with the time of each one. At eight seconds, Wrocław sounds like rock law. At twenty-one seconds, the safety distance is read too quickly. And at fifty seconds, the last three sentences feel rushed.

Fix one. In the tool only, she types a phonetic spelling of Wrocław, and generates that paragraph again. Fix two. She changes the number to the words one and a half metres, and adds a full stop after the rule, so the voice pauses.

Fix three. She lowers the speed a little, and adds a paragraph break before the final sentence. Then comes the most important step. She listens again from the very beginning, not only to the parts she changed. All three fixes work.

Finally, she exports the file as a WAV and writes the tool name, the plan and the date in her project notes. You will need these for your rights log later in the course.

One common mistake is to fix a problem and export without listening to the whole file again. A change in one place can create a new problem somewhere else. And never paste personal or confidential information into an online tool unless your organisation has approved it.

Let's recap. First, the workflow is choose the voice, set the speed, add pauses, fix pronunciation, listen and export. Second, listen for rhythm, stress, pronunciation, pace and odd sounds, and note the time of each problem. Third, free plans often limit characters, voices and commercial use, so check the plan and note the tool and date.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will make a sixty-second voiceover of your own script, then fix three problems in pace or pronunciation. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, Transcripts and Captions with Whisper, we turn speech back into text.
```

## L04 Transcripts and Captions with Whisper

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 4.9 minutes (679 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Pronunciation: Mateus = ma-TAY-oos; Recife = heh-SEE-fee; Manizales = man-ee-SAH-les.

```text
Many people watch videos with the sound off, and some cannot hear the audio at all. Captions help all of them. Typing captions by hand can take hours. With a speech-to-text model, you get a first draft in minutes, and spend your time checking it.

In the last lesson, you turned text into speech. Today, we go the other way. Whisper is a speech-to-text model. It listens to audio and produces a transcript. The easiest way to use it is a free app on your own computer, so your audio does not leave your device.

We use an app called Buzz. If you like the terminal, you can also run Whisper with one command, but that is optional. Whisper comes in several sizes. Smaller models are faster but make more mistakes. A small or medium model is a good start on a normal laptop.

Whisper makes predictable mistakes. It often gets names wrong. It mixes up numbers, like fifteen and fifty. And it writes common words in place of technical terms. Noise and people talking at the same time also cause trouble. So always check the transcript by hand.

Captions are a transcript split into short lines, each with a start and end time. The most common caption file is called SRT. It is plain text. Each caption has a number, then a timing line, then the words. Most video editors accept it.

Think of Whisper as a fast assistant who types everything they hear in a meeting. They are quick and usually right. But they do not know your guests' names or the special words of your field. So you read their notes and correct them before anyone else sees them.

Let's follow Mateus. He is a science teacher in Recife, in Brazil, and he records short videos in Portuguese for his students. He wants captions for a two-minute video about the water cycle. First, he exports the audio of his video as an MP3 file.

He opens Buzz on his computer and chooses the file. He sets the language to Portuguese and selects the medium model. Then he clicks transcribe. After a short wait, the transcript appears, with a timestamp on every line.

Now the real work. He plays the audio and reads along at the same time. He finds three errors. A long scientific word about water rising from plants and soil was split into two wrong words. The temperature thirty-two degrees was written in a strange way. And one sentence appears twice, although he said it once.

He corrects each error in the app's editor, and deletes the repeated sentence. Then he exports the result as an SRT file, imports it into his video editor, and watches the whole video once with captions on.

You can also open the SRT file in a plain text editor before you use it. Check the caption numbers, the timings and the length of each line. Short lines are much easier to read, especially on a small phone screen.

If you prefer the terminal, the same job takes one command, with the file name, the model, the language and SRT as the output. You can see it on screen. This is optional.

A common mistake is to trust the transcript because most of it is correct. But the errors are usually in the most important words. One wrong number can change the meaning. And never upload other people's recordings to an online service without their permission.

Let's recap. First, Whisper is a speech-to-text model you can use through a free app, or optionally with one command. Second, always check names, numbers, technical terms and repeated sentences by hand while you listen. Third, an SRT file holds numbered captions with start and end times, and most video editors accept it.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will transcribe a two-minute recording of your own voice, correct every error, and export an SRT caption file. It takes about thirty minutes. In the next lesson, Voice Cloning: Consent Comes First, we talk about people before tools.
```

## L05 Voice Cloning: Consent Comes First

- **Filename:** `ai-22-ai-voice-audio-and-music_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.0 minutes (694 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.
- **Pronunciation:** Pronunciation: Kwame = KWAH-meh; Efua = EH-foo-ah; Accra = a-KRAH.

```text
Your voice is part of who you are. Now imagine hearing your voice in an advert you never recorded, saying things you never said. Voice cloning makes this possible. That is why this lesson starts with people, not tools.

In the last lesson, you worked with transcripts. Today, we look at something more personal. Voice cloning means training or adapting a voice model so that it sounds like one specific real person. The model can then read any text in that person's voice.

Cloning has helpful uses. A podcaster who loses their voice through illness can still publish episodes. But the same technology can deceive. Think of fake phone calls, fake endorsements, or fake statements that damage someone's reputation.

The rules about voices and deepfakes are different from country to country. This course gives no legal advice. Instead, it teaches five principles that are good practice everywhere. Always check local rules and your tool's terms before a real project.

Principle one. Get clear, informed and recorded consent. The person agrees in writing, after you explain in plain language what will happen. Informed means they understand what a clone is, what it could say and who will hear it. Principle two. Limit the use to what was agreed, meaning a purpose, a time period and specific uses.

Principle three. Allow withdrawal. The person can change their mind, and the agreement says what happens to the voice model and files. Principle four. Disclose. Tell your audience when a voice is AI or cloned. A short line in the show notes, or at the start of an episode, is often enough.

Principle five. Never clone anyone without permission. That includes famous people, colleagues, family members and people who have died. In this course, we do not clone anyone's voice. You will write a consent form instead.

Here is a way to remember it. Consent for a voice clone is like lending someone your car. You agree who will drive it, where, for how long, and when they give it back. Yes, you can borrow it, does not mean you can keep it forever and lend it to strangers.

Let's see the principles in a real plan. Kwame and Efua host a weekly business podcast in Accra, in Ghana. Efua will travel for six weeks. She suggests that Kwame uses a clone of her voice to read the short introduction to each episode while she is away. Kwame starts with a one-page agreement, not a tool.

The purpose is to read the thirty-second introduction during Efua's travel. The duration is six weeks from a stated start date, and after that the voice model is deleted. The only allowed use is the introduction script, which Efua reads and approves each week. Adverts, social media clips, other podcasts and anything she has not approved are not allowed.

Each episode's notes say the introduction uses an AI version of Efua's voice, with her permission. And Efua can withdraw at any time by message. Kwame will stop at once and delete the model and samples within a stated number of days. Both sign and date the page, and Kwame checks the tool's terms too.

A common mistake is to think that a public voice is free to clone. It is not. Being able to hear a voice does not give you permission to copy it. Another mistake is to treat a quick verbal yes as consent. Without a written record, nobody can show what was agreed.

Let's recap. First, only clone a voice with clear, informed and recorded consent, and never clone anyone without permission. Second, consent has limits: a purpose, a duration, allowed uses and a way to withdraw. Third, disclose AI and cloned voices to your audience, and check local rules and tool terms, because they differ by country and change often.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will draft a one-page voice consent form for a podcast co-host. It states the purpose, the duration, the allowed uses and how to withdraw. It takes about twenty-five minutes. That completes week one. In the next lesson, Cleaning Up Recordings in Audacity, we start week two.
```
