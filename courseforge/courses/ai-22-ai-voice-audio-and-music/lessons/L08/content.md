# L08 Licences for AI Voices and Music

Course: AI-22 · Module: M2 · Objectives: O2, O6 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
You made a voiceover and a music bed with free tools. Can you put them in a video that earns money? Can a client use them in an advert? Can someone else copy your AI music? The answers are not in the audio file. They are in the licence, and they can change from one tool, plan and country to another.

## Explanation
A **licence** is the set of permissions that tells you what you may do with something you did not fully create or own. For AI voices and music, the licence usually comes from the tool's terms of service or a separate licence page. It answers four main questions.

**1. Personal or commercial use?** Personal use means non-commercial projects, such as a class assignment. Commercial use means anything connected to earning money: a monetised video, a sponsored podcast, an advert or client work. Free tiers often allow personal use only, or add conditions for commercial use, while paid plans may allow more [VERIFY] [VERSION]. Do not assume; read the terms for the plan you actually used.

**2. Attribution?** Some licences require you to credit the tool, for example "Music made with [tool name]" in your show notes or video description [VERSION]. If attribution is required, it is part of the licence, not a polite extra.

**3. Platform rules.** Video and podcast platforms have their own rules. Some ask you to label realistic AI-generated content, and some have systems that detect music they believe is copyrighted [VERIFY] [VERSION]. Your content must follow both the tool's licence and the platform's rules.

**4. Who owns the output?** Some tools say you own what you create; others keep some rights or give you a licence to use it [VERSION]. There is also a wider legal question: whether music or audio made mostly by AI can be protected by copyright at all. The answer is unsettled and differs by country [VERIFY] [REGION]. This matters if you want to stop others copying "your" AI track. This course gives no legal advice; check current official guidance in your country.

Licence terms change, so keep a **rights log**: a table that records, for each asset, the tool and plan, what the licence said and when you checked. It also records consent for any real person's voice (L05) and shows clients that you did your checks.

A simple rights log has these columns:

| Asset | Type | Tool and plan | Licence terms (short) | Attribution needed? | Consent needed/obtained? | Date checked | Link or screenshot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Analogy:** A licence is like the rules for a rented flat. You live there, but the contract says whether you can run a business from it, whether you can sublet it and whether you must put your name on the door. A rights log is like keeping a signed copy of the contract, so you can prove what was agreed if anyone asks.

## Worked Example
Aroha is a video creator in Wellington, New Zealand. She has just signed her first paid project for a local tourism business. Her video uses a TTS voiceover and an AI music bed, both made on free plans.

She checks each asset:

- **Voiceover:** The TTS tool's free plan does not allow commercial use, so she cannot use this voiceover for a client. She records her own voice instead.
- **Music bed:** The music tool's free plan allows use in online videos but requires a credit in the description. She adds the credit line.
- **Platform:** She checks the video platform's current rules on AI content labels and adds the label that applies.

She adds a row for each asset to her rights log, with the date and a screenshot of the terms. She also tells the client that the music is AI-generated.

These terms are examples only. Real tools have different terms, so always read the ones for your tool and plan.

## Common Mistake
Many people think "free to use" means "free for any use". It usually means free to create; what you may do with the result is answered by the licence. Another mistake is checking terms only once. Record the date and check again before each new commercial project.

## Key Takeaways
1. A licence tells you whether you can use an asset commercially, whether you must credit the tool, and what the tool says about ownership.
2. Free tiers often have different terms from paid plans, platforms have their own rules, and the copyright status of AI music is unsettled and differs by country.
3. Keep a rights log for every voice and music asset, with the tool, plan, licence terms, consent and the date you checked.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Start a rights log for your project that lists each voice and music asset with the tool, plan, licence terms and date checked.
**Tools:** A free spreadsheet or document tool; the terms pages of the TTS and music tools you used [VERSION].
**Steps:**
1. Create a table with the columns shown in this lesson.
2. Add one row for each voice and music asset in your project, including your own recordings.
3. For each AI asset, open the tool's current terms or licence page for the plan you used [VERSION].
4. Write a short summary: personal or commercial use, attribution, ownership, and any platform notes.
5. Record consent status for any real person's voice. For your own voice, write "self".
6. Add today's date and save a screenshot or link for each licence.
7. Mark any asset that you cannot use for your intended purpose, and write what you will do instead.
**What good looks like:** A complete row for every asset, with clear, short licence summaries in your own words, dates, evidence links, and a note on any asset that needs to be replaced or credited.
**Time:** about 25 minutes
