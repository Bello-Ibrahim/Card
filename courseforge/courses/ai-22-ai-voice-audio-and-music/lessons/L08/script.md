# L08 Licences for AI Voices and Music | Presenter Script

Course: AI-22 · Video: 5 min · Words: 693

## Hook
You made a voiceover and a music bed with free tools. Can you use them in a video that earns money? Can a client use them in an advert? The answers are not in the audio file. They are in the licence.

## Explain
In the last lesson, you made a music bed. Today, we check what you are allowed to do with it. A licence is the set of permissions that tells you what you may do with something you did not fully create or own. For AI voices and music, it usually comes from the tool's terms of service.

A licence answers four main questions. The first is personal or commercial use. Personal use means projects that do not earn money, like a class assignment. Commercial use means anything connected to earning money, like a sponsored podcast, an advert or client work.

Free and paid plans can have different terms. So do not assume. Read the terms for the plan you actually used.

The second question is attribution. Some licences require you to credit the tool, for example in your show notes. If a credit is required, it is part of the licence, not a polite extra. The third question is platform rules. Video and podcast platforms have their own rules, and your content must follow them too.

The fourth question is who owns the output. Some tools say you own what you create. Others keep some rights, or give you a licence to use it. There is also a wider legal question about whether music made mostly by AI can be protected at all. This course gives no legal advice, so check current official guidance in your country.

Here is a way to picture it. A licence is like the rules for a rented flat. The contract says whether you can run a business from it, or rent it to someone else. Keeping a signed copy means you can prove what was agreed.

## Demonstrate
That signed copy is your rights log. It is a simple table with one row for each asset. It records the tool and plan, a short summary of the licence, whether you need a credit, any consent for a real person's voice, the date you checked, and a link or screenshot.

Let's see it in use. Aroha is a video creator in Wellington, in New Zealand. She has just signed her first paid project, for a local tourism business. Her video uses a text-to-speech voiceover and an AI music bed, both made on free plans. So she checks each asset.

In her example, the voice tool's free plan does not allow commercial use, so she records her own voice instead. The music tool's free plan allows online videos, but requires a credit, so she adds a credit line. Then she checks the platform's current rules on AI labels, and adds the label that applies.

She adds a row for each asset to her rights log, with the date and a screenshot of the terms. She also tells the client that the music is AI-generated. Remember, these terms are only examples. Real tools have different terms, so always read your own.

A common mistake is to think that free to use means free for any use. It usually means free to create. What you may do with the result is in the licence. And check again before each new commercial project, because terms change.

## Recap
Let's recap. First, a licence tells you if you can use an asset commercially, if you must credit the tool, and what the tool says about ownership. Second, plans can have different terms, and platforms have their own rules. Third, keep a rights log for every voice and music asset, with the tool, plan, terms, consent and date.
