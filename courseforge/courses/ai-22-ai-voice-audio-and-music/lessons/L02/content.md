# L02 Writing for the Ear

Course: AI-22 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O3 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Read this sentence aloud: "The collection, which was assembled between 1922 and 1931 by a team of 14 specialists (see Figure 3), comprises 2,450 objects." Did you run out of breath? Did you stop to work out the numbers? A listener cannot go back and read it again. That is why text for the ear must be written differently.

## Explanation
A reader controls the speed. They can look back, read a long sentence twice and see brackets, tables and headings. A listener cannot do any of this. They hear each word once, in order, at the speed of the voice. A good spoken script helps the listener follow without effort. This is true for a human voice and even more true for a text-to-speech voice, which reads exactly what you write.

Use these five habits:

1. **Short sentences.** Aim for about 10 to 20 words. If a sentence has "which", "although" or brackets in the middle, split it.
2. **One idea at a time.** Give each sentence one main point. Put the most important words at the end of the sentence, where the voice naturally lands.
3. **Spoken numbers.** Round numbers when you can, and write them the way you want them said. "About two and a half thousand objects" is easier to hear than "2,450 objects". Write dates in full: "the fourteenth of March", not "14/3".
4. **Clear signposts.** Tell the listener where they are: "First...", "Next...", "Now look to your left." "There are three things to notice." Signposts replace the headings a reader would see.
5. **Pauses and pronunciation help.** Use full stops and paragraph breaks where you want a pause. For difficult names, add a **phonetic spelling**: a simple respelling that shows how to say the word, such as "Tutankhamun (TOOT-ank-AH-moon)". You can use it in your own notes, or type it directly into a TTS tool if the tool says the name wrongly.

Remove anything the ear cannot use: "see Figure 3", links, abbreviations such as "e.g." and symbols such as "&" or "%". Write "for example", "and" and "per cent".

**Analogy:** Written text is like a map. The reader can see the whole route at once and look at any part again. A spoken script is like a guide walking beside you. The guide must say each turn at the right moment, one step at a time, because you cannot see the whole route.

## Worked Example
Nour works for a museum in Cairo, Egypt, and is preparing an audio guide for one display case. The original label says:

> "This limestone stela (c. 1350 BCE), discovered in 1912 at Amarna, depicts the royal family beneath the rays of the Aten, a key feature of the religious reforms introduced during the reign of Akhenaten."

It is 35 words in one sentence, with a bracket, an abbreviation and two difficult names. Nour rewrites it for the ear:

> "Look at the flat stone in front of you. It is called a stela. It is more than three thousand years old. It shows a royal family. Above them, you can see long lines coming down from a sun. That sun is the Aten (AH-ten). King Akhenaten (ak-en-AH-ten) made the Aten the centre of religious life in Egypt. This stone is one of the clearest pictures of that change."

The new script has short sentences, one idea in each, and a signpost at the start ("Look at..."). "C. 1350 BCE" became "more than three thousand years old", which a listener can understand at once. Nour kept the phonetic spellings in brackets as notes for the voice and removed them from the text she typed into the TTS tool. She only put them back when the tool said a name wrongly. The details she removed, such as the discovery date, can go on the printed label.

## Common Mistake
Many beginners paste their written text straight into a TTS tool and expect it to sound natural. The voice may be good, but the script is not. Long sentences make the voice run out of natural stress points, and brackets and symbols are read in strange ways. The correction is to rewrite first, then generate. Reading your script aloud yourself is the fastest test: if you stumble, the listener will stumble too.

## Key Takeaways
1. Listeners hear each word once, so spoken scripts need short sentences, one idea at a time and clear signposts.
2. Write numbers, dates and symbols the way you want them said, and round numbers when the exact figure is not important.
3. Use full stops and paragraph breaks for pauses, and phonetic spellings for difficult names.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Rewrite a 150-word written paragraph as a spoken script, then read it aloud and mark where you need to breathe.
**Tools:** Any text editor or pen and paper; your voice; a phone voice recorder (optional).
**Steps:**
1. Choose a written paragraph of about 150 words that you might use in your own podcast or video, such as a product description, lesson notes or a blog post. Do not use text that contains personal or confidential information.
2. Underline every sentence longer than 20 words, every number, and every bracket, symbol or abbreviation.
3. Rewrite the paragraph using the five habits: split long sentences, give each sentence one idea, write numbers as spoken words, add at least 2 signposts, and remove symbols.
4. Add a phonetic spelling in brackets for any difficult name or term.
5. Read the new script aloud at a natural speed. Mark each place where you need to breathe with a slash (/).
6. If a sentence has no breathing point for more than about 20 words, split it again.
7. Keep this script. You will turn it into a voiceover in L03.
**What good looks like:** A script of roughly the same length or slightly longer, where most sentences are under 20 words, numbers are written as they are spoken, there are clear signposts, and breathing marks appear at natural places. You can read it aloud once without stumbling.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
