# L01 What AI Governance Is and Why It Matters

Course: AI-30 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
Your marketing team started using an AI writing tool last month. Your HR team is testing an AI tool that ranks job applicants. Nobody asked the legal team first. Who decided these tools were acceptable, and who will answer for them if something goes wrong? That question is what AI governance exists to answer.

## Explanation
**Important:** this course is educational and is not legal advice. Laws, guidance and deadlines change, and every organisation's facts are different. For decisions about your own organisation, consult qualified legal advisers in each country where you operate.

**AI governance** is the set of roles, policies, processes and controls that decide how an organisation builds, buys and uses AI. It is not one document and it is not one team. It connects four areas that are often managed separately: legal compliance, ethics, information security and business risk.

Most AI governance frameworks, whatever their source, contain six building blocks:

1. **Roles.** Who is accountable for AI at executive level, who approves new systems, and who owns each system day to day. The data protection officer (DPO), where one exists, has a clear advisory role.
2. **Principles.** A short list of commitments, such as fairness, transparency, human oversight, privacy and security, that guide every decision.
3. **Inventory.** A list of every AI system in use or in development, including tools bought from vendors and features inside existing software.
4. **Risk assessment.** A repeatable way to judge each system's risks to people and to the organisation, including legal classification and impact assessments.
5. **Controls.** The measures that reduce risk: approval steps, testing, human review, contract terms, access limits and training.
6. **Monitoring.** Checks that systems keep working as expected after launch, and a process for incidents, complaints and regular review.

Why does this matter to legal, risk and compliance professionals? First, AI-specific law, such as the EU AI Act, places duties on organisations according to the risk of each AI use. Second, data protection laws, such as the GDPR and Nigeria's NDPA, already apply whenever an AI system processes personal data. Third, regulators and courts usually ask the same question: can you show what you decided and why? Governance produces that evidence.

**Analogy:** AI governance is the building code and the inspection plan, not the building itself. Engineers still design and build. The code says what standards the building must meet, and the inspection plan says who checks it, when, and what happens if a check fails. Without them, each builder follows their own idea of "safe".

Good governance is also proportionate. A spelling assistant does not need the same review as a system that decides who gets a loan. The building blocks help you scale your effort to the level of risk.

## Worked Example
Farida Haddad is the new compliance manager at a hypothetical engineering consultancy with offices in Amman and Dublin. She is asked to "sort out AI". She starts by checking the six building blocks.

- **Roles:** nobody is named. IT buys software, and each department chooses its own tools.
- **Principles:** there is a general code of conduct, but it does not mention AI.
- **Inventory:** she sends a short survey and finds eleven AI tools in use. Four were bought by individual staff with company cards.
- **Risk assessment:** none. The GDPR records of processing do not mention the AI tools.
- **Controls:** one team has a rule against pasting client drawings into public chatbots. Other teams do not.
- **Monitoring:** nothing is reviewed after purchase.

Farida does not try to fix everything at once. She proposes an accountable executive, a small review group and a two-page interim rule, while a full policy is drafted. Her gap list becomes the plan for the next quarter.

## Common Mistake
Many people treat AI governance as "writing an AI policy". A policy is only one control. If nobody owns the inventory, nobody assesses new tools and nobody checks systems after launch, the policy is a document without effect. Start with roles and the inventory, because every other building block depends on knowing who is responsible and which systems exist.

## Key Takeaways
1. AI governance is the set of roles, policies, processes and controls that decide how an organisation builds, buys and uses AI.
2. Six building blocks appear in most frameworks: roles, principles, inventory, risk assessment, controls and monitoring.
3. This course is educational, not legal advice; governance helps you show what you decided and why, but decisions about your organisation need qualified advisers.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Use a free policy template to list the 6 building blocks of AI governance and write one sentence on how your own organisation handles each today.
**Tools:** A free AI policy or governance template from an official or reputable source, such as a data protection regulator or a government digital agency [VERIFY]; a word processor or paper.
**Steps:**
1. Open the template and find where it covers roles, principles, inventory, risk assessment, controls and monitoring. Note any block it does not cover.
2. Make a table with two columns: "Building block" and "How we handle it today".
3. For each block, write one honest sentence. "We do not do this" is a valid answer.
4. Mark the block you think is the largest gap, and write one reason.
5. Use general descriptions only. Do not paste confidential company information or personal data into any AI tool.
**What good looks like:** Six rows, each with a specific sentence (for example, "IT approves purchases, but nobody checks AI features"), one clearly marked largest gap, and a note on any block the template did not cover.
**Time:** about 20 minutes
