# L03 The EU AI Act: A Risk-Based Approach

Course: AI-30 · Module: M1 · Objectives: O1, O2 · Video: 5 min

## Hook
A spam filter and a tool that decides who gets a job interview are both AI. Should they face the same rules? The EU AI Act says no. It asks one main question first: how much harm could this use cause?

## Explanation
This lesson is educational and is not legal advice. It explains the structure of the Act; the precise wording, article references and dates must always be taken from the official text.

The EU AI Act sorts AI uses into tiers. The tier depends mainly on the **intended purpose** and context of use, not on the technology. The same model can be minimal-risk in one use and high-risk in another.

1. **Prohibited practices.** A short list of uses considered unacceptable, which may not be placed on the market or used in the EU. Examples of the kinds of practices on the list include certain manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, exploiting the vulnerabilities of specific groups, some forms of social scoring and some uses of biometric identification [VERIFY] [REGION]. The full list and its exceptions are in [the article on prohibited practices] [VERIFY] [REGION].
2. **High-risk systems.** Systems that can significantly affect health, safety or fundamental rights. There are two main routes into this tier: AI that is a safety component of certain regulated products, and AI used in listed areas, such as employment, education, access to essential private and public services (for example, credit scoring), law enforcement, migration and critical infrastructure [VERIFY] [REGION]. These are set out in [the high-risk classification article and its annexes] [VERIFY] [REGION]. High-risk systems carry the heaviest duties, covered in L04.
3. **Transparency duties.** Some systems must tell people that they are dealing with AI, or label content that AI generated or changed. Examples include chatbots that talk with the public and systems that create synthetic images, audio or video [VERIFY] [REGION]. See [the transparency article] [VERIFY] [REGION].
4. **Minimal risk.** Everything else, such as spam filters or AI in video games. The Act adds no specific duties here, although other laws, including data protection law, still apply.

Separately, the Act has rules for **general-purpose AI models**, the large models that can be adapted to many tasks. These duties fall mainly on the model providers, with extra duties for models considered to carry systemic risk [VERIFY] [REGION].

**Analogy:** The Act works like product safety rules. A child's toy, a kitchen knife and a car seat are all products, but they receive different checks because the possible harm is different. Some products are banned completely. Most carry a warning label or no special rule at all.

**Timeline.** The Act applies in phases: different groups of obligations start at different times, and the timeline has been the subject of proposed changes [VERIFY] [REGION]. Do not rely on a date from memory or a news article. Check [the application dates in the official text and any amendments] on the day you give advice [VERIFY] [REGION].

## Worked Example
Sara Lindqvist is a risk analyst at a hypothetical retail group in Sweden. She reviews four AI uses:

- **Product description writer** that drafts online shop text. Staff check the text before publishing. Likely **minimal risk**, though Sara notes it should be checked if AI-generated content is ever published without review, because transparency duties may apply [VERIFY] [REGION].
- **Customer chatbot** on the website. People must know they are talking with AI. Likely **transparency duties**.
- **CV-ranking tool** used by HR to shortlist candidates. Employment is a listed area. Likely **high-risk** [VERIFY] [REGION].
- **Camera system** proposed to infer shoppers' emotions and target offers. Sara flags it for legal review as a **possible prohibited or high-risk use**, depending on the exact purpose and setting [VERIFY] [REGION].

Sara records the tier, reason and confidence for each, and sends the fourth case to external counsel.

## Common Mistake
Many learners classify by technology: "it uses a large language model, so it is high-risk" or "it is only a small model, so it is minimal risk". The Act classifies mainly by purpose and context. Always ask what the system is used for, who it affects and what decisions it supports.

## Key Takeaways
1. The EU AI Act sorts AI uses into prohibited, high-risk, transparency and minimal-risk tiers, with separate rules for general-purpose AI models.
2. The tier depends mainly on the intended purpose and context of use, not on the type of technology.
3. The Act applies in phases, so every date and article reference must be checked against the official text; this lesson is educational, not legal advice.

## Hands-on Exercise
**Task:** Sort 8 short, hypothetical AI use cases into the four risk tiers and note which ones you are unsure about and why.
**Tools:** Pen and paper or a spreadsheet; the official text of the EU AI Act or an official summary from the European Commission [VERIFY].
**Steps:**
1. Use these cases: a spam filter for a law firm; a chatbot answering questions for a bank; an AI tool that ranks university applicants; a system that scores citizens' general trustworthiness for a city council; a tool that creates realistic synthetic videos for advertising; an AI feature that suggests stock levels for a warehouse; a tool that checks exam candidates for cheating by webcam; an AI safety component in a medical device.
2. Place each case in one tier and write one sentence of reasoning.
3. Give each a confidence level: high, medium or low.
4. For every low-confidence case, write what fact you would need to know.
**What good looks like:** All 8 cases placed with a reason, honest confidence levels, and specific missing facts, such as "Is the output used to make the final decision?"
**Time:** about 20 minutes
