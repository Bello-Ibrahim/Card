## L01 What AI Governance Is and Why It Matters

- **Filename:** `ai-30-ai-governance-privacy-and-compliance_M1_L01_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (728 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Your marketing team started using an AI writing tool last month. Your HR team is testing a tool that ranks job applicants. Nobody asked the legal team first. So who decided these tools were acceptable, and who will answer for them if something goes wrong?

Hi, and welcome to AI Governance, Privacy and Compliance. That question is exactly what AI governance exists to answer. Before we start, one important point. This course is educational and is not legal advice. For decisions about your own organisation, speak to qualified legal advisers.

So what is AI governance? It is the set of roles, policies, processes and controls that decide how an organisation builds, buys and uses AI. It is not one document, and it is not one team. It connects four areas that are often managed separately: legal compliance, ethics, information security and business risk.

Most frameworks, whatever their source, share six building blocks. Roles say who is accountable, who approves and who owns each system. Principles are a short list of commitments, such as fairness and human oversight. The inventory lists every AI system you use, including features inside software you already have.

Risk assessment is a repeatable way to judge each system's risks to people and to the organisation. Controls are the measures that reduce risk, such as approval steps, testing, human review and contract terms. And monitoring checks that systems keep working after launch, with a process for incidents and complaints.

Why does this matter to you? First, AI-specific law, such as the EU AI Act, places duties on organisations according to the risk of each AI use. Second, data protection laws, such as the GDPR and Nigeria's NDPA, already apply whenever AI processes personal data. Third, regulators usually ask one question: can you show what you decided, and why?

Here is a helpful way to picture it. AI governance is the building code and the inspection plan, not the building itself. Engineers still design and build. The code says what standards the building must meet. The inspection plan says who checks it, when, and what happens if a check fails.

Good governance is also proportionate. A spelling assistant does not need the same review as a system that decides who gets a loan. The building blocks help you match your effort to the level of risk.

Let's see this in practice. Farida Haddad is the new compliance manager at a fictional engineering consultancy with offices in Amman and Dublin. She is asked to sort out AI. So she checks the six building blocks, one by one.

Roles: nobody is named, and each department chooses its own tools. Principles: the code of conduct does not mention AI. Inventory: a short staff survey finds eleven AI tools in use, and four were bought by individual staff with company cards.

Risk assessment: there is none, and the records of processing do not mention the AI tools. Controls: one team bans pasting client drawings into public chatbots, but other teams do not. Monitoring: nothing is reviewed after purchase.

Farida does not try to fix everything at once. She proposes an accountable executive, a small review group and a short interim rule, while a full policy is drafted. Her gap list becomes the plan for the next quarter.

One common mistake is to treat AI governance as simply writing an AI policy. A policy is only one control. If nobody owns the inventory, and nobody assesses new tools, the policy has no effect. Start with roles and the inventory, because everything else depends on them.

Let's recap. First, AI governance is the set of roles, policies, processes and controls that decide how an organisation builds, buys and uses AI. Second, six building blocks appear in most frameworks: roles, principles, inventory, risk assessment, controls and monitoring. Third, governance helps you show what you decided and why.

Now it is your turn. In the exercise below this video, you will use a free policy template to list the six building blocks, and write one honest sentence on how your own organisation handles each today. Please do not paste confidential information into any AI tool.

By the end of the course, you will draft a governance policy and a risk register for a fictional company, Velmora Tradeways. In the next lesson, we look at the regulatory map: three laws, different jobs. See you there.
```

## L02 The Regulatory Map: Three Laws, Different Jobs

- **Filename:** `ai-30-ai-governance-privacy-and-compliance_M1_L02_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (741 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A lending app is built in Lagos, runs on servers in Ireland and approves loans for customers in Portugal. Which law applies? The honest answer is often more than one, and each law asks different questions.

In the last lesson, we met the six building blocks of governance. Now let's map the laws they serve. As always, this course is educational and is not legal advice. We work with three legal frameworks, and each one has a different job.

The EU AI Act regulates AI systems and certain AI models. Its main tool is risk: the greater the possible harm of an AI use, the stricter the rules. It places duties on roles such as providers, who develop a system and place it on the market, and deployers, who use it under their own authority.

The GDPR regulates the processing of personal data about people in the EU. It does not care whether that processing is done by AI or by a spreadsheet. Its key roles are the controller, who decides why and how data is processed, and the processor, who processes data on the controller's behalf.

Nigeria's Data Protection Act, the NDPA, also regulates the processing of personal data. It has a similar structure of principles, lawful bases, rights and duties for data controllers and processors, with its own regulator, its own transfer rules and extra duties for some organisations.

Think of a lorry carrying food across a border. Vehicle safety rules check the lorry itself. Food hygiene rules check what it carries. Customs rules check where it goes. The AI Act is like the vehicle rules for the AI system. The GDPR and the NDPA are like the hygiene rules for the personal data inside.

Each law also decides for itself when it applies, and each one can reach organisations outside its home territory. The EU AI Act can reach providers that place systems on the EU market, and sometimes organisations whose system output is used in the EU.

The GDPR can reach organisations outside the EU that offer goods or services to people in the EU, or monitor their behaviour. The NDPA can reach some organisations outside Nigeria that process data of people in Nigeria. The exact tests are in each law's scope provisions, and they must be checked.

Each law also has its own supervisors, at national level and, in the EU, at EU level too. Names and designations can change, so always confirm who supervises what from official sources.

Back to our lending app. Adaeze Okonkwo founds a fictional lending app in Lagos. It uses an AI model to score applicants. Then the company decides to accept customers in Portugal.

First, the NDPA. The company is based in Nigeria and processes data of Nigerian customers as a data controller, so the NDPA very likely applies. Second, the GDPR. By offering loans to people in Portugal, the company may fall within the GDPR's reach, even without an office in the EU.

Third, the EU AI Act. The company developed the scoring model, so it is likely a provider. Credit scoring of individuals is an example of a use that may be high-risk. And when it uses the model on its EU customers, it may also be a deployer.

Adaeze's first task is to record which law applies to which activity, and why. That record becomes the start of her compliance map.

A common mistake is to say, we comply with the GDPR, so our AI is covered. The GDPR protects personal data. It does not set the AI Act's rules on risk management, documentation or human oversight. Map each law separately, then look for overlaps you can handle together.

Let's recap. First, the EU AI Act regulates AI systems and models by risk, while the GDPR and the NDPA regulate the processing of personal data. Second, each law has its own territorial scope, so one AI system can fall under all three. Third, roles differ: provider and deployer under the AI Act, controller and processor under the data protection laws.

In the exercise below this video, you will fill in a comparison table for the three laws: who each one protects, what it regulates, its key roles and who supervises it. Use official guidance only, write the source and the date, and mark anything you could not confirm.

In the next lesson, we look closely at the EU AI Act and its risk-based approach. See you there.
```

## L03 The EU AI Act: A Risk-Based Approach

- **Filename:** `ai-30-ai-governance-privacy-and-compliance_M1_L03_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (716 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A spam filter and a tool that decides who gets a job interview are both AI. Should they face the same rules? The EU AI Act says no. It asks one main question first: how much harm could this use cause?

Last time, we mapped three laws. Today, we focus on the structure of the EU AI Act. A quick reminder: this course is educational and is not legal advice. Precise wording and references must always come from the official text.

The Act sorts AI uses into tiers. The tier depends mainly on the intended purpose and the context of use, not on the technology. So the same model can be minimal risk in one use, and high-risk in another.

At the top are prohibited practices, a short list of uses considered unacceptable. The kinds of practices that may be on it include certain manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, exploiting the vulnerabilities of specific groups, some forms of social scoring and some uses of biometric identification.

Next come high-risk systems, which can significantly affect health, safety or fundamental rights. There are two main routes in: AI that is a safety component of certain regulated products, and AI used in listed areas, such as employment, education, access to essential services like credit, and critical infrastructure.

Then there are transparency duties. Some systems must tell people they are dealing with AI, or label content that AI created or changed, such as chatbots and synthetic video. Everything else is minimal risk, like spam filters. The Act adds no specific duties there, but data protection law still applies.

Separately, the Act has rules for general-purpose AI models, the large models that can be adapted to many tasks. These duties fall mainly on the model providers, with extra duties for the most powerful models.

Think of product safety rules. A child's toy, a kitchen knife and a car seat are all products, but they get different checks, because the possible harm is different. Some products are banned completely. Most carry a warning label, or no special rule at all.

One more point. The Act applies in phases, and the timeline has been the subject of proposed changes. So never rely on a date from memory or a news article. Check the official text on the day you give advice.

Let's apply this. Sara Lindqvist is a risk analyst at a fictional retail group in Sweden. She reviews four AI uses.

First, a product description writer. Staff check its text before publishing, so it is likely minimal risk, though transparency duties may apply if AI text is ever published without review. Second, a customer chatbot on the website. People must know they are talking with AI, so it likely has transparency duties.

Third, a tool that ranks CVs to shortlist candidates. Employment is a listed area, so it is likely high-risk. Fourth, a camera system proposed to read shoppers' emotions and target offers. Sara flags it for legal review as possibly prohibited or high-risk, depending on the exact purpose and setting.

For each use, Sara records the tier, the reason and her confidence. She sends the fourth case to external counsel. That is not a weakness. Knowing when to escalate is part of the method.

A common mistake is to classify by technology. It uses a large language model, so it must be high-risk. Or, it is a small model, so it must be minimal risk. The Act looks mainly at purpose and context. Always ask what the system is used for, and who it affects.

Let's recap. First, the EU AI Act sorts AI uses into prohibited, high-risk, transparency and minimal-risk tiers, with separate rules for general-purpose models. Second, the tier depends mainly on intended purpose and context, not technology. Third, the Act applies in phases, so every date and reference must be checked against the official text.

In the exercise below this video, you will sort eight short, fictional AI use cases into the four tiers, from a spam filter to a webcam exam checker. Give each one a reason and a confidence level, and for every uncertain case, write down the fact you would need to know.

In the next lesson, we look at EU AI Act roles and obligations: who has to do what. See you there.
```

## L04 EU AI Act Roles and Obligations

- **Filename:** `ai-30-ai-governance-privacy-and-compliance_M1_L04_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.3 minutes (729 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
Two organisations use the same high-risk AI system. One built it. The other bought it. Do they have the same duties? No. Under the EU AI Act, your obligations depend on your role, and one organisation can hold several roles at once.

In the last lesson, we saw how the Act sorts AI uses into risk tiers. Now we ask who carries the duties. This lesson is educational and is not legal advice. The exact wording of each duty is in the official text.

There are four main roles. A provider develops an AI system and places it on the market under its own name. A deployer uses an AI system under its own authority at work. An importer brings a system from a provider outside the EU onto the EU market. And a distributor, such as a reseller, makes it available.

Roles can change. A deployer may be treated as a provider if it puts its own name on a high-risk system, makes a substantial change to it, or changes its purpose so that it becomes high-risk.

Providers of high-risk systems carry the heaviest duties. At a high level, these include risk management across the system's life, data governance with checks for bias, technical documentation and instructions for use, automatic logging, design for human oversight, and appropriate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity.

Providers also need a quality management system, a conformity assessment before launch, registration where required, and monitoring after the system is on the market.

Deployers have lighter duties, but they are real. They must use the system as instructed, assign trained people to oversee it, make sure the input data they control is relevant, monitor it, report serious problems, keep the logs they control and inform affected people where required.

The Act also sets penalties for breaches, and supervision is shared between EU and national levels. Never quote penalty figures from memory. Always check the relevant provision in the official text.

Think of a lift in an office building. The manufacturer must design it safely, test it and provide a manual. The building owner must use it as the manual says and arrange inspections. If the owner rebuilds the motor, the owner starts to carry some of the manufacturer's responsibility.

Now let's follow one product through three fictional organisations. It is a triage-support tool that ranks hospital patients by urgency. For this example, we assume the tool is high-risk.

Lucía Ferrer leads compliance at a software vendor in Valencia, Spain, which built the tool and sells it under its own brand. Her company is the provider. It carries the full list, from risk management and data governance to conformity assessment and monitoring after launch.

Pieter de Vries is the data protection officer at a hospital in Utrecht, in the Netherlands, which buys the tool. The hospital is a deployer. It must use the tool as instructed, assign trained staff to oversee its output, check input data, monitor it and report serious incidents. It is also a GDPR controller for patient data.

Emeka Nwosu runs a data science company in Lagos that builds a similar tool and sells it directly to clinics in the EU. His company is a provider from outside the EU, so it may need an authorised representative in the EU. Each organisation writes down its role, the reason and its source.

A common mistake is to say, the vendor is responsible for compliance. The provider carries the heaviest duties, but deployers have their own. And a deployer that customises or rebrands a system may become a provider. Check your role for each system, and check again whenever you change how you use it.

Let's recap. First, duties depend on role: provider, deployer, importer or distributor, and one organisation can hold more than one. Second, providers of high-risk systems carry the heaviest duties, such as risk management, data governance, documentation, logging and human oversight. Third, deployers have their own duties, and can become providers through rebranding or substantial changes.

In the exercise below this video, you will take today's three organisations, but change the product to a tool that screens job applicants. For each one, write its likely role, three to five main duties in plain words, and one note on what would change its role.

In the next lesson, we put this together by classifying an AI system step by step. See you there.
```

## L05 Classifying an AI System Step by Step

- **Filename:** `ai-30-ai-governance-privacy-and-compliance_M1_L05_presenter.mp4`
- **Expected length:** about 5.2 minutes (713 words). The quality gate accepts ±10%.

```text
A regulator asks you: why did you decide this system is not high-risk? If your answer is, it seemed fine, you have a problem. A classification is only as strong as the reasoning you can show later.

So far, we have learned the Act's risk tiers and its roles. Today, we turn them into a repeatable method. This lesson is educational and is not legal advice, and every step must be checked against the official text of the EU AI Act.

The method has five steps. Work through them in order, and write down your answer and your reason at each step. Step one: describe the system and its purpose. What does it do, who uses it, who is affected, and what decisions does its output support? If the vendor's stated purpose and your actual use differ, record both.

Step two: is it an AI system under the Act? The Act has its own definition, based on features such as working with some autonomy and inferring from inputs how to produce outputs like predictions, recommendations or decisions. Simple rule-based software may fall outside it.

Step three: check the prohibited list. If there is a possible match, stop and escalate to legal counsel. Step four: check the high-risk categories. Is it a safety component of a regulated product, or used in a listed area such as employment or education?

Some systems in listed areas may not be treated as high-risk, for example if they only perform a narrow procedural task. That exception has conditions, so record exactly why you think it applies. Step five: check the transparency duties. These can apply in addition to high-risk duties.

Finish with a classification note. It records the tier, the reasoning at each step, the sources you used and their dates, your confidence, the reviewer's name and a date to review the decision again.

Think of a doctor's diagnosis checklist. A good doctor does not jump to a conclusion from one symptom. She goes through the same questions every time and writes down what she found. Another doctor can read the notes later and see exactly how she reached her decision.

Let's try it. Katrin Hoffmann is legal counsel at a fictional recruitment agency in Hamburg, Germany. The agency plans to use a CV-screening tool bought from a vendor.

Step one. The tool reads CVs, scores each one against a job description and produces a shortlist. Recruiters normally interview only the top twenty, so candidates are affected directly. Step two. The vendor says the tool uses a trained language model to infer match scores, so it is likely an AI system.

Step three. It does not use emotion recognition or manipulative techniques, so there is no match with the prohibited list. Step four. Recruitment is a listed employment use, and the tool shapes who gets an interview, so the narrow-task exception does not seem to fit. It is likely high-risk, and the agency is a deployer.

Step five. Candidates do not chat with the tool, but they must still be informed about its use under other rules, such as data protection law. Katrin's note says high-risk, deployer, confidence high. It lists her sources and sets a review date in six months, or earlier if the vendor changes the tool.

A common mistake is to classify once and never look again. If the agency starts using the tool to rank staff for promotion, or the vendor adds a feature that analyses facial expressions in video interviews, the answer may change. So write the review triggers into the note itself.

Let's recap. First, use five steps in order: describe the system, check the definition, check prohibited practices, check the high-risk categories, and check transparency duties. Second, write a classification note with reasoning, sources, confidence, reviewer and review date. Third, classification is not permanent. A new purpose or feature can change the tier.

In the exercise below this video, you will use the five steps on two fictional systems: a billing chatbot at a telecoms company in Belgium, and an exam-proctoring tool at a university in Italy. Write a short classification note for each, with at least one review trigger.

That completes module one. In the next lesson, we move to data protection principles across the AI lifecycle. See you there.
```
